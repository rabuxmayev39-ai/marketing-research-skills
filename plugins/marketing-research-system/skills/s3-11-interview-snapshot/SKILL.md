# Interview snapshot after each interview (S3-11)

An interview snapshot is a one-page record of what was learned from a single interview, written straight after it. It comes from Teresa Torres, a product discovery coach, who describes it in Continuous Discovery Habits (2021) and in free articles on her site Product Talk. Her template has a name and photo, quick facts, a memorable quote, an experience map (the respondent's story drawn as a sequence of key moments), insights and opportunities. Its purpose is to make each interview memorable, actionable and easy to find later, and to keep every finding tied to a specific person and context before the team looks across interviews. This skill adapts it for agency research by adding a list of open questions and by replacing the photo with a respondent code.

Evidence grade: C. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. No peer-reviewed test of the snapshot was found for this skill. Treat it as a disciplined note-taking habit that protects raw detail and speeds synthesis. A snapshot is not analysis and proves nothing by itself: one interview is one case, and patterns are claimed only in S4 and S5 methods.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (what the respondent said or did, quotes, quick facts) versus INTERPRETATION (insights, opportunities, the team's reading). Draft only from the notes, recording or transcript the user supplies; never fill a gap with a plausible detail. When the snapshot is saved and shared, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- Any in-depth, switch, expert or B2B interview has just finished (S1-04, S1-07, S1-18), including client and stakeholder interviews (S0-12).
- Several people interview in parallel and need one shared format.
- Fieldwork runs for weeks and early interviews would otherwise fade before synthesis.

Do not use it when:
- The session was a focus group; write a group debrief by topic (S1-05), because a snapshot is built around one person's story.
- It would replace the transcript or recording. The snapshot points to the raw data and does not stand in for it.
- The team wants a cross-interview summary. That is synthesis (S4-01, S5-01) and comes after the snapshots exist.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. The interviewer's notes, and the recording or transcript if available, from an interview finished today.
2. The research question and screening criteria, so quick facts match the segments (S2-01, S2-06).
3. The agreed quick-facts list for this project (the same fields for every respondent).
4. A respondent code and the consent terms, including whether a name or photo may be kept (S2-10).
5. The shared folder or channel where snapshots are stored.

## Procedure
1. Fix the template and the quick-facts fields before the first interview. Torres says quick facts are context details that differ by company and product and that later allow opportunities to be compared across types of customer. Working rule: 5 to 8 fields taken from the screening criteria and segments, identical for every respondent.
2. Reserve the time. Torres advises making the snapshot right after the interview while the stories are fresh; she reports that practised teams need 15 to 20 minutes and beginners longer. Working rule for this agency: block 30 minutes directly after each interview, and finish on the same day at the latest. A snapshot written later is marked "written late".
3. Write it with the people who were in the interview. Torres recommends doing it collaboratively; the interviewer and note-taker each say what they heard before one of them types.
4. Who. Enter the respondent code, date, interviewer, language, interview length and how the person was recruited. Working rule: no real name or photo in the snapshot unless consent allows it; the key linking codes to names is stored separately (S2-10).
5. Quick facts. Fill every field from what the respondent said; write "not asked" where it is missing, never a guess. FACT.
6. Memorable quote. Choose one sentence that will bring the story back weeks later. Torres suggests moments of strong emotion or surprising, unusual behaviour. Copy it word for word in the original language and check it against the recording or transcript; add a time stamp. If it cannot be checked, mark it "from notes, not verified".
7. Key moments. List the respondent's story as 4 to 7 moments in time order (what triggered it, what they did, where it went wrong, how it ended). Torres draws this as an experience map and notes that not every detail is needed. Past behaviour only, no opinions about the future (S3-04, S1-23).
8. Opportunities. In Torres's terms these are unmet needs, pain points and desires that appear in the story. Working rules: word each from the respondent's side ("I cannot ...", "I need ..."), tie each to the key moment where it appeared, and do not write solutions; a line that names a feature, price or advert is a solution and goes back into need form.
9. Insights and surprises. Record notable observations that are not yet a need, pain or desire, for example unusual context or behaviour; Torres keeps them because they may later become opportunities or point to a new segment. Add anything that contradicts the team's written expectations (S2-11). INTERPRETATION.
10. Open questions (this skill's addition). Note what was unclear, what to ask the next respondent, and anything to change in the guide or recruitment.
11. If an AI model drafts the snapshot, the human who was in the room writes or corrects it first-hand. Torres recommends doing single-interview synthesis yourself before using AI as a second view, working on one interview at a time and not pooling all transcripts, and verifying against the source; in her own test about 30% of the direct quotes an AI tool returned were inaccurate or absent from the interviews. Every quote and fact the assistant drafts is checked against the transcript.
12. Save as one page with the standard file name and share it where the team already works, with a two-line note. Torres warns against passing snapshots to stakeholders without context: explain why the interviews are done and what "opportunity" means.
13. Use the set. After each new snapshot update the count of interviews in which each opportunity appears, by quick-fact group; Torres uses snapshots this way to size opportunities and to find people to re-interview. Feed the counts to the saturation log (S2-04) and the snapshots to synthesis. Return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. Input: a flour mill studies how small bakeries choose flour. Interview 7 of a planned 12, a tandir-bread baker in a regional town, in Uzbek, 40 minutes, ended 11:40. Snapshot written 12:05 to 12:25.

```
INTERVIEW SNAPSHOT  R07 | 14 May | interviewer: A. | Uzbek | 40 min | recruited at flour market
QUICK FACTS (FACT)  bakery type: tandir non | staff: 3 | flour: 20 bags x 50 kg a week = 1,000 kg
  current supplier: local mill via dealer | changed supplier in last 12 months: 2 times | pays: on delivery, cash
QUOTE (FACT, verified, 18:32)  "Un bir xil kelmasa, xamir aytganimni qilmaydi."
KEY MOMENTS (FACT)
  1 A batch in winter gave flat bread; customers complained.
  2 He blamed the flour, tried a second brand from the same dealer for 2 weeks.
  3 The second brand was fine, then one delivery differed; he went back.
  4 Now he tests each delivery with one small dough before the morning bake.
OPPORTUNITIES (INTERPRETATION, tied to moments)
  O1 "I cannot tell if a new delivery will behave like the last one." (moments 1, 3)
  O2 "I need someone to tell me what changed when the flour changes." (moment 2)
INSIGHTS / SURPRISES  Runs his own test bake - a workaround. Did not mention price until asked;
  the team expected price to come first (expectation note E2).
OPEN QUESTIONS  Do dealers pass on batch information? Ask the next baker how they test flour.
TALLY AFTER 7 SNAPSHOTS  O1 appears in 4 of 7 (3 of 4 regional bakers, 1 of 3 Tashkent bakers).
```
Arithmetic check: 20 x 50 = 1,000 kg; 11:40 to 12:05 = 25 minutes after the interview; 12:05 to 12:25 = 20 minutes of writing; 3 + 1 = 4 snapshots with O1; 4 + 3 = 7 bakers. The tally counts interviews; it is not a market share.

## Common mistakes
- Writing snapshots in a batch at the end of the week, when interviews have merged in memory.
- Summarising the whole transcript; the snapshot is a selection, with a pointer to the raw data.
- Tidying or translating the quote so that it sounds better.
- Listing solutions ("wants a loyalty card") as opportunities.
- Mixing what the respondent said with what the team concluded, with no labels.
- Different quick-facts fields for each respondent, so nothing can be compared later.
- Letting an AI tool produce the snapshot from the transcript with no check by someone who was present.
- Treating one vivid snapshot as a finding about the market.

## Quality checklist
- Was the snapshot written on the day of the interview by someone who was present?
- Does it fit on one page and point to the recording or transcript?
- Is the quote word for word, in the original language and verified or marked unverified?
- Are all quick-facts fields filled or marked "not asked"?
- Is every opportunity phrased as a need, pain or desire and tied to a moment in the story?
- Are FACT and INTERPRETATION sections labelled?
- Are open questions listed and passed to the next interviewer?
- Are personal data handled as the consent allows?

## Output template
```
INTERVIEW SNAPSHOT  [code] | [date] | interviewer / note-taker | language | length | recruited via ...
Written: [time, same day / written late] | Raw data: [link to recording or transcript]
QUICK FACTS (FACT): field 1 ... | field 2 ... | field 3 ... | field 4 ... | field 5 ...
MEMORABLE QUOTE (FACT, original language): "..." [time stamp] verified / from notes
KEY MOMENTS (FACT): 1 ... 2 ... 3 ... 4 ...
OPPORTUNITIES (INTERPRETATION): O.. "I need / I cannot ..." (moment ...)
INSIGHTS / SURPRISES (INTERPRETATION): ... | against expectation no. ...
OPEN QUESTIONS: for next interview ... | guide change ... | recruitment change ...
TALLY: opportunity | appears in x of n snapshots | by quick-fact group
```

## Works with
- S1-04 in-depth interviews, S1-07 JTBD switch interviews, S1-18 B2B key informants, S0-12 client and stakeholder interviews: every interview ends with a snapshot. S3-04 story-based guides: produce the story the key moments record.
- S2-04 saturation and S2-05 information power: the tally of new opportunities per snapshot is the theme log. S2-11 written expectations: surprises are judged against them.
- S4-01 thematic analysis, S4-04 coding cycles, S4-05 matrix displays: snapshots are the first-cycle input, with quick facts as matrix columns. S4-07 negative cases and S4-09 forces of progress: read from key moments.
- S5-01 affinity diagram and S5-11 opportunity solution tree: opportunities from snapshots are grouped and structured there. S5-14 member checking: a snapshot can be read back to the respondent.
- S2-10 ethics and consent: codes, photos and storage. S7-04 project retrospective: open questions that were never answered.

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Write the snapshot in the language of the interview, Uzbek or Russian, and keep the quote in the original with a translation underneath only if the client needs it.
- Polite or agreeing answers make weak snapshots. If the page has praise but no key moments from real past behaviour, record that as a data-quality note and adjust the guide.
- For interviews in regions, write the snapshot before travelling back; note the place and whether others were present, since a boss, relative or neighbour in the room changes what is said.
- Respondents recruited through Telegram channels and referrals resemble each other; keep "recruited via" in quick facts so the tally shows who is missing, including older and rural people.
- The method comes from software product teams in Western companies. Its use for agency research here is this skill's adaptation and has not been tested.
