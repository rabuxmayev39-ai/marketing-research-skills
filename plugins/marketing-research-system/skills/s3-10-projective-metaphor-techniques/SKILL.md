# Projective and metaphor techniques (S3-10)

Projective techniques give respondents a vague or unfinished stimulus (a picture, a half sentence, a brand to describe as a person) and let them fill it with their own meaning. They came from clinical psychology and entered marketing as "motivation research". Sunette Donoghue's (2000) open review describes them as a structured, indirect way to study the why of behaviour and stresses that they are not measurement tools. Metaphor elicitation is the best-known modern branch: Gerald Zaltman and Robin Coulter (1995) introduced the Zaltman Metaphor Elicitation Technique (ZMET), in which people bring their own pictures and explain them in a long one-to-one interview, and Zaltman's 1997 article is the usual reference for the underlying argument, summarised in later descriptions as: thought is largely image-based and often not conscious, so direct questions miss much of it. Clive Boddy (2005) asked in the title of his review whether the techniques give "valueless subjectivity or insightful reality" and, as a published reply reports, called for more evidence on how they are applied.

Evidence grade: B. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. The techniques are long-established in professional qualitative research, but their reliability and validity are disputed: Donoghue notes that experts often disagree on interpretation, and a 2024 review of 75 projective studies found that only three reported inter-coder agreement. Use the output to generate hypotheses and language. Do not use it as proof, and never without a second source.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (what the respondent chose, drew or said, in their words) versus INTERPRETATION (what the team thinks it means). Do not offer psychological readings of individual respondents. When the findings table is delivered, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- The topic is brand image, an emotional or status-laden category, or something people find hard to put into words.
- Direct questions return polite, rational or socially acceptable answers and the team suspects more underneath.
- The stage is exploratory and the aim is hypotheses, vocabulary and creative territory.

Do not use it when:
- The client needs numbers, sizes or a ranking. Use S1-08 after the hypotheses exist.
- The question is factual (what was bought, where, for how much). Ask about past behaviour (S1-23, S3-04).
- Nobody skilled is available to moderate and to code; Donoghue lists the need for trained interpreters as a main drawback.
- There will be no second source. Without triangulation (S2-07) the method should not be run.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. The research question and the brand, category or experience to explore (S2-01).
2. The method that will carry the exercises: in-depth interviews (S1-04) or focus groups (S1-05), and the respondent profile (S2-06).
3. Stimuli: stems, word lists, picture sets, or a pre-task brief for respondents' own pictures.
4. Informed consent that covers recording, photos of collages and use of respondents' own images (S2-10).
5. Two people who will code independently, and the second data source planned for triangulation.

## Procedure
1. Choose the techniques from Donoghue's five families and match them to the question. Association: word association (first words for a brand or category), brand personification (the brand as a person), picking pictures or words for a brand. Completion: unfinished sentences or stories; suited to limited time. Construction: a scene with people, where the respondent says what they think, say and do next; third-person questions ("what would a typical buyer say?"); speech and thought bubbles. Expressive: role play, drawing. Choice ordering: sorting or ranking brands and explaining the groups. Working rule: two or three techniques per session, not a full battery.
2. Write the stimuli. Donoghue's design rule is that a stimulus must be ambiguous enough for a personal reading yet structured enough to relate to the research concept. Working rules: 5 to 8 sentence stems, mixing the brand, the category and the user ("People who buy ... are ..."); for personification ask age, occupation, clothes, how the person speaks, who their friends are, what they would never do; for picture sorts use a neutral, varied set without logos.
3. Pretest every stimulus with two or three people (S3-02) and in both working languages (S3-03). Drop any that produce only literal descriptions or confusion.
4. Place the exercises after a warm-up, once the respondent is comfortable; in groups they can also be used to open discussion. Say that there are no right answers. Collect individual answers in writing before any group discussion so that people do not copy each other.
5. Always ask the respondent to explain their own output: "Why this picture?", "Tell me more about this person". The respondent's explanation is the data; Donoghue describes qualitative analysis as working from respondents' own descriptions. A picture or drawing without an explanation is not analysed.
6. For depth, run a metaphor elicitation interview. Published descriptions of ZMET report: 20 to 25 participants; a brief sent about a week before asking each person to collect pictures that show their thoughts and feelings about the topic (6 to 8 in some accounts, at least 12 in another); a recorded one-to-one interview of about two hours. Steps reported in those accounts: storytelling about each picture; missed images (what they could not find a picture for); a triad task (how two of three pictures are alike and differ from the third); metaphor probe or widening the frame of a picture; sensory metaphors (colour, taste, smell, touch, sound); a short vignette or story; a summary collage. ZMET itself is a proprietary method that needs trained interviewers. Working rule: this skill runs a shorter "metaphor interview" of 60 to 90 minutes with the first five steps and calls it that, not ZMET.
7. Record everything verbatim in the original language and photograph each output with the respondent code.
8. Code in two passes with two coders working separately. First mark what was said (FACT). Then group into constructs or themes with a short codebook (S4-01, S4-04). Count respondents per theme, not mentions. Compare the two coders, discuss differences and report simple agreement; the 2024 review recommends an agreement statistic such as Cohen's kappa when coded categories are counted.
9. For metaphor interviews, build a shared map only from constructs that recur. One account of the ZMET rule is that a construct enters the consensus map when at least one third of participants mention it and a link when at least one quarter do. A methods paper notes that this mapping step is not standardised and depends on expert judgement, so show the rule used.
10. Triangulate each projective finding (S2-07) against direct answers from the same people, reported behaviour, or another method, and record agree, disagree or untested. A disagreement is a finding to explore (S4-08), not an error to hide.
11. Report each finding as a hypothesis with its evidence, the coder agreement and the triangulation result. State that the sample is small and purposive and that frequencies do not describe the market. Hand hypotheses that matter for the decision to a survey (S1-08). Return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. Input: a Tashkent sofa maker ("Brand X") wants to know how its brand is seen. 12 in-depth interviews with recent sofa buyers. Each included personification of Brand X and 5 sentence stems, after the story-based part of the guide.

```
SENTENCE COMPLETION  12 respondents x 5 stems = 60 answers, coded by two coders
  Coders agreed on 51 of 60 = 85% simple agreement; 9 discussed and resolved            FACT
  Theme "expensive but lasts": 7 of 12 respondents | "for guests, not every day": 5 of 12
  Example: "Brand X divanini oladigan odamlar ... mehmon kutadiganlar"                  FACT
PERSONIFICATION  "If Brand X were a person..."
  Older, respected, formal man: 8 of 12 | young modern couple: 3 of 12 | no clear image: 1 of 12
  (8 + 3 + 1 = 12)   Example: "Солидный мужчина лет пятидесяти, в костюме"              FACT
DIRECT QUESTION in the same interviews: "Who is Brand X for?" - "for everyone": 9 of 12  FACT
TRIANGULATION  projective (formal, for guests) versus direct ("for everyone"): disagree
HYPOTHESIS  Brand X is felt to be respectable and formal, a sofa for the guest room, which
  may keep it out of everyday living-room choices.                              INTERPRETATION
NEXT  test in the usage and attitude survey (S1-08); check showroom sales by room type.
```
Arithmetic check: 12 x 5 = 60; 51 / 60 = 0.85; 60 - 51 = 9; 8 + 3 + 1 = 12. Counts are out of 12 interviews and are not market percentages.

## Common mistakes
- Treating the analyst's reading of a picture as the finding without the respondent's own explanation.
- One person coding alone, with no codebook and no agreement check.
- Presenting "8 of 12" as "two thirds of buyers".
- Running exercises cold in the first minutes, or in a group where the first speaker sets the answer.
- Stimuli that are too literal (they return descriptions) or too strange (they return embarrassment).
- Skipping triangulation because the story is attractive.
- Calling a 40-minute collage exercise "ZMET".
- Translating outputs before coding, which strips the metaphors.

## Quality checklist
- Is the stage exploratory and is a second data source planned?
- Were stimuli pretested in each language?
- Did every respondent explain their own output, recorded verbatim?
- Did two people code separately, and is their agreement reported?
- Are counts given as respondents out of the number interviewed, with no market percentages?
- Is every finding labelled FACT or INTERPRETATION and worded as a hypothesis?
- Is the triangulation result shown for each finding, including disagreements?

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Prepare stems and word lists separately in Uzbek and Russian with a native speaker; a metaphor rarely survives literal translation. Keep answers and quotes in the original language through coding.
- Polite or agreeing answer habits are a reason to use third-person and projective questions, and also a reason to check them: people may give a polite projective answer too. Compare with behaviour.
- Expect some respondents to find personification or drawing childish or uncomfortable. Offer a simpler option (picture choice, sentence stems) and never press.
- Pre-task picture collection by Telegram is practical in Tashkent; recruit and brief differently in regions and for older respondents, where phone-based pre-tasks may exclude people.
- Picture sets should come from the local visual world. Whether a symbol carries the meaning the team assumes is a question for the pretest, not something to assume.
- The theory and the published tests come mostly from Western samples. Treat any transferred reading of a symbol or metaphor as a hypothesis.
