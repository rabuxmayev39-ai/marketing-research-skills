# 95-5 rule: most buyers are out of market (S6-06)

The 95-5 rule says that in categories bought rarely, only a small share of possible buyers is ready to buy in any short period, so most advertising reaches people who will not buy soon. It comes from Professor John Dawes of the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute, written up in 2021 for the LinkedIn B2B Institute, and was popularised by Peter Weinberg and Jon Lombardo of that institute. The arithmetic is simple: if firms replace a supplier about once in five years, about one fifth are in the market in a year and about one twentieth (5%) in a quarter. The conclusion drawn is that advertising in such categories works mainly by building memory of the brand that is used later, when the buyer enters the market on their own schedule.

Evidence grade: B. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. The rule is a mental model from a respected research institute, not a measured law. Dawes's own text calls the 95% a heuristic and says "up to" 95%; the real share depends on the purchase cycle and on the length of the period chosen. The Marketing Week article notes that Dawes had not written the rule down before, and the figures in it are illustrations. A published criticism (Harrison and Moroney, opened for this skill) is that the arithmetic assumes a stable market of repeat buyers; in a fast-growing category many buyers are first-time buyers and the in-market share can be several times higher. Use the logic and compute your own number; never quote "5%" as a fact about a client's category.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (cycle data, counts) versus INTERPRETATION (what the share means for the plan). Always state the time window next to any in-market share. When the estimate and the split of goals are delivered, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- The category is bought rarely: B2B equipment and services, furniture, housing, renovation, cars, education.
- A client expects a short campaign to produce large immediate sales and needs a realistic ceiling.
- The team must explain why brand advertising and lead generation need different goals and time frames.

Do not use it when:
- The category is bought weekly or monthly (bread, juice, mobile top-ups). Nearly everyone is in the market in any month; use S6-03 and S6-05.
- The category is new and growing fast. The stable-market arithmetic understates the in-market share; correct it in step 4 or do not use the rule.
- Someone wants it as proof that performance advertising is useless. Dawes's text says targeting in-market buyers is worthwhile; the point is balance.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. A definition of the category and the buying unit (a household, a company, a clinic), plus the size of the buyer universe (S4-23).
2. Evidence on how often the category is bought: client order history, survey answers, published statistics (S1-03).
3. Whether the category is growing: new households, new businesses, first-time buyers per year.
4. The client's current sales, share of orders and campaign expectations.
5. The planning window: month, quarter or year.

## Procedure
1. Define the buyer universe and the purchase event. Write one sentence each: who counts as a potential buyer, and what counts as "buying" (first purchase, replacement, switching supplier). FACT if counted from records, otherwise an estimate with its source.
2. Estimate the average time between purchases. In order of preference: the client's repeat-order records; a survey question on how often the product is bought, with answer options from "each quarter" to "once in five years or less often" (Dawes suggests this approach when the interval is unknown); desk sources. Stated frequency is often inaccurate, so compare it with records where possible (S1-23).
3. Compute the stable-market share. Working rule derived from Dawes's arithmetic: in-market share in a window = window length / average time between purchases. His examples: a five-year cycle gives 20% a year and about 5% a quarter; a two-year cycle gives 50% a year and about 13% a quarter. The rule only makes sense when the window is shorter than the cycle.
4. Correct for growth. Add buyers who enter the category for the first time in the window (new firms, new households, new users). Working rule: if first-time buyers are more than a quarter of all buyers in the window, report the two groups separately, because the stable-market model no longer describes the category. The critics' figures on growing markets are their own estimates; do not reuse them.
5. Set the ceiling. In-market buyers in the window x the brand's realistic share of them = the most orders a campaign in that window can win. Dawes makes this point directly: a campaign cannot bring in buyers who are not in the market. Compare the ceiling with the client's target and say plainly if the target is out of reach.
6. Split the objectives in two (S9-01, S6-05). For in-market buyers: be easy to find and to buy from (search, marketplaces, sales response, price lists). For out-of-market buyers: build memory links between the brand and the situations in which the category is bought, called category entry points (S1-21, S9-02). The Marketing Week article gives three consequences: expect most effects to come later, favour memorable creative over "buy now" pressure, and reach the whole category over long periods.
7. Plan timing. Dawes advises spreading spend across the year, since a brand that advertises in one quarter is absent when the other buyers enter the market. Favour reach across all potential buyers over repeated contact with the same few (S9-03).
8. Choose measures that fit each goal (S7-01). Short term: enquiries and orders from in-market buyers against the ceiling in step 5. Long term: brand recall and links to category entry points among all category buyers (S4-19, S1-22). The Institute's page reports that established brands are typically linked to entry points by 20-30% of respondents and leaders by about 50%; treat these as rough reference points from its own studies, not targets.
9. Report the estimate with its window, the growth correction, the ceiling and the two sets of goals. Return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. Input: a Tashkent maker of office furniture wants 60 orders from a one-month campaign. Client records suggest an office is refurnished about once in 8 years.

```
INPUTS                                                              FACT (invented)
Universe: 4,000 existing offices in the target group
Average time between purchases: 8 years
New offices opening: 200 per year (first-time buyers)
Brand's share of orders it competes for: 8%
ESTIMATE
Replacement buyers per year:    4,000 / 8 = 500
Replacement buyers per quarter: 500 / 4 = 125
First-time buyers per quarter:  200 / 4 = 50   (29% of the 175: report separately)
In market per quarter:          125 + 50 = 175
Share of universe per quarter:  175 / 4,050 = 4.3%   (4,000 + 50 new)
In market per month:            175 / 3 = about 58
CEILING
Orders per quarter at 8% share: 175 x 0.08 = 14
Client target: 60 orders in one month = 103% of all in-market buyers (60 / 58.3)
READING                                                          INTERPRETATION
The target cannot be met by any campaign. A realistic quarter is about 14
orders; raising the share of in-market buyers needs memory built earlier
among the roughly 96% not buying this quarter.
```
Arithmetic check: 4,000 / 8 = 500; 500 / 4 = 125; 200 / 4 = 50; 125 + 50 = 175; 50 / 175 = 0.286, or 29%; 4,000 + 50 = 4,050; 175 / 4,050 = 0.0432, or 4.3%; 175 / 3 = 58.3; 175 x 0.08 = 14; 60 / 58.3 = 1.03, or 103%; 100% - 4.3% = 95.7%, about 96%.

## Common mistakes
- Quoting "5% are in market" for any category without computing the cycle and the window.
- Giving a share with no time window. "5%" per quarter and "5%" per month are different claims.
- Ignoring first-time buyers in a growing category.
- Reading the rule as "do only brand advertising" and neglecting the buyers who are ready now.
- Judging memory-building advertising by orders in the first weeks.
- Trusting stated purchase frequency from a survey without checking it against records.

## Quality checklist
- Is the buyer universe defined and its size sourced?
- Is the time between purchases based on records or a stated estimate, with the source named?
- Does every in-market share carry its time window?
- Were first-time buyers added and reported?
- Is the campaign ceiling compared with the client's target?
- Are there separate goals and measures for in-market and out-of-market buyers?
- Is the result labelled as an estimate from a mental model, not a law?

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Purchase cycles here may differ from the Western B2B examples in the sources. Hypothesis to check, not a fact: in categories tied to new construction, weddings or new business openings, first-time buyers may be a large part of demand, so step 4 matters.
- Ask the client for order history first. Many firms keep it in spreadsheets or a CRM, and it is better evidence than a survey answer.
- A Telegram-bot survey on purchase frequency misses older and rural buyers and may over-represent active, recent buyers, which shortens the apparent cycle.
- Polite or agreeing answer habits can inflate "we plan to buy soon"; ask about the last purchase date instead of intentions.
- Compute Tashkent and the regions separately if the client sells in both; do not assume one cycle fits all.
