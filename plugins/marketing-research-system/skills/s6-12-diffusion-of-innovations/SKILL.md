# Diffusion of innovations (S6-12)

Diffusion of innovations is Everett Rogers's theory of how something perceived as new (an idea, practice or product) spreads through a group of people over time. It was first published in 1962; the standard reference is the fifth edition (2003). The theory has four elements: the innovation, the communication channels that carry it, time, and the social system. Two parts matter most for marketing. First, five perceived attributes affect how fast a thing is adopted: relative advantage, compatibility, complexity, trialability and observability. Second, adoption over time tends to follow an S-shaped curve (slow start, steep middle, levelling off), and adopters can be sorted by how early they adopt: innovators, early adopters, early majority, late majority and laggards.

Evidence grade: A. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. A systematic review opened for this skill (Greenhalgh and colleagues, 2004) rates the evidence for relative advantage, compatibility, low complexity and trialability as strong, and adds a sixth helpful attribute, reinvention (adopters can adapt the thing). The same review sets limits. The attributes are necessary but not sufficient, and they are not fixed properties: they depend on the adopter and the setting. It found little empirical support for using labels such as "early adopter" and "laggard" as explanations. Other published criticisms: the theory assumes adoption is good (pro-innovation bias), can blame individuals for barriers such as cost or access, and defines adopter groups after the fact by adoption date, so the groups describe but do not predict. Use the attributes as a diagnostic with good support, and the adopter groups as a descriptive vocabulary only.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (adoption counts, what respondents said) versus INTERPRETATION (attribute ratings, who will adopt next). Do not label a named person or segment a "laggard" as if it were a trait. When the diagnosis and plan are delivered, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- A client launches something buyers see as new: a product type, a channel, a way of ordering or paying, a new habit.
- Adoption has started but is slower than hoped and the team needs to know why.
- The team must decide whom to reach first and through which channels.

Do not use it when:
- The product is a familiar item in an established category; growth there is better read with S6-03 and S4-18.
- A sales forecast is needed. The curve describes past diffusion; it does not give a date or a ceiling for a new case.
- Non-adoption is caused by price or access alone. Check S6-14 (4A) and S5-07 first, because the theory tends to look at the person and miss the barrier.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. A plain description of the innovation and of what people do today instead.
2. The social system: who the potential adopters are and how many (S4-23).
3. Adoption data over time if any exist: first purchases or first uses per week or month.
4. Qualitative evidence from adopters, people who tried and stopped, and people who refused (S1-04, S1-07).
5. Who potential adopters ask for advice, and where they can see others using the thing.

## Procedure
1. Define the innovation from the adopter's side. Ask: what is new for them, and what must they stop doing? Note that newness is a perception; something old elsewhere can be new here.
2. Define the unit that adopts (person, household, shop, company) and the population size. For companies, map who decides (S1-18).
3. Rate the five perceived attributes using evidence from potential adopters, not the team's view:
   - Relative advantage: how much better it seems than the current way (cost, time, convenience, status).
   - Compatibility: how well it fits existing values, routines and needs.
   - Complexity: how hard it seems to understand or use. Lower is better.
   - Trialability: whether it can be tried on a small scale first.
   - Observability: whether others can see it being used and see its results.
   Working rule: score each 1 to 5 from interview or survey evidence, with complexity reverse-scored as "ease", and quote one piece of evidence per score. The review notes that demonstration lowers perceived complexity.
4. Check for reinvention: can adopters adapt it to their own situation? The review found adaptable innovations are adopted more easily.
5. Find the weakest attributes and design a fix for each. Working rule menu: low advantage - change the offer or show the gain in the adopter's own numbers; low compatibility - fit it to an existing routine; high complexity - simplify, demonstrate, set up for the customer; low trialability - samples, pilot, trial period, guarantee; low observability - make use visible and show named peers.
6. Locate people in the decision process. Sources describe adoption as a process running from first awareness through a decision to later confirmation. Ask where people stop: never heard, heard but not convinced, decided but did not start, started and dropped. Working rule: count people at each stop from survey or CRM data (S0-06).
7. Plan channels. The theory treats communication channels as a core element, and the review describes influence through social networks and opinion leaders (people whose views others follow). It also reports that formal programmes to train opinion leaders showed positive but small effects, and that opinion leaders can push in either direction. Working rule: use broad media for awareness and peers, demonstrations and visits for persuasion.
8. If adoption data exist, plot cumulative adopters over time and new adopters per period. Rogers's descriptive split, as reported in teaching sources, is innovators 2.5%, early adopters 13.5%, early majority 34%, late majority 34%, laggards 16%. Use it to describe where the count stands, not to forecast. Working rule: when new adopters per period fall for two periods in a row, check whether the reachable group is running out or a barrier has appeared.
9. Study non-adopters as people with reasons. Run S4-07 and S4-09 on refusers and drop-outs.
10. Deliver the attribute table, the stop-point counts, the fixes and a test plan (S7-02). Return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. Input: an invented flour mill in Samarkand region moves its 400 bakery customers from phone orders to a Telegram ordering bot. Twenty bakers were interviewed; CRM gives first bot orders by month.

```
ADOPTION (FACT)            month 1   2     3     4     5
Cumulative bakeries          8      22    58    130   196
New in the month             8      14    36    72    66
Share of 400                 2.0%   5.5%  14.5% 32.5% 49.0%
Descriptive split of 400: first 10 (2.5%), next 54 (13.5%), next 136 (34%),
  next 136 (34%), last 64 (16%)
ATTRIBUTES, mean of 20 interviews, 1-5 (INTERPRETATION of answers)
Relative advantage 3.9 | Compatibility 2.4 | Ease (low complexity) 3.1
Trialability 4.2 | Observability 1.8
READING                                                       INTERPRETATION
New adopters fell in month 5 (66 against 72): one period, so watch, not act.
Weakest: observability (nobody sees a neighbour ordering by bot) and
  compatibility (owners order by phone at night after counting cash).
Fixes: drivers show the bot at delivery; bot accepts voice messages;
  delivery note prints "ordered by bot". Test in two districts (S7-02).
```
Arithmetic check: 8/400 = 0.020; 22/400 = 0.055; 58/400 = 0.145; 130/400 = 0.325; 196/400 = 0.490. New: 22-8 = 14; 58-22 = 36; 130-58 = 72; 196-130 = 66. Split: 400 x 0.025 = 10; 400 x 0.135 = 54; 400 x 0.34 = 136; 400 x 0.16 = 64; 10+54+136+136+64 = 400.

## Common mistakes
- Rating the attributes from the team's opinion of its own product.
- Treating adopter groups as fixed personality types or as ready-made target segments.
- Reading the percentage split as a forecast of how many will adopt and when.
- Assuming non-adopters are backward when the cause is price, access or a real disadvantage.
- Targeting only the most novelty-loving buyers and assuming the rest will follow.
- Ignoring people who adopted and then stopped.

## Quality checklist
- Is the innovation described from the adopter's side, including what they give up?
- Does each attribute score cite evidence from potential adopters?
- Are the two weakest attributes named with a specific fix each?
- Are stop points in the decision process counted, not guessed?
- Were refusers and drop-outs interviewed?
- Are adopter-group labels used only as description?
- Does the report avoid forecasting from the curve?

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Collect attribute evidence in Uzbek and Russian and keep quotes in the original language.
- Polite or agreeing answers inflate stated interest in new things. Count trials and repeat use (S1-23), not "would you use it".
- Word of mouth through family, neighbourhood and trade peers may carry much of the persuasion; treat this as a hypothesis and ask each respondent whom they consulted.
- Adoption in Tashkent may run ahead of the regions; plot them separately before drawing one curve.
- A Telegram-bot survey reaches people who already use Telegram, so it overstates readiness for digital innovations and misses older and rural people.
- The theory was built largely on studies in other countries; the review itself warns that findings do not transfer readily to new settings.
