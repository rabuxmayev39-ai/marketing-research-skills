# Message strategy from Category Entry Points (S9-02)

A Category Entry Point (CEP) is a cue that brings a category to a buyer's mind when a buying situation starts: a moment, a place, a need, a feeling, a companion. The idea was developed by Professor Jenni Romaniuk of the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute for Marketing Science; her argument is that buyers first pull a short list of brands from memory, so a brand linked to more of these cues, in more buyers' minds, gets considered more often. This skill covers one job only: taking a measured list of CEPs and deciding which of them the communication will link the brand to, in what order, and with what message. Finding and measuring the CEPs is S1-21 and S4-19.

Evidence grade: B. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. CEPs come from an academic research institute, and its journal papers were not opened for this skill; the selection routine used here (the "3Cs") is a procedure from an institute report published by LinkedIn, which sells advertising. It is not a tested experiment. The evidence opened for this skill is correlational: in a study of 17 United States business insurance products, buyers who linked more CEPs to their insurer gave a lower stated chance of switching in 16 of them. Romaniuk herself notes that stated chances are not actual switching. The Institute also warns that the term is turning into a buzzword and that more CEPs do not automatically mean more growth. S6-03 lists published criticism of the wider Ehrenberg-Bass approach, mainly that it underplays differentiation. Listed limit: without CEP research the situations are guesses, so the output must then be labelled a hypothesis.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (survey figures) versus INTERPRETATION (which CEPs to choose, message wording). Never present a CEP as "owned" by the brand. When the message matrix is written, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- CEP survey results exist and the team must decide what the brand layer of communication should say.
- Messages so far describe the product ("quality", "natural") and never show a buying situation.
- Several executions are planned for the year and their topics must be chosen and ordered.

Do not use it when:
- No CEP data exist and there is time to collect them. Run S1-21 first.
- The task is a short-term offer (discount, deadline). That is activation; see S6-05 and S6-15.
- The question is how the brand differs from rivals in a buyer's comparison. Use S6-02; CEPs say when the brand is thought of, not why it is preferred.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. The CEP list with, for each one, how common it is among category buyers (S1-21).
2. Brand-by-CEP link figures for the client and main rivals, with expected values and deviations (S4-19).
3. The communication objectives, especially the THINK objective (S9-01).
4. What the product range can and cannot honestly deliver in each situation.
5. The budget and number of executions the year allows (S9-05, S9-07).
6. The brand's distinctive assets (S6-04), because every message must be clearly branded.

## Procedure
1. Check that each item on the list is a real CEP. A CEP describes the buyer's situation, not the brand. The Institute lists as common errors mixing CEPs up with brand values, selling points or "category drivers", and treating the list as complete. Delete items such as "high quality" and reword them as situations where possible. The report's prompts help: when, where, while doing what, with what, why, how feeling, with or for whom.
2. Apply the first C, credibility. The report uses two filters: an outside one (do buyers already link at least some brands, and ours, to this cue, or do they answer "none"?) and an inside one (can our products and pipeline really serve it?). Ask the user for proof for each remaining CEP. Drop those the offer cannot deliver.
3. Apply the second C, competitiveness. Using S4-19 output, mark where a rival's link is clearly above what its size predicts. The report treats a deviation of more than 5 percentage points above expected as a mental advantage and more than 5 below as a disadvantage, and calls this a sensible cut-off, not a law. Avoid CEPs where several rivals hold an advantage.
4. Apply the third C, commonality or currency. Prefer CEPs that many buyers meet. Where deal sizes differ a lot, as in B2B, the report advises weighing by money value instead of by count.
5. Form the shortlist and the longer list. The report suggests about five CEPs to start, inside a longer list of five to eight for the short, medium and long term. Working rule: with a small budget, start with two or three and add one per budget period.
6. Do not try to claim a CEP for the brand alone. The Institute calls this a strategic error because buyers rarely link one brand only to a cue. The aim is to be among the brands that come to mind, across many cues.
7. Build the message matrix: one row per chosen CEP, with the situation in the buyer's words, the message, the proof, the distinctive assets shown, and the layer (brand). The report's rule is one CEP per execution, since a single clear message is easier to remember, and a portfolio of executions over time.
8. Show brand and situation together. The report asks for the buying situation to be pictured and clearly branded so the two are stored together in memory. Working rule: the brand should be recognisable while the situation is on screen, not only in the last frame.
9. Order and weight the executions. Working rule: start with CEPs where the brand is at or above expected and commonness is high (easy to defend and extend), then add weaker ones; weight exposure roughly by commonness. Plan to return to each CEP, because the report stresses that memory links fade and need refreshing.
10. Hand over: the matrix goes to S6-18 (one brief per execution), S9-03 (broad reach, since links must be built among all category buyers), S9-06 (testing) and S4-19 for the next measurement wave. Return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. A flour mill near Tashkent sells to small bakeries. A survey of 200 bakery owners (S1-21, S4-19) gave these figures. "Link" is the share of owners who connect the mill with the situation; "Exp." is the share expected for a brand of its size.

```
CEP (buyer's situation)                    Common  Link  Exp.  Dev.  Rival adv.  Credible
A  last batch from supplier behaved badly    58%    27%   20%   +7    none        yes (batch lab)
B  stocking up before a peak season          64%    17%   18%   -1    none        yes (24h delivery)
C  opening a second shop                     12%     9%    8%   +1    none        yes
D  flour price has just jumped               71%    13%   19%   -6    big mill +8 yes
E  customer asks for a new bread type        27%    12%   14%   -2    none        no (no special flours)
F  cash is short, need deferred payment      40%    16%   15%   +1    none        no (owner refuses credit)

DECISION                                                              INTERPRETATION
Shortlist: A (advantage, defend and extend), B (common, uncontested, build)
Longer list: C (rare, but each case can double an order -> currency rule)
Dropped: D (rival advantage; price is not our way to win), E and F (not credible)
MESSAGE MATRIX
A  "When a batch lets you down" -> same dough behaviour every batch | proof: batch log
B  "Before the busy weeks" -> ordered today, delivered within 24 hours | proof: route plan
Exposure weight by commonness: A 47.5%, B 52.5%. One execution each; C next year.
```
Arithmetic check: deviations 27 - 20 = +7; 17 - 18 = -1; 9 - 8 = +1; 13 - 19 = -6; 12 - 14 = -2; 16 - 15 = +1. Counts: 0.58 x 200 = 116 owners; 0.64 x 200 = 128; 0.71 x 200 = 142; 0.12 x 200 = 24. Weights: 58 + 64 = 122; 58 / 122 = 47.5%; 64 / 122 = 52.5%; 47.5 + 52.5 = 100. With 200 respondents the margin of error is roughly 6 to 7 points (S2-03), so +7 is a borderline advantage, to be confirmed in the next wave.

## Common mistakes
- Calling brand values or product claims ("trust", "quality") Category Entry Points.
- Choosing CEPs in a meeting with no buyer data and presenting them as findings.
- Trying to own one CEP exclusively, as in classic single-idea positioning.
- Putting three situations into one advertisement.
- Picking a common CEP the product cannot serve.
- Showing the situation with weak branding, so the memory goes to the category or to the leader.
- Running one CEP once and never returning to it.
- Reading small deviations from a small sample as real differences.

## Quality checklist
- Is every item a buyer situation and not a brand claim?
- Does each chosen CEP have commonness and link figures from research, or a clear "hypothesis" label?
- Was each of the three Cs applied and the reason for every drop written down?
- Is there one CEP per execution?
- Does every message have proof the client can show?
- Are brand assets present while the situation is shown?
- Is there a schedule for adding and refreshing CEPs?
- Is a follow-up measurement planned?

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Write each CEP in the buyers' own words in Uzbek and in Russian; do not translate word for word (S3-03). The same situation may be named differently in the two languages.
- Situations may differ between Tashkent and the regions. Treat a CEP list built in Tashkent as a hypothesis elsewhere and check commonness separately.
- Telegram-bot survey samples miss older and rural people, so commonness figures describe the sample, not the whole market.
- Polite or agreeing answer habits can inflate brand links for every brand. Read deviations from expected, not raw percentages (S3-07).
- The CEP studies opened were run in the United States and the United Kingdom. Whether more links mean more buying here is a hypothesis to track, not a fact.
