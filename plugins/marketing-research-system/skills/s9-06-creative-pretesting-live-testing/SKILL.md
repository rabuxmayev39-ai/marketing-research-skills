# Creative pre-testing and live testing (S9-06)

Creative pre-testing means showing draft or finished advertising to people from the target group before the media money is spent, to see whether they notice it, understand it and connect it to the right brand. Live testing means running two or more versions for real, on a small budget, and comparing what people actually do (click, write, sign up, buy). Pre-testing has no single author; it grew out of the "copy testing" services of research companies. Live testing follows the logic of the controlled experiment, described for online use by Ron Kohavi, Diane Tang and Ya Xu in Trustworthy Online Controlled Experiments (2020). The purpose of both is the same: to find weak or misunderstood creative while changing it is still cheap.

Evidence grade: B. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. Testing before spending is standard practice, but the evidence that test scores predict sales is mixed, and this is the main limit: what people say they like predicts what sells poorly. The Ehrenberg-Bass Institute reported finding no consistent evidence that traditional pre-test measures reliably predict sales, while noting that some techniques are better than others. In a study of 96 pairs of print ads, Armstrong and colleagues found that copy tests based on purchase intention picked the better-recalled ad in 57% of predictions, where guessing gives 50%. Hartnett and colleagues found that marketers' own predictions of which TV ads sell were no better than chance, so skipping tests and trusting judgement is not safer. Test vendors publish their own validations (one is listed in Sources); these are the sellers' figures and were not independently checked here. Critics in the research trade also argue that single pass/fail scores push advertisers towards safe, similar-looking ads. Live tests measure behaviour and so deserve more weight, but tests run inside advertising platforms have their own flaw (step 6). Use this method to remove clear failures and to choose between versions, not to forecast sales.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (counts, rates, what respondents said) versus INTERPRETATION (why one version did better, what will happen at full spend). Do not repeat sample-size and experiment-design detail that belongs to S1-09 and S4-10; point there. When the test is read and the decision recorded, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- A media buy is planned that is large for this client, and the creative can still be changed.
- Two or more messages or executions are on the table and the team disagrees on which to run.
- The message could be misread, or the audience speaks two languages and both versions must work.

Do not use it when:
- The strategy and message are not agreed. Testing cannot repair a missing strategy; go back to S6-18 and S9-02.
- The spend is so small that a test would cost more than a mistake. Working rule: just run it and read the results.
- The team wants a number to defend a decision already taken. Say so and decline to present it as a test.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. The creative brief (S6-18) with the objective, audience and single message, and the communication objectives (S9-01).
2. The versions to test, at the same level of finish, plus the current ad as a comparison if one exists.
3. A way to reach the target group: a recruited sample, a Telegram bot, or a small paid media budget.
4. One success measure agreed in advance, with the outcome that would lead to each decision.
5. The time available before the media booking deadline.

## Procedure
1. Write the decision and the criteria before any testing. Ask: "What will we do if A wins, if B wins, if neither is better, if both fail?" An IPA guide on briefing reports that only 30% of brands have evaluation criteria for creative work; a test without them becomes an argument about taste. Record the team's own prediction as well (S2-11).
2. Pick the level of test. (a) Understanding check: 5 to 10 one-to-one sessions where people watch or read and then say in their own words what it was and who it was from; this borrows the think-aloud idea of S3-02. (b) Survey pre-test: each respondent sees one version only, then answers the same questions. (c) Live test: versions run in real media on a small budget and behaviour is counted. Working rule: always do (a); add (c) whenever behaviour can be counted; use (b) when it cannot (TV, outdoor, radio).
3. Choose measures, behaviour first (S1-23). In a live test: sign-ups, messages, orders, redeemed codes, in that order of preference over clicks and views. In a survey pre-test, working rule, ask in this order: what do you remember (open answer), which brand was it for (open answer), what was it telling you (open answer), was anything unclear or unpleasant. Treat liking scores and "would you buy" as weak signals, for the reasons given under the evidence grade.
4. Make the comparison fair. Following Kohavi and colleagues: split people at random between versions, agree one evaluation measure in advance, and change nothing else (same audience, dates, budget, placements). Their 2009 guide notes that an even 50/50 split gives the most statistical power and advises running for whole weeks, at least one to two, so weekday and weekend behaviour are both covered, and watching for novelty effects that fade.
5. Size the test and decide how big a difference matters (S1-09 for design, S2-03 for survey samples, S4-10 for the significance check). Working rule: if the difference is inside the noise, report "no detectable difference", not a winner.
6. Know what a platform test can tell you. Braun and Schwartz (Journal of Marketing) show that ad platforms deliver each version to a different mix of users chosen by the platform's algorithm, which they call divergent delivery. So a platform "A/B test" shows which ad will perform better on that platform under those settings; it is not clean evidence about why, and it should not be carried over to TV, outdoor or strategy without saying so.
7. Read users and non-users of the brand separately when the data allow. Ehrenberg-Bass research found that people who already use a brand remember its advertising more than non-users do, so a sample full of current customers flatters every version.
8. Judge brand-building creative with care. Binet and Field's work for the IPA finds that short-term response and long-term brand effects behave differently, so a quick response count under-reads advertising meant to build memory. Working rule: for brand-layer creative (S6-05), put most weight on correct brand naming and message take-out, and do not kill it on click rate alone.
9. Decide using the criteria from step 1: run the winner; if no difference, choose on fit with the strategy and on cost; if both fail on understanding or brand naming, return to the brief. Write what was learned for the next brief (S8-08).
10. Report with FACT and INTERPRETATION separated and the limits stated. Return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. A language school in Tashkent plans to spend 300,000,000 soum on lead advertising in one quarter. Two video versions exist: A shows a student answering a job interview in English; B shows prices and a discount. Criterion agreed in advance: sign-ups for a trial lesson per person reached. Team prediction: B wins.

```
UNDERSTANDING CHECK (8 sessions): 3 of 8 could not name the school after B.  FACT
LIVE TEST: 14 days, 6,000,000 soum per version, same audience settings
                     A            B
People reached     40,000       40,000
Clicks to the bot     520          610      click rate  1.30%  vs 1.525%
Trial sign-ups         96           68      sign-up rate 0.24% vs 0.17%
Cost per sign-up   62,500       88,235 soum
Significance (S4-10): pooled rate 0.205%, standard error 0.032 points,
  difference 0.07 points, z = 2.19 (above 1.96)                         FACT
DECISION: run A. B wins on clicks but loses on the agreed measure.
INTERPRETATION: the discount attracts clicks from people who do not sign up.
LIMIT: platform test, delivery not fully random (step 6); result holds for
  this platform and audience only. Team prediction (B) was wrong.
```
Arithmetic check: 520 / 40,000 = 0.0130; 610 / 40,000 = 0.01525; 96 / 40,000 = 0.0024; 68 / 40,000 = 0.0017; 6,000,000 / 96 = 62,500; 6,000,000 / 68 = 88,235; pooled (96 + 68) / 80,000 = 0.00205; standard error = square root of (0.00205 x 0.99795 x 2 / 40,000) = 0.00032; 0.0007 / 0.00032 = 2.19; test cost 12,000,000 / 300,000,000 = 4% of the budget.

## Common mistakes
- Choosing the success measure after seeing the results.
- Declaring a winner on clicks or likes when sign-ups or sales were available.
- Asking a focus group "which one do you like?" and treating the vote as a forecast.
- Showing every version to the same respondent, so the first one colours the rest.
- Testing versions that differ in five ways and then explaining the result by one of them.
- Stopping the test on the first day one version is ahead.
- Using a platform test, or a sample of the client's own subscribers, as proof about people in general.

## Quality checklist
- Were the decision rule and the single success measure written down before the test?
- Is the main measure a behaviour, or, where that was impossible, brand naming and message take-out in open answers?
- Were people split between versions at random, with everything else kept the same?
- Did the test run for whole weeks, and was the sample size planned (S1-09, S2-03)?
- Was the difference checked against noise (S4-10)?
- Are the platform and audience limits of the result stated?
- Were users and non-users read separately where possible?
- Is the team's prior prediction recorded next to the result?

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Test the Uzbek and the Russian version separately; a line that works in one language may be misread or sound translated in the other. Keep respondents' answers in the original language.
- Polite or agreeing answers are likely when people are shown an ad and asked their opinion face to face. Open "what was it about, who was it from" questions and behaviour counts are less exposed to this than rating scales (S3-07).
- A Telegram bot or the client's own channel reaches existing followers and misses older and rural people; treat such a sample as a convenience sample and say so.
- Tashkent results may not hold in the regions; if the media plan covers both, test in both or mark the transfer as a hypothesis. The studies cited used Western and Australian data; treat their numbers as background, not as local benchmarks.
- Hypothesis, not verified: platform audiences here may be small enough that a two-week test reaches too few people for a clear result. Check the reach estimate before promising a winner.
