# Project retrospective (S7-04)

A project retrospective is a short, structured team discussion held when a project ends. It compares what was expected with what happened, looks for the causes of the gap, and fixes what the team will keep or change next time. This skill joins two traditions. The first is the after-action review (AAR) of the US Army, developed in the mid-1970s for training exercises and described in Army guidance as a professional discussion in which the participants themselves work out what happened and why. The second is the project retrospective of Norman Kerth (2001), who brought the practice to software projects and gave it its name and its ground rule: assume everyone did the best they could with what they knew and had at the time. The output of this skill is one written case file, which S8-08 (own case studies) stores and reuses.

Evidence grade: C. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. The grade is given for the agency use described here: a written end-of-project review. The honest picture is mixed. A meta-analysis by Tannenbaum and Cerasoli (2013; 46 samples, 2,136 people) found that debriefs raised later performance by about 25% on average, but most of those studies were quasi-experimental (not fully randomised), many were in training or simulation, and none tested a marketing agency's written case files. A US Army Research Institute report (Morrison and Meliza, 1999) says support for the AAR rests largely on testimonials and wide adoption, not on controlled measurement. The listed limit is the main risk: a retrospective is only useful if it is written down and reread. A US audit of NASA (GAO, 2002) found that 58% of surveyed managers said the lessons systems did not give them the right lesson at the right time, and that managers were reluctant to record negative lessons. Treat the output as the team's reasoned account of one project, not as proof of what causes what.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (what the records show) versus INTERPRETATION (why the team thinks it happened). Never name a person as the cause of a problem; write causes as conditions, decisions and processes. When the case file is written and filed, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- A project has just closed, whether it went well, badly or was stopped early.
- A stage inside a long project has ended (fieldwork, a campaign wave) and the next stage can still be changed.
- The team is about to repeat a similar project and no written record of the last one exists.

Do not use it when:
- The aim is to judge or rate staff. Army and wildland-fire guidance both state that the review is not a critique or a reprimand.
- The results are not in yet. Wait for the numbers agreed in S7-01, or run a short interim review and mark it interim.
- The aim is to prove that the campaign caused the sales change. That needs S7-02 or S7-03; the retrospective only records what was seen.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. The expectations written before the work started: objectives and KPIs (S7-01), the hypotheses list (S2-11), the brief (S2-01), the plan and budget.
2. The results: final numbers with their sources, the deliverables, and client feedback in the client's own words.
3. The project log: dates, changes of plan, problems and who was told.
4. Everyone who did the work, for 60 to 90 minutes, and a facilitator who was not the project lead if possible. Working rule: with only two or three people, the lead may facilitate but speaks last.
5. The case file template from S8-08.

## Procedure
1. Fix the timing. Hold the session as soon as possible after the end; Army practice is to review while events are fresh. Working rule: within 10 working days of delivery, plus a 15-minute update when delayed results (sales, enrolments) arrive.
2. Prepare the facts sheet before the meeting. One page: each expectation next to its result, with the source. Do not use meeting time to look numbers up.
3. Open with the ground rules. State Kerth's rule (everyone did their best given what they knew and had). Add the rules listed in the wildland-fire AAR guidance: candour, equal voice for all roles, focus on improving the work, and confidentiality of what is said.
4. Question 1: what was expected? Read out the objectives, the plan and the written hypotheses. If nothing was written beforehand, record that as the first finding and reconstruct expectations from the brief, marking them "reconstructed".
5. Question 2: what happened? Build one shared timeline from records and from each person's account. Mark each line FACT with its source. Where two accounts differ, write both.
6. Question 3: why? For each important gap, positive or negative, ask why several times until a cause the team can act on appears (see S0-04). Write each cause as INTERPRETATION and note what evidence supports it. Ask what else could explain the gap: season, competitor action, price change, a measurement problem.
7. Question 4: what will we keep and what will we change? The fire-service guidance suggests spending about a quarter of the time on questions 1 and 2 together, a quarter on question 3 and half on question 4. For each change write the action, the owner, the date and where it will be built in (a template, a checklist, a skill). Working rule: at most three changes per project; a long list is not carried out.
8. Record wins and mistakes with equal care. A lucky success should be recorded as luck. Ask "what did we get wrong or nearly get wrong?" directly.
9. Write the case file the same day using the S8-08 format: decision, what was expected, method, what happened, numbers, mistakes, what transfers. Remove or mask confidential client details as S8-08 requires.
10. Close the loop. File the case in the Case-Studies folder, add the changes to the relevant templates, and put a line in the next project's kickoff agenda: "read the last three similar case files". Return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. A language school in Tashkent ran a six-week Telegram and Instagram lead campaign planned with the agency.

```
FACTS SHEET                          EXPECTED          HAPPENED
Budget (soums)                       10,000,000        10,400,000
Leads (form filled)                  400               520
Cost per lead                        25,000            20,000
Share of leads who enrolled          15%               7.5%
New students                         60                39
Cost per new student                 166,667           266,667
WHY (INTERPRETATION)
1. Leads rose because a quiz-style ad was cheap to click; many quiz leads
   wanted the result, not a course (28 of 30 sampled quiz leads said so).
2. The school called leads back after 2 days on average; no call-back
   time was agreed in the plan.
3. Other possible causes not ruled out: exam season, a rival's discount.
KEEP: weekly numbers review. CHANGE (max 3):
 a) KPI is cost per enrolled student, not cost per lead - owner: strategist
 b) agree call-back within 24 hours before launch - owner: account manager
 c) test quiz vs trial-lesson ad with S7-02 before scaling - owner: media
```
Arithmetic check: 10,000,000 / 400 = 25,000; 10,400,000 / 520 = 20,000; 400 x 0.15 = 60; 39 / 520 = 0.075; 10,000,000 / 60 = 166,667 (rounded); 10,400,000 / 39 = 266,667 (rounded; 39 x 266,667 = 10,400,013).

## Common mistakes
- Holding the talk and writing nothing, or writing a file nobody opens again.
- No written expectations, so every result looks as if it was expected (hindsight).
- Looking for a guilty person; people then stop reporting problems.
- Reviewing only failed projects, so the team never learns why good ones worked.
- Stating a cause as fact when it is the team's best guess.
- Ten "lessons" with no owner and no date.
- Managers dominating; junior fieldworkers, who saw the most, staying silent.

## Quality checklist
- Were expectations taken from documents written before the work?
- Does every result have a number and a source?
- Are causes marked INTERPRETATION, with rival explanations listed?
- Are mistakes and near-misses recorded, not only wins?
- Are there at most three changes, each with owner, date and place to build it in?
- Is the case file written in the S8-08 format and filed, with client details masked?
- Is rereading scheduled in the next kickoff?

## Output template
```
PROJECT RETROSPECTIVE - [project code] - [date] - facilitator: ...
Present: [roles, not names in the filed copy]
1 EXPECTED: objective ... | KPIs and targets ... | hypotheses (S2-11) ...
2 HAPPENED (FACT + source): timeline ... | results vs targets ...
3 WHY (INTERPRETATION): gap -> cause -> evidence -> other explanations
4 KEEP: ...   CHANGE: action | owner | date | built into ...
MISTAKES AND NEAR-MISSES: ...
WHAT MAY TRANSFER TO OTHER PROJECTS (hypothesis): ...
CASE FILE WRITTEN (S8-08): yes / no | filed at: ... | reread at: ...
```

## Works with
- S7-01 objective and KPI hierarchy and S2-11 write expectations down before fieldwork: supply question 1. S2-01 research brief: the original plan.
- S7-02 controlled tests and S7-03 before and after tracking with benchmark: supply the results and say how far a causal claim can go.
- S0-04 5 Whys and Ishikawa: digging for causes in question 3. S0-13 facts, assumptions, unknowns register: which assumptions turned out wrong.
- S8-08 own case studies: stores, indexes and reuses the case file this method writes. S7-04 produces one file per project; S8-08 manages the collection.
- S2-10 research ethics and consent: what may be kept in the file. S5-13 pyramid storyline: writing the summary for the client.

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Practical caution: where junior staff are reluctant to contradict seniors in a meeting, collect each person's "what happened" and "what I would change" in writing first, and have the most senior person speak last.
- Polite agreement is not a finding. Ask for one concrete example behind every "everything went well".
- Hold the session in the team's working language, Uzbek or Russian, and keep client quotes in the original language in the file.
- Record Tashkent and regional results separately; a lesson from one may not hold in the other, so mark transfers as hypotheses.
- If the client joins, run the internal session first. Whether clients here welcome frank reviews is something to ask each client, not to assume.
