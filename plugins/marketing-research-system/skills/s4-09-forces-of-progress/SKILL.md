# Forces of progress analysis (S4-09)

Forces of progress analysis is the analysis step for Jobs-to-be-Done switch interviews (S1-07). Each interview with a recent buyer is rebuilt as a timeline from the first thought of changing to actual use, and the evidence is sorted into four forces. Two drive change: the push of the current situation and the pull of the new solution. Two hold it back: anxiety about the new solution and the habit of the present. The model's working claim is that a switch happens when push plus pull outweigh anxiety plus habit. It comes from Bob Moesta and colleagues at The Re-Wired Group (jobstobedone.org credits Moesta and Chris Spiek) and is set out in Moesta's Demand-Side Sales 101. Christensen, Hall, Dillon and Duncan (2016, Harvard Business Review) popularised the wider theory that customers "hire" products to make progress in a given circumstance and that jobs have social and emotional sides as well as functional ones.

Evidence grade: C. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. This is a practitioner tool. The four forces and the timeline stages are a useful way to organise interview evidence, but the claim that the forces sum up and tip a decision has not been tested in peer-reviewed research that this skill could find. Use the output to generate hypotheses and ideas for messaging and product changes; confirm anything important with other data before spending on it.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (events and quotes from the interview) versus INTERPRETATION (which force a statement belongs to, how strong it is, what the job is). Return to the research-orchestrator skill when done.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- There are interviews with people who recently switched to, or away from, a product or supplier.
- The client asks "why do people come to us / leave us / never get round to buying?"
- The purchase involved a real decision over time (supplier change, furniture, housing, equipment).

Do not use it when:
- Respondents have not made the switch and only talk about what they might do; that is intention, not a timeline (see S4-08).
- The purchase is a routine low-thought repeat buy with no story to rebuild.
- The interviews asked about opinions of the product and not about the sequence of events.
- The team needs market size or shares; this method gives neither.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. Switch-interview transcripts or recordings in the original language, with respondent IDs.
2. For each respondent: what they switched from, what to, and the purchase date.
3. Whether the sample includes people who looked and did not buy, or who left; if not, record that as a limit.
4. The client's current beliefs about why customers buy.
5. The decision the analysis will feed (messaging, offer, sales process, product).

## Procedure
1. Check interview quality. For each transcript ask: does it contain a dated sequence of events before the purchase? Working rule: if the first thought and at least one later event cannot be placed in time, mark the interview "timeline incomplete" and use it only as supporting material.
2. Rebuild the timeline for each respondent, using the stages described on jobstobedone.org: (a) First thought: the moment the person first felt something should change. The source notes that people tend to report it later than it really happened, so look for earlier signs in the transcript; (b) Passive looking: noticing options without effort; (c) Event one: what turned "I should look into this" into "I need to act"; (d) Active looking: comparing options and making trade-offs; the source notes that people often cannot say what they want until they compare; (e) Event two: what moved the person from looking to deciding, often something with a deadline; (f) Deciding and buying; (g) Use: what was expected at purchase versus what happened. Put each event on a line with its date or approximate time and a quote.
3. Extract force statements. Go through the transcript and copy each statement that shows a force, with its place on the timeline: (a) Push: what was wrong or frustrating in the old situation; (b) Pull: the better situation the person imagined, which the source stresses is not the feature list; (c) Anxiety: fears about the new option (will it work, will I look foolish, what if it fails); (d) Habit: what was comfortable, familiar or already paid for in the old way. Record the quote as FACT and the force label as INTERPRETATION.
4. Probe vague words. Moesta warns that what people say and what they mean can differ and recommends unpacking vague words (his examples include "cool" and "handy"; in this market think of words like "quality" or "convenient"). If a force statement rests on such a word and the transcript does not unpack it, mark it "unclear" and do not build on it.
5. Rate each force per respondent as weak, medium or strong. Working rule of this skill: strong = tied to a dated event and a cost the person actually bore; medium = repeated but with no event; weak = mentioned once, in answer to a direct question. Do not add the ratings up into a score; the "push plus pull greater than anxiety plus habit" line is a way of reading the story, not a measurement.
6. Find what tipped the balance. For each respondent state: what changed at event one and event two, which restraining force was reduced and by what (a guarantee, a trial, a recommendation, a deadline). For non-buyers, state which restraining force stayed in place.
7. Compare across respondents. Build a table with respondents as rows and the four forces plus the two events as columns. Group respondents whose push and circumstance are alike; each group is a candidate job, written as: "When [circumstance], I want to [progress], so that [outcome]." Note functional, social and emotional sides. Working rule: a job needs at least three respondents with a matching push and circumstance; otherwise report it as a single case.
8. Test the pattern. Look for respondents who had the same push and did not switch, and for forces the team expected and did not find. Send exceptions to S4-07. Compare stated reasons with the events on the timeline (S4-08); where they differ, the timeline takes priority.
9. Turn forces into options, clearly marked as hypotheses: ways to make the push visible, sharpen the pull, lower anxiety, and loosen habit. The source's guidance is that anxiety is lowered by reducing risk (guarantees, proof from others, help with starting), not by adding features, and that habit is loosened by making the change feel small and reversible.
10. Report and return to research-orchestrator. State the number of interviews, how many timelines were complete, and that the results are hypotheses.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. A Tashkent furniture maker interviewed 9 cafe owners who replaced their seating in the last six months.
Input (respondent C2, extract of timeline):
- March: a guest's chair leg broke during a banquet (first thought).
- April to May: saw a new cafe's sofas on Instagram, saved the post (passive looking).
- June: landlord renewed the lease for three years (event one); visited two showrooms, asked for prices (active looking).
- July: a wedding booking for 20 August was confirmed (event two); ordered on 28 July.
Output:
```
C2 FORCES
Push (strong): "Mehmon oldida stul sindi, uyatga qoldim"          FACT / label INTERP
Pull (medium): "Хочу, чтобы зал выглядел как на той странице"     FACT / label INTERP
Anxiety (strong): "Muddatida yetkazib bera olisharmikan?"         FACT / label INTERP
Habit (medium): old chairs repaired by a familiar usta each year  FACT / label INTERP
Tipping: lease renewal removed fear of investing in a rented hall;
  wedding date set the deadline; written delivery date helped     INTERP
ACROSS 9: push "embarrassment in front of guests" in 5; anxiety
  about delivery date in 6; 7 timelines complete                  FACT
Candidate job: "When an important booking is coming and my hall looks worn,
  I want seating that will not fail, so that my name is protected."  INTERP - hypothesis

```

## Common mistakes
- Sorting opinions about the product into the four boxes with no timeline. Listing product features as pull.
- Filling only push and pull and leaving anxiety and habit empty. Accepting the first reason given without rebuilding the events.
- Accepting the first "first thought" reported without looking earlier. Adding force ratings into a numeric score.
- Interviewing only happy buyers and presenting the forces as the whole market. Presenting a job statement as proven when it rests on a few interviews.

## Quality checklist
- Does every respondent have a dated timeline with first thought, two events and purchase?
- Is each force statement a quote with a timeline position?
- Are all four forces addressed, with "none found" stated where empty?
- Were vague words unpacked or marked unclear?
- Is the tipping point explained for each respondent?
- Does each job rest on at least three respondents, or is it flagged as a single case?
- Were non-switchers or exceptions looked for and reported?
- Are options and jobs labelled as hypotheses, with the grade C limit stated?

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Keep force quotes in the original Uzbek or Russian; words for shame, trust or obligation carry meaning that a translation can flatten.
- Polite answer habits can make push statements mild and hide anxiety. Rely on dated events more than on stated feelings, and ask the interviewer whether the respondent softened criticism of the old supplier.
- Many purchases involve several people (family members, a partner, an accountant). Record whose push and whose anxiety each statement is.
- Do not pool Tashkent and regional respondents into one job without checking that the circumstances match. Recruiting switchers through a Telegram bot will miss older and rural buyers; state this as a limit.
- The framework was developed with buyers in the United States. Treat the four forces as an organising hypothesis here and stay open to forces that do not fit the four boxes; record them separately.
