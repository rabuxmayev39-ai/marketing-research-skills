# Exploratory, descriptive and causal research designs (S1-01)

A research design is the plan for getting the information a decision needs; Naresh Malhotra's textbook calls it a "framework or blueprint" for the project. The standard marketing research textbooks (Malhotra; Churchill and Iacobucci; Burns and Bush) sort designs by purpose into three types. Exploratory research discovers: it clarifies a vague problem, finds options and produces hypotheses. Descriptive research measures: how many, how often, who, and which things go together. Causal research proves cause: whether changing one thing changes another. Malhotra groups the last two as "conclusive" research. In Iacobucci and Churchill's six-stage research process, choosing the design is stage two, directly after formulating the problem, because the design decides the data collection method, the sample and the analysis that follow.

Evidence grade: B. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. This classification is a teaching and planning convention found in every major textbook, not a tested theory. Use it with confidence as a sorting tool and a check on over-claiming. It does not tell you which specific method is best, and the three types overlap in practice.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (what the client knows with a source, what a source says about a design) versus INTERPRETATION (your classification of a question and the proposed sequence). When the design statement is agreed, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- A decision and research questions exist (from S0-02 or S2-01) and no method has been chosen yet.
- The client has already named a method ("we need a survey", "let's do focus groups") and you need to check that it matches the purpose.
- A project has several questions and the team must decide what comes first.
- A finished study is being reviewed and you need to check whether its claims go beyond its design.

Do not use it when:
- The decision itself is still unclear. Go back to S0-02 or S0-03 first; a design cannot be chosen for an undefined problem.
- The method is already fixed and correctly matched. Go straight to that method's skill.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. The management decision and the research questions, in one or two sentences each.
2. What is already known and from where (S0-13 register, earlier studies, internal sales data).
3. Whether the client can change something on purpose and watch the result (price, pack, message, shelf, offer). Without this, causal research is not possible.
4. Deadline, budget, who will act on the result, and any hypotheses written down (S2-11).

## Procedure
1. Restate the decision. Ask the user: "What will you do differently depending on the answer?" If nothing would change, stop and say research is not needed for this question.
2. List the research questions separately. One project usually holds several, and each may be a different type. Classify each question, not the whole project.
3. Classify by purpose, using three tests in this order.
   - Discover: "Can we already list the options, reasons or variables that matter?" If no, the question is exploratory. Malhotra describes the information need here as loosely defined, the process as flexible, the sample as small and not representative, and the findings as tentative.
   - Measure: "Do we need a number or a profile: how many, how often, who, where, which go together?" If yes, it is descriptive. Malhotra's rule is that a descriptive design needs the six Ws fixed in advance: who is studied, what is measured, when, where, why, and the way of collecting. If the team cannot fill in all six, the question is still exploratory.
   - Prove cause: "Do we need to know whether changing X will change Y?" If yes, it is causal. Descriptive data can show that two things move together; they cannot show which causes which.
4. Check that the question is ready for its type (decision rules). Descriptive without prior exploration is only safe when the answer options and wording are already known from earlier research or solid experience; otherwise the questionnaire will measure the team's guesses. Causal needs a variable the client can manipulate, other conditions kept as similar as possible, and several observations under each condition so that one unusual week does not explain the result (Penn State course notes). If these cannot be met, say so and offer descriptive evidence with the limit stated.
5. Choose the sequence. The usual order is exploratory, then descriptive, then causal, but the order is not fixed. Malhotra notes that exploration is not always the first step and that descriptive or causal results that are hard to interpret may need exploratory follow-up. Working rule: propose the shortest sequence that reaches the decision, and name what each stage hands to the next (hypotheses, answer options, the variable to test).
6. Match methods to each stage, then open that method's skill.
   - Exploratory: desk research (S1-03), interviews with knowledgeable people, in-depth interviews (S1-04), focus groups (S1-05), observation (S1-06), earlier cases (S1-19), pilot surveys.
   - Descriptive: surveys (S1-08), structured observation and counts, existing company or published data. Decide cross-sectional (one sample at one time) or longitudinal (the same sample measured repeatedly). Only the same respondents over time show individual change; repeated fresh samples show only aggregate change.
   - Causal: experiments and A/B tests (S1-09, S7-02). Regression on existing data is used too, but it is weaker than an experiment for proving cause.
7. Write the claim limit for each stage before fieldwork. Exploratory output is hypotheses, never percentages. Descriptive output is sizes and associations, never "because". Causal output holds for the tested change, place and period only.
8. Plan for error, not only sample size. Malhotra's point: total error is sampling error plus non-sampling error (non-response, wrong or misrecorded answers), and the second is often larger. A bigger sample does not fix a bad question. Pass this to S2-13.
9. Check the plan against time and budget. If the full sequence does not fit, cut scope (fewer questions), not the exploratory stage by default. State what the shorter plan cannot answer.
10. Deliver the design statement (template below), get the user's agreement, and return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. Input: a Tashkent juice brand says "sales in the regions are falling, we want a survey". Decision: whether to change the pack size, the price, or the distribution in regional shops. The client can change price and pack in selected shops.

```
Research question                         | Type        | Why                      | Method
Q1 Why do regional shopkeepers order      | Exploratory | reasons not yet known;   | S1-03 desk, then
   less of our juice?                     |             | six Ws cannot be filled  | S1-04 with 10 owners
Q2 What share of regional shops stock us, | Descriptive | need numbers; options    | S1-08 survey of
   and how do stockers differ?            |             | come from Q1             | shops, cross-section
Q3 Does a 1-litre pack at a lower price   | Causal      | client can change pack   | S1-09 test in
   lift weekly sales per shop?            |             | and price on purpose     | matched shops

Sequence: Q1 (3 weeks) -> Q2 (4 weeks) -> Q3 (6 weeks) = 13 weeks.          INTERP
Hand-overs: Q1 gives answer options for Q2; Q2 shows where to run Q3.       INTERP
Claim limits: Q1 = hypotheses only. Q2 = shares and associations, no
  "because". Q3 = effect of this pack and price, in these shops, this season.
Client asked for a survey first: not ready, because answer options are unknown. INTERP
Deadline is 8 weeks: run Q1 + Q2 (7 weeks); Q3 becomes a later project and
  the pack decision stays a hypothesis until then.                          INTERP
```

## Common mistakes
- Starting with a survey because it is familiar, when the reasons and answer options are still unknown.
- Reading cause from a descriptive result ("buyers who saw the ad buy more, so the ad works").
- Reporting exploratory interviews as percentages of the market.
- Treating the usual order as a law and paying for exploration when good earlier research already supplies the hypotheses.
- Calling a before-and-after comparison an experiment when other things changed at the same time.
- Enlarging the sample to "increase accuracy" while ignoring non-response and question error.

## Quality checklist
- Is there a decision that would change depending on the result?
- Is each research question classified separately, with a reason?
- For every descriptive question, are all six Ws filled in?
- For every causal question, can the client manipulate the variable and observe several times per condition?
- Does each stage name what it hands to the next?
- Is a claim limit written for each stage?
- Does the plan fit the deadline, with what was cut stated openly?

## Output template
```
RESEARCH DESIGN STATEMENT - [project] - [date]
Decision: ... | Who decides: ... | Deadline: ...
Already known [FACT, with source]: ...
QUESTIONS
No | Research question | Type (E/D/C) | Reason for type | Method (ID) | Ready? (yes/no, what is missing)
Descriptive questions - six Ws: who | what | when | where | why | way
Causal questions: variable manipulated | conditions held similar | observations per condition
SEQUENCE
Stage | Weeks | Hands over to next stage
CLAIM LIMITS
Stage | May claim | May not claim
Not covered by this plan and why: ...

```

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Published market data are thin for many categories, so the exploratory stage carries more weight and should rarely be skipped; start with S1-03 and the S8 sources.
- Polite or agreeing answer habits can inflate stated intentions in descriptive surveys. Where the client can run a small real test (two prices, two packs, two Telegram messages), prefer the causal stage to asking "would you buy".
- A Telegram-bot survey is a descriptive tool with a sample that misses older and rural people. Write that into the "who" of the six Ws and into the claim limit.
- Run exploratory work in both Uzbek and Russian and in at least one region as well as Tashkent before fixing answer options; keep respondents' wording in the original language for the questionnaire.
- The textbooks behind this classification use Western market examples. The logic transfers; specific expectations about which method works with which audience are hypotheses here.
