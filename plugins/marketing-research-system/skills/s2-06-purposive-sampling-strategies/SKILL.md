# Purposive sampling strategies (S2-06)

Purposive (Patton says "purposeful") sampling means choosing each case deliberately because of what it can teach, instead of drawing people at random. The standard reference is Michael Quinn Patton, Qualitative Research and Evaluation Methods (Sage; the strategy list goes back to his 1990 edition), which describes the aim as selecting "information-rich cases" for study in depth. Patton names about sixteen strategies; the ones this agency uses most are extreme or deviant, intensity, maximum variation, typical, critical, criterion and snowball cases. In marketing research the method is how a team decides to talk to switchers, lost customers, heavy users and people who refuse the category, because those people explain things an average buyer cannot.

Evidence grade: B. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. The strategy list is a long-standing standard across qualitative research, but it is a set of design choices, not a tested formula: an opened worked example (Benoot et al., 2016) states that its claim of better conceptual quality was not tested against an exhaustive sample. Put weight on the reasons, mechanisms and range the sample reveals. Put no weight on how often something appeared: the sample is unrepresentative by design.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (who was selected, by which rule, how recruited) versus INTERPRETATION (what you expect each group to reveal). When the sampling plan is delivered, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- The study is qualitative (interviews, groups, observation, case studies) and the sample will be small.
- The team needs explanations, variety and surprises: why people leave, why some buy ten times more, why some never buy.
- Some groups are rare or hard to find and must be sought out on purpose.

Do not use it when:
- The client needs shares, sizes or averages for a population. Use probability or quota sampling (S2-02) and a survey (S1-08).
- The plan is "whoever is easiest to reach". That is convenience sampling; an opened summary of Patton's list rates it lowest for credibility and notes it yields information-poor cases.
- Nobody can say what each chosen group is expected to teach. Fix the research questions first (S2-01).

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. The decision, research questions and written expectations (S2-01, S2-11).
2. What is already known about the customer base: CRM or sales records, lists of lapsed buyers, distributor contacts, desk research (S1-03).
3. The method the sample is for (S1-04, S1-05, S1-07, S1-19 and so on) and a rough budget for the number of cases. Recruitment routes available, and a screener question set to confirm each person really fits their cell.

## Procedure
1. Check fit. Ask the user: "Do you need to know how many, or why and in what ways?" If how many, hand over to S2-02 and S2-03. If both, plan a qualitative stage now and a survey later (S1-02).
2. Set the entry criteria (criterion sampling: every case must meet a predefined standard). Write two or three screening conditions tied to behaviour and time, for example "bought the category in the last three months" or "stopped ordering from the client in the last six months".
3. For each research question, ask: "Which kind of person or organisation could teach us most about this?" Then pick strategies from this menu (definitions paraphrased from the opened sources):
   - Extreme or deviant case: unusual instances such as notable successes, failures, dropouts or crises. Marketing use (working mapping): lost customers, rejecters of the category, the single largest account.
   - Intensity: cases that show the phenomenon strongly but are not freak cases. Marketing use: heavy users, loyal repeat buyers.
   - Maximum variation: a deliberately wide spread of cases, to see both the range and what stays common across it. Marketing use: city and region, large and small, new and long-standing buyers.
   - Typical case: what is normal or average. Use it to describe the ordinary experience, and to give context for the unusual cases.
   - Critical case: a case chosen so that what holds there can be argued logically to hold elsewhere ("if it fails even here, it fails everywhere").
   - Homogeneous: a similar group, to describe one subgroup in depth; also suits group interviews. Stratified purposeful: purposeful selection within chosen subgroups so they can be compared.
   - Snowball or chain: participants or well-placed contacts refer further cases. Use it for hard-to-find people.
   - Confirming and disconfirming: cases chosen during fieldwork to check or challenge an emerging pattern. Opportunistic: following an unexpected lead that appears during fieldwork. Theory-based: cases that represent a concept the team wants to examine.
4. Combine strategies and say why. Patton lists combination as a strategy in its own right, and Palinkas et al. (2015, abstract) argue that combining strategies may suit applied studies better than a single one. An opened worked example sequenced them: intensity cases first to build core concepts, then maximum variation along the dimensions those cases revealed, then disconfirming cases to challenge the draft explanation. Working rule: start with intensity and extreme cases, widen with variation, and keep two or three slots open for disconfirming cases.
5. Build the sampling grid: one row per cell, with strategy, definition in behavioural terms, what the cell should teach (INTERPRETATION), planned number, and recruitment route. Planned numbers and the stopping rule come from S2-04 or S2-05, not from this skill.
6. Write the screener. Each cell needs questions about recent behaviour that prove membership; do not accept self-labels such as "I am a loyal customer".
7. Plan recruitment per cell and record the route for every case. If snowballing, set a working rule limiting referrals per starting contact (for example, no more than two) and start from several unconnected contacts, so one social circle does not fill the sample.
8. Keep the plan flexible. Opened guidance (Moser and Korstjens, 2018) says qualitative sampling plans start broad, may change as questions arise in fieldwork, and must justify their choices because those choices follow conceptual needs, not representativeness. After every few interviews ask: "What pattern is forming, and who could prove it wrong?" Fill the open slots with those people. Log each change and the reason.
9. Report the sample honestly: the grid as planned and as achieved, how each person was reached, who was not covered, and a plain statement that counts from this sample are not market shares.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. Input: a juice brand wants to understand why some households stopped buying it. Method: in-depth interviews (S1-04). Budget: 16 interviews.

```
ENTRY CRITERION: makes the household's drinks purchases; aged 20-55.
CELL                 STRATEGY            DEFINITION (screener)                    TASHKENT  REGIONS  n
Heavy users          Intensity           buys the brand weekly, 3+ months            2        2      4
Switchers            Extreme / deviant   bought it regularly, moved to another       2        2      4
                                         brand in the last 6 months
Rejecters            Extreme + snowball  no packaged juice at home in 12 months      2        1      3
Typical buyers       Typical case        buys packaged juice 1-3 times a month       2        1      3
Shop owners          Critical case       small shop that stocks 3+ juice brands      1        1      2
TOTAL                                                                                9        7     16
Reserve: 2 further interviews after interview 8 for disconfirming cases, if the
budget is extended (16 + 2 = 18).
Expected lessons [INTERPRETATION]: switchers show the trigger event; rejecters
show barriers nobody mentions in a survey; shop owners show what happens at shelf.
Recruitment [FACT to log]: heavy users via loyalty list; rejecters via referral,
max 1 referral per starting contact.
```
Arithmetic check: n column 4 + 4 + 3 + 3 + 2 = 16. Tashkent 2 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 1 = 9. Regions 2 + 2 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 7. 9 + 7 = 16. Each row: 2+2 = 4, 2+2 = 4, 2+1 = 3, 2+1 = 3, 1+1 = 2. Reserve: 16 + 2 = 18.

## Common mistakes
- Calling a convenience sample "purposive" because it sounds better. Reporting "5 of 16 switched because of price" as if it described the market.
- Sampling only satisfied current customers, because the client's list contains nobody else.
- Defining cells by demographics alone when the research question is about behaviour.
- Letting one recruiter's friends or one chain of referrals supply most of the sample.
- Fixing the full list of people before fieldwork and leaving no room for disconfirming cases.
- Choosing extreme cases only, then describing them as the normal customer.

## Quality checklist
- Is there a written reason for every cell, linked to a research question?
- Is each cell defined by recent behaviour that a screener can verify?
- Does the sample include at least one group expected to disagree with the client's view (lost customers, rejecters)?
- Is the recruitment route recorded for every case, with referrals limited?
- Were open slots kept and used for disconfirming cases?
- Are planned numbers justified through S2-04 or S2-05?
- Does the report state who was not covered and avoid percentages?

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Recruit and screen in Uzbek or Russian, as the person prefers, and record the language. Keep screener answers and quotes in the original language. Plan Tashkent and regions as separate cells; do not let a Tashkent-only sample stand for the country.
- Recruitment through acquaintances, mahalla contacts or the client's own staff is practical but narrows the sample. Log every referral chain and start from several unconnected contacts.
- Recruiting through a Telegram bot or channel misses older and rural people; add an offline route (shops, bazaars, distributors) if those groups matter.
- People may agree to take part out of politeness and then give agreeable answers. Lost customers and rejecters recruited by the client's staff may soften criticism; use a neutral recruiter.
- Patton's strategies were developed mainly in Western evaluation practice. The logic transfers; which groups turn out to be information-rich here is a hypothesis to test in the first interviews.
