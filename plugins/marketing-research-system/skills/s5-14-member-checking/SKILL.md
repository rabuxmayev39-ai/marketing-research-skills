# Member checking / respondent validation (S5-14)

Member checking means taking data, interpretations or conclusions back to the people they came from and asking whether the account is accurate and fits their experience. Lincoln and Guba (Naturalistic Inquiry, 1985) presented it as a central technique for establishing the credibility of qualitative findings. Birt, Scott, Cavers, Campbell and Walter (2016, Qualitative Health Research) reviewed the variants and proposed a five-step version, Synthesized Member Checking (SMC), in which a plain-language summary of themes is returned to participants and their responses are added to the data.

Evidence grade: B. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. Member checking is a standard, widely cited step in qualitative research, but its value as proof of validity is disputed in the literature (for example by Sandelowski and by Morse, as reported by Birt et al.). Agreement from respondents does not prove a finding is true: people change their views, forget what they said, struggle with abstract summaries, or agree to please the researcher. Treat it as a way to catch errors, add data and find disagreement, not as a stamp of approval.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Keep FACT (what respondents said in the check) separate from INTERPRETATION (what the team concludes from it). When the check is reported, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it

Use it when:
- Findings come from interviews or groups and rest on the team's reading of what people meant.
- The findings will drive a costly decision and a wrong reading would be expensive.
- Respondents are reachable and willing (B2B customers, dealers, staff, loyal buyers).
- The team wants to deepen thin themes with extra data from the same people.

Do not use it, or limit it, when:
- The finding concerns things respondents cannot judge: patterns across the whole sample, behaviour they are unaware of, or the team's strategic conclusions.
- The topic is sensitive and reading the summary could distress or embarrass people.
- Respondents depend on the client (employees, tied suppliers) and cannot safely disagree. Use an anonymous written format or skip.
- Time allows only a token check. A check that cannot change anything should not be claimed in the report.

## What you need before starting (inputs)

1. The stage reached: raw transcripts, first themes, or near-final findings.
2. The list of respondents, contact details, and what they consented to at the interview (was re-contact agreed?).
3. The purpose: check accuracy of records, check that interpretations resonate, or collect additional data. Choose one main purpose.
4. A decision made in advance on what happens if respondents disagree, and whether there is time and budget to re-analyse.
5. Languages of respondents and their comfort with reading text or using messaging apps.

## Procedure

1. Set the purpose and the rule for disagreement. Ask the user: "What are we checking: that we recorded people correctly, that our themes match their experience, or both? If several respondents reject a theme, are we ready to revise it?" Write the answer down before any contact. Deciding in advance how feedback will be used is recommended in the literature.

2. Choose the format. Options described by Birt et al.:
   - Return the transcript: checks accuracy of the record only. Risk: people edit or withdraw statements, and some dislike seeing their speech written down.
   - Member check interview: a second conversation about the transcript or the interpretations. Richer, but costs more, and it is harder to disagree face to face.
   - Member check focus group: efficient, but a lone dissenting voice may not be heard.
   - Return synthesised findings (SMC): a short written summary of themes with space to respond. Tests whether the themes resonate and gathers new data; needs analysis to be advanced first.
   Decision rule: for agency projects default to SMC in writing, because it checks interpretations and lets people disagree without confronting anyone. Use a short call for respondents who do not read comfortably. Use transcript return only when exact wording will be published.

3. Prepare the summary (SMC step 1). One or two pages. For each theme: a plain-language statement (no jargon, no theme codes), one or two anonymised quotes in the original language, and open space for comments. Include themes the team is unsure about. Phrase statements so that disagreement is easy: neutral wording, not "we found that everyone...".

4. Write the questions. For each theme ask the three things used in SMC: does this match your experience; do you want to change anything; do you want to add anything. Add one deliberately open prompt at the end: "What did we miss or get wrong?" Avoid yes/no-only forms; ask for an example when someone agrees.

5. Check who should be contacted (SMC step 2). Remove anyone who did not agree to re-contact, whose situation has changed in a way that makes contact inappropriate, or whose contact details are outdated. Check anonymity: no respondent should be recognisable to another or to the client from the quotes. Record who was excluded and why.

6. Send (SMC step 3). Send the summary with a short cover note: why they are receiving it, that disagreement is useful, that taking part is voluntary, how long it takes, how to reply (written, voice message, call), and the deadline. Send one reminder. After that, accept non-response.

7. Gather and log responses (SMC step 4). Record: how many were sent, how many replied, and how much each reply contained (none, short, detailed). For each theme count agree / partly / disagree. Add all comments to the dataset as new data, labelled as member-check data with respondent ID. Compare responders with non-responders on basic profile (segment, role) and note any group that stayed silent.

8. Read agreement critically. Flag any reply that agrees with everything and adds nothing as "low-information agreement"; do not count it as confirmation of the same strength as agreement with an example. Agreement to please the researcher is a known weakness of the method.

9. Integrate (SMC step 5). Code the new comments against existing themes. For each theme decide:
   - Confirmed with new examples: keep; add the examples.
   - Partly disputed: narrow the theme or split it by segment.
   - Disputed by several respondents with reasons: return to the original data and re-analyse; treat as disconfirming cases (S4-07).
   - Disputed by one respondent: keep the theme if the data support it, and report the dissent.
   Where the respondent's view and the team's view differ and both are defensible, report both rather than forcing agreement. Consensus is not always the aim.

10. Report. In the method section state: format used, who was contacted and excluded, response numbers, what changed as a result, and what disagreement remains. If member checking was not done, say so and why. Then return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example

INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. A juice brand interviewed 14 small-shop owners in Tashkent about how they choose which juices to stock.

Input: three draft themes. T1 "Owners stock what the distributor's agent pushes." T2 "Owners fear unsold stock more than they value margin." T3 "Shelf fridges from brands decide placement."

Process: one-page summary in Uzbek and Russian sent by Telegram to 12 owners (2 had not agreed to re-contact); voice replies accepted; one reminder.

Output:

```
Sent 12 | replied 8 (5 detailed, 2 short, 1 "hammasi to'g'ri" only)
Non-responders: 3 of 4 are from one district - note as limit
T1 agree 3 | partly 4 | disagree 1                               FACT
   "Agent aytgani uchun emas, qaytarib olgani uchun olaman" (S6)  FACT
   -> revised: owners follow agents who take back unsold stock    INTERP
T2 agree 7 | partly 1 | disagree 0, five gave new examples       FACT
   -> kept; strengthened                                          INTERP
T3 agree 4 | partly 1 | disagree 3: "Холодильник есть у всех,
   это уже не решает" (S2)                                        FACT
   -> returned to transcripts; theme narrowed to shops without
      own fridge; dissent reported                                INTERP
Low-information agreement: 1 reply, not counted as confirmation
```

## Common mistakes

- Doing it as a formality: sending the final report and counting silence as agreement.
- Reporting "respondents confirmed the findings" with no numbers and no account of what changed.
- Sending abstract, jargon-heavy themes that respondents cannot relate to their own experience.
- Asking leading questions ("You agree that..., right?").
- Having the original interviewer ask face to face, which makes disagreement harder.
- Treating respondent agreement as proof and dropping other credibility checks.
- Letting one respondent's objection overturn a theme, or ignoring it completely.
- Breaking anonymity through recognisable quotes in the summary.
- No plan for disagreement, so disputed themes are quietly kept.

## Quality checklist

- Was the purpose and the rule for handling disagreement written down before contact?
- Was the format chosen to make disagreement easy for these respondents?
- Was the summary in plain language, in the respondent's language, with quotes anonymised?
- Were the questions open, including a prompt for what was missed or wrong?
- Are sent / replied / excluded numbers and the depth of replies recorded?
- Was low-information agreement separated from agreement with examples?
- Were disputed themes re-checked against the original data and the dissent reported?
- Does the report say what changed because of the check?

## Works with

- S2-08 trustworthiness criteria: member checking is one credibility technique among several; never the only one.
- S4-07 negative case analysis: disputed themes become negative cases to examine.
- S5-01 affinity diagram and S5-02 insight ladder: produce the themes to be checked; check before S5-03 insight tests are finalised.
- S5-09 needs-based segments: respondents can react to the description of "their" segment.
- S5-13 pyramid storyline: run the check before the storyline is fixed, and state the result in the method note.
- S5-11 and S5-12: respondent corrections update opportunities, pains and gains.

## Notes for Uzbekistan

- Plan for polite agreement. Respondents may say "hammasi to'g'ri" / "всё правильно" out of courtesy to the researcher. Practical counters: use a written or voice-message format instead of a face-to-face meeting; let someone other than the original interviewer make contact; include one or two themes you are unsure of and say openly that some statements may be wrong; ask "give one example from your shop" after any agreement; ask what a colleague or neighbour might disagree with.
- Send the summary in the language the person used in the interview (Uzbek or Russian; check script preference for Uzbek). Keep quotes in the original language.
- Many respondents will answer faster by messaging app or phone than on paper; accept voice replies and transcribe them.
- If the client is an owner-founder who knows the respondents personally, tell respondents their replies go to the agency and are reported without names.
- These are practical precautions for the team, not claims about how any group of consumers behaves.
