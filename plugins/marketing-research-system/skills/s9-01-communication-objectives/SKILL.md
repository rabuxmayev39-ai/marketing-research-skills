# Communication objectives and the role of communication (S9-01)

Communication objectives state what advertising and other communication must change in people's heads and actions so that the behaviour named in the marketing strategy changes. The "role of communication" is one sentence that says what communication, as distinct from price, product or distribution, is expected to contribute. The practice is standard in agency planning and is documented in guides and essays of the Institute of Practitioners in Advertising (IPA). Its best-known evidence base is Les Binet and Peter Field's Marketing in the Era of Accountability (2007), an analysis of 880 IPA Effectiveness Awards case studies, which argued that marketing is unlikely to work, or to be measurable, when objectives are not spelled out.

Evidence grade: B. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. The objective chain is a professional standard taught by the IPA, not an experimentally tested procedure. The supporting analysis has known limits: it uses award entries, which are self-reported and are not a representative sample of all campaigns, and it shows that campaigns with clear, hard objectives did better, not that writing objectives caused the result. The sources also pull in two directions. Binet and Field found that "soft" intermediate goals such as awareness and image were the most common objectives yet did not reliably go with business results; the IPA and BetterBriefs guide (strategy section by Mark Ritson) says sales and profit are final outcomes and a brief needs the few steps on the buyer's path that will produce them. This skill resolves the tension by always writing the whole chain and never one level alone. That resolution is this skill's working rule.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (baselines, measured results) versus INTERPRETATION (the assumed links between levels). Do not accept "grow sales" as a communication objective. When the objectives are written and checked, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- A marketing strategy is agreed and a communication plan (S9-07) or creative brief (S6-18) is about to be written.
- A client asks for "awareness" or "more sales" and nobody can say what the advertising itself should change.
- A finished campaign must be judged and no objectives were written beforehand.

Do not use it when:
- No strategy exists: target, positioning and growth logic are undecided. Go to S6-08 and S6-03 first.
- The barrier to purchase is price, stock, quality or sales staff. Communication cannot fix these; send the problem back to the strategy (S6-14).
- The task is the full KPI system of the business. Use S7-01; this skill fills only its communication rows.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. The commercial objective with a number and a date, and the behaviour the strategy wants to change (who should do what more often).
2. The target audience as defined in the strategy, and its size (S4-23).
3. Research on why the behaviour does not happen now: S5-07, S4-19, S0-06 or interview findings.
4. Baselines for anything that will be measured (S1-22, client records), with their sources.
5. The planned budget range, because objectives and budget limit each other (S9-05).

## Procedure
1. Write the commercial objective exactly as the strategy states it (for example sales, enrolments, share) with number, date and source. Mark it "not a communication objective". The BetterBriefs guide calls this level the commercial objective and notes it is the one finance cares about.
2. Write the behavioural objective: which people must do what, and how many more of them. Binet and Field's analysis found campaigns aimed at winning more buyers (penetration) were less common but more effective than loyalty campaigns, so by default ask first "how many new or lapsed buyers?" (S0-05 shows which term is the gap).
3. Do the arithmetic from behaviour to the commercial objective. If the behavioural number, multiplied through, does not give the commercial number, the chain is wishful thinking; the guide warns against exactly this. Fix it before going on.
4. Name what blocks the behaviour, using evidence. Typical blocks: people do not think of the brand in the buying situation, do not know it exists, do not believe a claim, feel a risk, or do not know how to take the next step. Ask the user: "What is the evidence that this is the block?" If there is none, mark it as an assumption (S0-13).
5. Sort the blocks. Working rule: a block is a communication task only if telling, showing or reminding people could remove it. Price, availability and product blocks go back to the strategy owner in writing.
6. Write the role of communication in one sentence: "Communication will get [who] to [behaviour] by [changing what they think or feel]." If the sentence still makes sense with any brand in the category, tighten it.
7. Write two to four communication objectives under three headings. THINK: what people should know or link to the brand (for example a buying situation, S9-02). FEEL: the attitude or emotion that should shift. DO: the first observable action that communication can trigger itself (search, visit, call, trial). The guide advises only a handful of objectives because each added one lowers the chance that any is met.
8. Give each objective a measure, a baseline, a target, a date and a data source. Binet's advice for effectiveness papers is that measures should fit how this campaign is meant to work; standard ones such as awareness are not always right.
9. Separate layers. If the plan has a long-term brand layer and a short-term activation layer (S6-05), give each its own objectives and time frame. The guide's rule is one main objective per brief.
10. Plan the reading of results. Binet and Field recommend several indicators together: no single number predicts performance, while four or five moving the same way usually signal a real effect. Add a comparison (S7-03, S7-02) so that other causes such as season, price or distribution can be ruled out.
11. Run the "copied objective" test. Working rule: if the objective could be reached by a price cut alone, it is a business goal, not a communication objective. Rewrite it.
12. Hand the chain to S9-02 (message), S9-03 (channels), S9-05 (budget) and S6-18 (brief). Return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. A language school in Tashkent sells adult evening English courses. Strategy: grow by winning new learners who need English for work.

```
COMMERCIAL OBJECTIVE (not a communication objective)          FACT (client records)
  Enrolments per year: 1,200 -> 1,500 by 31 December
BEHAVIOURAL OBJECTIVE
  Free trial lessons booked: 3,000 -> 3,750 (conversion to enrolment stays 40%)
BLOCK (survey of 400 adults planning to study, S1-21)         FACT (invented)
  Only 22% link the school to "I need English for a job interview"
  Price and location were not named as reasons -> block is a communication task
ROLE OF COMMUNICATION                                         INTERPRETATION
  Get working adults who face a job interview in English to book a trial
  lesson, by making this school the one they think of in that situation.
COMMUNICATION OBJECTIVES
  THINK: link to "English for a job interview" 22% -> 30% (survey, n=400, Dec)
  FEEL:  "I would not be embarrassed in a group of adult beginners"
         agree 35% -> 45% (same survey)
  DO:    trial bookings 3,000 -> 3,750 (+25%), tracked monthly in CRM
READING: THINK, FEEL, DO and enrolments read together, against last year.
```
Arithmetic check: 1,500 - 1,200 = 300 extra enrolments; 300 / 0.40 = 750 extra trials; 3,000 + 750 = 3,750; 3,750 x 0.40 = 1,500; 750 / 3,000 = 25%; 0.22 x 400 = 88 people and 0.30 x 400 = 120 people; the 8-point rise is above the sampling noise of about 6 points for two samples of 400 (S2-03), so it can be detected; the 10-point FEEL target also is.

## Common mistakes
- Copying the business goal ("grow sales 20%") into the communication plan as its objective.
- Writing only "raise awareness" with no link to behaviour. Binet and Field found awareness the most common objective and a poor guide to business results.
- A long list of objectives with no priority.
- No baseline, so nothing can be shown afterwards.
- Giving communication a task that belongs to price, distribution or product.
- Measuring only people who recall the advertising. Binet warns that customers notice a brand's ads more than non-customers do, which can fake an effect.
- Setting targets the budget cannot buy (check with S9-05 and S9-03).

## Quality checklist
- Is the commercial objective written separately and marked as not a communication objective?
- Does the behavioural number multiply through to the commercial number?
- Is each block backed by evidence or marked as an assumption?
- Is there a one-sentence role of communication that names who, behaviour and change?
- Are there no more than four communication objectives, in priority order?
- Does each have measure, baseline, target, date and source?
- Do brand and activation layers have separate objectives?
- Is there a plan to rule out other causes when results are read?

## Output template
```
COMMUNICATION OBJECTIVES - [brand] - [date]
Commercial objective (FACT, from strategy): ... by ... | source ...
Behavioural objective: [who] do [what] | from ... to ... | arithmetic to commercial: ...
Blocks: 1 ... (evidence / assumption) | communication task? yes / no -> sent to ...
Role of communication: Communication will get ... to ... by ...
Objectives (priority order):
  THINK: ... | measure ... | baseline ... | target ... | date ... | source ...
  FEEL:  ... | measure ... | baseline ... | target ... | date ... | source ...
  DO:    ... | measure ... | baseline ... | target ... | date ... | source ...
Layer: brand / activation | time frame ...
Reading plan: indicators read together ... | comparison ... | other causes to rule out ...
Open assumptions: ...
```

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Clients often state the goal only as sales. Practical caution: do not argue with it; write it at the top and build the chain under it together with the owner.
- Baselines are often missing. Plan a small baseline survey before the campaign and say plainly that Telegram-bot samples miss older and rural people (S2-13).
- Polite or agreeing answer habits can inflate THINK and FEEL scores. Prefer brand-linking questions and behaviour counts over agree-disagree items (S3-07, S3-01).
- Write objectives in the language the team works in, Uzbek or Russian, and keep survey wording identical between waves (S3-03).
- The findings behind this skill come mostly from UK campaigns. Treat any expected size of effect as a hypothesis for Tashkent and, even more, for the regions.
