# Strategy kernel: diagnosis, guiding policy, coherent actions (S0-01)

The strategy kernel is a three-part skeleton for any strategy. The diagnosis explains what kind of challenge the organisation faces and which few aspects of the situation are critical. The guiding policy is the overall approach chosen to deal with the obstacles named in the diagnosis. The coherent actions are steps that are coordinated with each other to carry out that policy. It was set out by Richard Rumelt, a strategy professor at UCLA Anderson School of Management, in the book Good Strategy / Bad Strategy (2011) and in a McKinsey Quarterly article of the same year. Rumelt's central claim is that a strategy is an answer to an obstacle; a list of ambitions, however precise, is not one. In this agency the kernel is used at intake to check whether the client has brought a diagnosis or only a goal.

Evidence grade: B. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. The kernel is a widely taught professional framework built from its author's cases and consulting experience. No controlled study comparing organisations that use it with ones that do not was found among the sources opened for this skill. Its known limits: it gives a structure and not answers, so the diagnosis is only as good as the evidence put into it; and a neat diagnosis written in a meeting can feel true without being tested. Published criticism (Harney, 2012, Irish Journal of Management): the good versus bad contrast may be too simple; the account looks at strategy from top management's side only; and bad strategy may persist because it suits powerful people, which a framework does not fix. Use the kernel to force the question "what is the obstacle?"; do not present its output as proven.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (records, counts, observed events) versus INTERPRETATION (the diagnosis, the policy and the expected effect). At intake treat every diagnosis as a hypothesis to be checked by research. When the kernel is drafted, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- A new brief arrives and the stated problem is a target ("grow 30%"), a tactic ("we need a new campaign") or a slogan.
- The client has a plan with many priorities and nobody can say which obstacle it is meant to overcome.
- Research has finished and the team must restate what the main challenge turned out to be.

Do not use it when:
- The client cannot say what decision they face. Start with S0-02 and S0-12.
- The task is to compare full strategic options in detail. Use S6-08; the kernel comes before it.
- The task is a root-cause search for one operational fault. Use S0-04.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. The client's problem in their own words, written down without editing.
2. Any existing plan, goal list or "strategy" document.
3. Basic records: sales by period, channel and region; leads and conversions; prices; main competitors.
4. A short list of what is known, assumed and unknown (S0-13), and access to the person who decides.

## Procedure
1. Record the stated problem word for word. Do not improve it yet.
2. Classify each sentence of it as one of four things. Working rule: GOAL (a number or state the client wants), SYMPTOM (something that is going wrong, for example falling sales), ACTION (something they already want to do) or DIAGNOSIS (an explanation of why the goal is hard to reach). Most briefs contain the first three and no diagnosis.
3. Check for Rumelt's four signs of bad strategy: the challenge is never named; goals are presented as if they were the strategy; the objectives are either a long list of everyone's wishes or a restatement of the desired result that is as hard as the original problem; and fluff, meaning obvious points dressed in impressive words. Note which signs appear. Do not criticise the client; use the signs to choose questions.
4. Ask the challenge questions. From Rumelt's 2022 interview: what about this goal is difficult, given that an easy goal would already have been reached? Then, as working rules: what has already been tried and what happened; what would have to change for the number to move; who or what is in the way.
5. Draft the diagnosis in one to three sentences (working rule on length). It must explain the nature of the challenge and reduce a complicated situation to the few aspects that matter most, which is Rumelt's definition. Attach the evidence for each part and mark it FACT or assumption.
6. Write at least one rival diagnosis that would explain the same facts (working rule). State what evidence would separate the two. This is what the research must find out.
7. Apply the crux test from Rumelt's interview: is the named challenge both important and something this organisation can actually solve now? If it cannot be solved now, set it aside and look for the hardest part that can be.
8. Draft a provisional guiding policy: the general approach to the obstacle, not a list of tasks. Working rule: a guiding policy should make clear what the organisation will not do or will postpone; Rumelt names the unwillingness to give up some goals as a root of bad strategy.
9. Draft provisional coherent actions. Working rule: list three to six, then check each pair for conflict (for example, buying more leads while the sales team cannot answer the ones it has). Remove or reorder actions that work against each other.
10. Mark the whole kernel "provisional, before research". List the evidence gaps and pass them on: S0-02 to turn them into a research problem, S0-03 to turn the rival diagnoses into hypotheses.
11. Return to research-orchestrator with the kernel and the list of gaps.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. Input: a furniture maker in Tashkent selling sofas through two showrooms and Instagram says: "Our strategy is to grow orders by 30% next year. We need a bigger advertising campaign."

```
CLASSIFICATION
"grow orders by 30%"            GOAL (180 orders a month -> 234)
"bigger advertising campaign"   ACTION
Diagnosis in the brief:         none -> sign 2, goals presented as strategy
FACTS from the client's records (invented)
Instagram inquiries: 3,000 a month; orders from them: 180 (6%)
Answered within 15 minutes: 750 inquiries -> 90 orders (12%)
Answered later (average wait 5 hours): 2,250 inquiries -> 90 orders (4%)
DIAGNOSIS (INTERPRETATION, provisional)
Demand is not the obstacle. Three quarters of inquiries wait hours for an
answer and those convert at a third of the rate of fast answers.
RIVAL DIAGNOSIS
Fast-answered inquiries arrive in working hours and come from more serious
buyers; speed is not the cause. Test: answer a random half of evening
inquiries within 15 minutes for four weeks (S7-02).
GUIDING POLICY (provisional)
Fix the handling of inquiries before buying more of them. No increase in the
advertising budget until most inquiries are answered within 15 minutes.
COHERENT ACTIONS (provisional)
1 evening and weekend shift for inquiries; 2 ready price and delivery answers;
3 pause new campaigns for two months; 4 weekly count of answer time and orders.
UPPER-BOUND ESTIMATE if speed is the cause and 75% are answered fast:
2,250 x 12% = 270; 750 x 4% = 30; total 300 orders (goal: 234)
```
Arithmetic check: 180 / 3,000 = 0.06; 90 / 750 = 0.12; 90 / 2,250 = 0.04; 750 + 2,250 = 3,000; 90 + 90 = 180; 4 / 12 = one third; 180 x 1.3 = 234; 3,000 x 0.75 = 2,250; 2,250 x 0.12 = 270; 750 x 0.04 = 30; 270 + 30 = 300.

## Common mistakes
- Accepting a target or a tactic as the problem and going straight to methods.
- Writing a diagnosis that is a symptom in other words ("sales are low because demand is weak").
- One diagnosis only, with no rival and no test.
- A guiding policy that is a goal again ("become the leader"), or a provisional kernel treated as final.
- Actions that pull in different directions, or a list so long that nothing is a priority.
- Filling in three boxes as a template; Rumelt names template-style planning as a source of bad strategy.

## Quality checklist
- Is the client's wording recorded and classified, and does the diagnosis explain why the goal is hard?
- Is each part of the diagnosis marked FACT or assumption, with its source?
- Is there at least one rival diagnosis and a way to tell them apart?
- Does the challenge pass the crux test (important and solvable now)?
- Does the guiding policy say what will not be done?
- Do the actions support each other, with no pair in conflict?
- Is the kernel marked provisional, with evidence gaps handed on?

## Output template
```
STRATEGY KERNEL - [client / unit] - [date] - status: provisional / after research
Stated problem (verbatim): ...
Classification: goal ... | symptom ... | action ... | diagnosis present? yes / no
Signs of bad strategy noted: ...
DIAGNOSIS (INTERPRETATION): ...
  Evidence: [FACT] ... [ASSUMPTION] ...
RIVAL DIAGNOSIS: ... | evidence that would separate them: ...
Crux test: important? ... | solvable now? ...
GUIDING POLICY: ... | will not do / postponed: ...
COHERENT ACTIONS: 1 ... 2 ... 3 ... | conflicts checked: yes / no
Evidence gaps -> S0-02 / S0-03: ...
```

## Works with
- S0-12 client and stakeholder interviews, S0-13 facts, assumptions, unknowns register: inputs to the diagnosis.
- S0-02 decision to research problem and S0-03 issue tree: turn the evidence gaps and rival diagnoses into research questions and hypotheses.
- S0-04 5 Whys and Ishikawa, S0-05 growth decomposition, S0-06 funnel diagnosis, S0-07 3C and 5C analysis: ways to find candidate diagnoses.
- S2-11 write expectations down before fieldwork: lock the provisional diagnosis before data arrive.
- S6-08 strategy choice cascade: develops the guiding policy into full choices after research. S5-05 what would have to be true: tests the policy.
- S7-02 controlled tests: checking a diagnosis in the field. S7-01 objective and KPI hierarchy: measures for the actions.

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- In owner-led firms the brief often arrives as the owner's target. Practical caution: ask the challenge questions respectfully and in private first; a public "your goal is not a strategy" may close the conversation.
- Polite agreement with a diagnosis is not confirmation. Ask for a record or a recent example behind each agreed point.
- Hold the conversation in the language the decision-maker thinks in, Uzbek or Russian, and keep their original wording in that language in the output.
- Records may be partial (sales in notebooks, inquiries in personal Telegram or Instagram accounts). Treat this as a hypothesis to check at intake and plan time to rebuild basic counts.
- Rumelt's cases are large Western companies and military history. Whether the same obstacles apply to a local firm is an assumption, not a finding.
