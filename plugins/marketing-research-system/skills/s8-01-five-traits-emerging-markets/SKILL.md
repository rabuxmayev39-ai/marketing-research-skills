# Five traits of emerging markets (S8-01)

This method is a checklist for adapting marketing frameworks that were built in rich industrial economies. It comes from Jagdish Sheth's 2011 article in the Journal of Marketing. Sheth argues that emerging markets differ from the traditional industrialised capitalist economy on five traits: market heterogeneity (large differences between buyers and places inside one country), sociopolitical governance (strong influence of government, religion, community and other non-market institutions on how markets work), unbranded competition (a large part of consumption is unbranded, home-made, second-hand or imitation), chronic shortage of resources (unreliable power, materials, skills or money), and inadequate infrastructure (weak roads, logistics, payment and communication systems). He concludes that core ideas such as segmentation, market orientation and differential advantage need rethinking: for example towards aggregating and standardising for scale, and towards developing a market instead of only responding to it. In this skill none of the five traits is assumed to be present. Each is a question to answer with current local evidence before a framework is used.

Evidence grade: A. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. The grade reflects that the source is a peer-reviewed article in a leading journal and is widely built on by later peer-reviewed work. Read the grade with care: the article is conceptual. It reasons from examples; it does not test the five traits with data, and Uzbekistan is not studied in it. Published work also warns against treating emerging markets as one group: Pels and Kidd (2012) stress the large variance between and inside countries and note that lists of characteristics in this literature are partial and overlapping. So the method is strong as a set of questions and weak as a set of facts. Any statement that a trait applies to a given category in Uzbekistan is a hypothesis until checked.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (sourced, dated local evidence) versus INTERPRETATION (what the trait would mean for the framework). Never write "in Uzbekistan, X" from this article alone. When the adaptation table is complete, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- The team is about to apply a method from the index that was developed on Western data (segmentation, positioning, brand growth rules, pricing, media planning).
- A client or a textbook says "this is how it works" and nobody has asked whether the conditions behind the rule hold here.
- A plan built for Tashkent is being extended to the regions, or the reverse.

Do not use it when:
- The team needs facts about the market. This checklist asks questions; S8-04 to S8-07, desk research (S1-03) and fieldwork answer them.
- The aim is to describe "the Uzbek consumer". The method gives no such description.
- The question is about missing market intermediaries specifically (S8-02) or about whether survey measures transfer (S8-03).

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. The framework to be adapted and the decision it serves (for example S6-01 for a targeting decision).
2. The assumptions the framework makes. Working rule: write them as "this method assumes that ...".
3. Current sources: S8-04 (digital use), S8-05 (consumer sentiment), S8-06 (household survey), S8-07 (national statistics), each with its date.
4. The client's own experience: sales by region and channel, delivery problems, payment terms, competitors including informal ones.
5. Any matching past projects from S8-08.

## Procedure
1. Name the framework and list its hidden assumptions. Typical ones: buyers can be reached by the same media; products are available wherever demand exists; competitors are other brands; buyers can pay at once or get credit; rules are applied the same way everywhere.
2. Ask the heterogeneity question. How much do buyers in this category differ by income, city versus village, region, language and access to shops? What evidence shows it (S8-06, S8-07, client sales by region)? If differences are large, decide whether the plan needs separate treatment by place, or, following Sheth's argument, one simple, affordable, standard offer that can reach scale across differences.
3. Ask the sociopolitical governance question. Which non-market actors shape this category: regulators, state-owned firms, local administrations, community and religious norms, family decision-makers? What rules or customs limit pricing, advertising, distribution or timing? Check with S0-09 and the client's experience. Record the source and date of every rule.
4. Ask the unbranded competition question. What do people use instead of any brand: home-made, loose or unpacked goods, bazaar supply, second-hand, imitation, doing without? What share of category use might this be? Check by observation (S1-06), interviews (S1-04) and store checks. If the share is material, the task may be converting non-users of brands, which is market development, and not taking share from rival brands.
5. Ask the resource shortage question. Which resources are unreliable for the client, its channel and its buyers: electricity, raw materials, packaging, skilled staff, cash and credit, time? What do they do when the resource fails (improvised substitutes are evidence)? Check with the client's operations data and S8-05.
6. Ask the infrastructure question. Can the product physically and digitally reach the buyer: roads, cold storage, shop density, payment terminals, internet quality, delivery services? Check with S8-04, S8-07 and a small outlet audit. S8-02 goes deeper on missing intermediaries.
7. Rate each trait for this category and place. Working rule: Present (two independent, dated sources agree), Partly, Not found, Unknown. "Unknown" stays a research question; do not round it up to Present.
8. Write the adaptation. For each trait rated Present or Partly, state which assumption of the framework fails and what changes: the segment base, the competitor set, the channel, the price or pack size, the message, the KPI. Mark each change INTERPRETATION and attach a test (S7-02) where money depends on it.
9. Return the adaptation table and the list of Unknowns to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. A juice brand wants to apply S6-01 (segmentation, targeting, positioning) built on a lifestyle segmentation from a European sister company. The agency audits 40 outlets in Tashkent and 40 in one regional town.

```
TRAIT / QUESTION            EVIDENCE (invented)                    RATING
Heterogeneity               client sales: 68% Tashkent, 32% rest   Partly
Unbranded competition       outlets selling loose or home-made
                            drinks: Tashkent 6/40, town 22/40      Present (town)
Infrastructure              outlets with a working fridge:
                            Tashkent 36/40, town 18/40             Present (town)
Resource shortage           client: 3 packaging stock-outs in 12
                            months                                 Partly
Sociopolitical governance   labelling rules: not yet checked       Unknown
ADAPTATION (INTERPRETATION)
- Lifestyle segments assume branded rivals; in the town the main rival
  may be home-made drinks -> add "non-users of packaged juice" as a group.
- Chilled 1-litre pack assumes fridges -> test shelf-stable 0.2-litre pack.
- Unknown -> ask a lawyer and read the current rule before any claim on the label.
```
Arithmetic check: 6 / 40 = 15%; 22 / 40 = 55%; (6 + 22) / 80 = 28 / 80 = 35%; 36 / 40 = 90%; 18 / 40 = 45%; 68% + 32% = 100%.

## Common mistakes
- Treating the five traits as facts about Uzbekistan instead of questions.
- Rating the whole country once; a trait can be present in one region or category and absent in another.
- Using the checklist to reject Western frameworks wholesale. The aim is to find which assumption fails, not to discard the method.
- Counting only branded competitors and missing home production and bazaar supply.
- Using undated or old sources; conditions in fast-changing markets move quickly.
- Filling "Unknown" cells with the team's opinion.
- Stopping at the rating and not saying what changes in the plan.

## Quality checklist
- Is the framework being adapted named, with its assumptions written out?
- Was each of the five traits asked as a question for this category and place?
- Does every rating cite a dated source or the client's own records?
- Are Tashkent and the regions rated separately where evidence allows?
- Are Unknowns listed as research questions and not guessed?
- Does each Present or Partly rating lead to a stated change and a test?
- Is every claim about local buyers marked as hypothesis or FACT with source?

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- This skill deliberately states no fact about Uzbekistan. Sheth's article does not study the country; every trait is a question for current sources and the client.
- Practical caution: answers may differ between Tashkent, regional cities and villages, so ask each question by place.
- Telegram-bot and online samples miss older and rural people, who may be the very buyers for whom a trait matters; add observation or face-to-face checks.
- Ask clients for records (sales by region, stock-outs, returns), not general impressions; polite or agreeing answers hide problems.
- Keep evidence and quotes in the original language, Uzbek or Russian, with the date collected.
