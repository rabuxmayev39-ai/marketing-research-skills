# 3C and 5C situation analysis (S0-07)

The 3C model, also called the strategic triangle, says that a strategy has to be worked out from three parties at once: the company (Ohmae's word is "corporation"), its customers and its competitors. It was set out by Kenichi Ohmae, a Japanese strategy consultant, in The Mind of the Strategist (1982) and in a journal article of the same year, where he advises studying each corner separately and then joining the three. The 5C version, common in marketing courses, adds collaborators (distributors, suppliers, partners, anyone who helps the offer reach the customer) and context (the outside conditions, usually scanned with PESTEL). No original author for the 5C version could be verified for this skill, so it is treated as a teaching convention.

Evidence grade: C. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. Ohmae built the triangle from his consulting work and illustrated it with company cases; no study was found, among the sources opened, that tests whether teams who use 3C or 5C reach better strategies. Its real limit is that it is a checklist: it tells you where to look and produces description, not a diagnosis. A filled-in 5C says nothing about which fact matters most or what to do. The free guides opened for this skill list the headings and questions but offer no evaluation of the tool, which is itself a sign of thin evidence. Use it to sort and to find gaps, then pass the material to a method that diagnoses (S0-01, S0-03). Put no weight on a 5C as proof of anything.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (sourced, dated) versus INTERPRETATION (what the facts might mean), and mark assumptions and unknowns. Do not end with the table; end with gaps and tensions. When the analysis is written, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- A project starts and desk research (S1-03) needs a frame so no area is forgotten.
- Material from the client arrives in a pile: price lists, sales files, competitor links, opinions.
- A new team member or the client needs a one-page picture of the situation.

Do not use it when:
- The team needs to know why something is happening. That is diagnosis: S0-01, S0-03, S0-04.
- A choice must be made between options. Use S5-05 or S6-08.
- The headings would be filled from opinion only. Then run S0-12 and S1-03 first.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. The decision the client faces, in one sentence (S0-02). It sets what counts as relevant.
2. Whatever exists: sales and CRM records, price lists, past research, competitor sites and ads, client interviews (S0-12).
3. A named unit of analysis: one brand or business line in one market, not the whole group.
4. A source and date for every item, or a note that it is an opinion.

## Procedure
1. Choose 3C or 5C. Working rule: use 3C for a quick first pass on a small business; use 5C when the offer depends on intermediaries (dealers, shops, platforms, agents) or on regulation, which is most projects.
2. Start with customers. Ohmae treats the customer corner as the foundation. Record who buys, for what use, how much, and which groups the company can serve profitably. He recommends dividing customers by what they use the product for and by which of them the company can actually cover, and looking for a fresh way to divide the market when all competitors divide it the same way. Record facts here; do not build the segmentation yet (S5-09).
3. Company. Record what the company sells, to whom, at what price and cost, and where it is strong in the functions that matter in this category. Ohmae's points to check: a firm need not lead in every function, only in a key one; what it makes itself and what it buys in; and where its costs differ from rivals'.
4. Competitors. List the brands customers actually choose between, including indirect alternatives and doing nothing. For each, record offer, price, channels and visible communication. Ohmae's lenses: image, differences in cost and profit structure, and how people, money and assets are used. Working rule: three to five competitors, chosen from customer evidence and not from the client's list alone.
5. Collaborators (5C). List distributors, retailers, suppliers, partners and influencers on whom the offer depends, and what each gains from working with the company.
6. Context (5C). Note the outside conditions that bear on the decision. For anything more than a short list, run S0-09 and paste in only the relevant factors, with sources.
7. Tag every entry FACT, ASSUMPTION or UNKNOWN, with source and date. Working rule: if more than half of a heading is assumption or unknown, that heading is a research gap, not a finding.
8. Join the corners. This is the step that separates Ohmae's triangle from a form: read across and write three to five tensions, places where a fact in one corner collides with a fact in another (for example a customer need no competitor meets, or a company strength customers do not pay for). Mark these INTERPRETATION.
9. Write the gap list and the tensions, pass them to S0-13 and to a diagnostic method, and return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. Input: a language school in Tashkent with three branches wants to know why enrolment has stopped growing.

```
5C SUMMARY (invented figures)                                         TAG
Customers: 1,200 students; 780 school-age, 420 adults                  FACT (CRM)
  Parents choose for exam results                                       ASSUMPTION
Company: capacity 1,500 seats, so 80% full; fee 600,000 soum a month    FACT
  "Our teachers are the best"                                           ASSUMPTION
Competitors: 4 schools near the branches; fees 450,000 / 550,000 /
  700,000 / 900,000; average 650,000; our fee is 7.7% below average     FACT (sites)
  Online tutors on Telegram: number and price                           UNKNOWN
Collaborators: 6 partner secondary schools sent 180 students = 15%      FACT (CRM)
Context: exam rules and demand for certificates                         UNKNOWN -> S0-09
Register: 22 entries = 13 FACT + 6 ASSUMPTION + 3 UNKNOWN (59% facts)
TENSIONS (INTERPRETATION)
1. Price is below average yet seats are empty: price is probably not the barrier.
2. 15% of students come through 6 schools: a channel that works but is small.
3. Adults are 35% of students but all advertising addresses parents.
GAPS: why parents choose (S1-04); online tutors (S1-14); context scan (S0-09).
```
Arithmetic check: 780 + 420 = 1,200; 1,200 / 1,500 = 0.80; (450 + 550 + 700 + 900) / 4 = 2,600 / 4 = 650 thousand; 600 / 650 = 0.923, so 7.7% below; 180 / 1,200 = 0.15; 420 / 1,200 = 0.35; 13 + 6 + 3 = 22; 13 / 22 = 0.59.

## Common mistakes
- Treating the filled table as the analysis and stopping there.
- Filling "company" with praise and "competitors" with faults.
- Listing competitors the owner dislikes instead of those customers compare.
- Mixing facts and opinions with no tags, sources or dates.
- Writing customers as one average person.
- Copying a full PESTEL into "context" without filtering for the decision.
- Analysing the whole company when the decision concerns one product line.

## Quality checklist
- Is the decision stated at the top?
- Does every entry have a tag, a source and a date?
- Were competitors chosen from customer evidence?
- Are collaborators listed with what each of them gains?
- Is the share of facts per heading shown, and are gaps named?
- Are there three to five cross-corner tensions, marked as interpretation?
- Is the next method named for each gap?

## Output template
```
5C SITUATION ANALYSIS - [brand / line] - [market] - [date]
Decision this serves: ...
CUSTOMERS:     entry | tag FACT/ASSUMPTION/UNKNOWN | source, date
COMPANY:       ...
COMPETITORS:   [3-5 named, incl. indirect] ...
COLLABORATORS: who | role | what they gain ...
CONTEXT:       [only factors relevant to the decision; from S0-09] ...
Register: ... entries = ... FACT + ... ASSUMPTION + ... UNKNOWN
TENSIONS (INTERPRETATION): 1 ... 2 ... 3 ...
GAPS and next method: ...
```

## Works with
- S0-02 management decision problem to research problem: sets relevance. S1-03 desk research first and S0-12 client and stakeholder interviews: supply the entries. S0-13 facts, assumptions, unknowns register: holds the tags.
- S0-09 PESTEL for context; S0-10 five forces for competitors; S0-06 funnel diagnosis and S0-05 growth decomposition for customers and company; S4-23 market sizing.
- S0-01 strategy kernel, S0-03 issue tree, S0-04 5 Whys and Ishikawa: the diagnosis this checklist cannot give.
- S0-08 SWOT: only as a summary slide afterwards. S1-18 B2B buying-centre mapping: when collaborators or buyers are organisations.
- S6-08 strategy choice cascade and S6-01 segmentation, targeting, positioning: where the sorted material is used later. S8-07 national statistics open data: a source for context and market facts.

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Collect in the language of the source (Uzbek or Russian) and keep original wording for quotes; translate only the summary.
- Practical caution: public data on private competitors may be thin. Expect many UNKNOWN tags under competitors and plan mystery shopping (S1-13) or social listening (S1-14) instead of guessing.
- Owners may describe the company more favourably in a meeting than the records show; ask for the file behind each claim (S0-12).
- Collaborators deserve their own heading: where sales run through dealers, bazaars or agents, treat the intermediary's interests as facts to collect, not as a footnote. Whether this applies is a hypothesis to check per client.
- Keep Tashkent and the regions as separate rows; do not assume one describes the other.
- Ohmae's examples are large Japanese and Western firms; treat any lesson carried over as a hypothesis.
