# Netnography and social listening (S1-14)

Netnography is ethnography adapted to online communities. Robert Kozinets developed it in the 1990s and set out the procedure for marketing researchers in 2002: choose online communities, immerse in them, collect posts and field notes, interpret them in their cultural context, follow ethics rules and check findings with members. Social listening is the broader commercial practice of monitoring mentions of brands and topics across social media, usually with software and at larger scale. This skill uses netnography as the disciplined core and treats listening tools as a way to find and collect material. The method is for learning how people talk about a category in their own words, what they complain about, and what meanings and rituals surround a product.

Evidence grade: B. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. Netnography is published in a leading journal and widely used, and it has a documented procedure. Its limits are also documented: only text and images are observed, posters' identities are unknown, and people who post are not a sample of all customers. Use it to generate language, themes and hypotheses. Do not use it to estimate how many customers hold a view.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (a post exists, with its wording, source and date) versus INTERPRETATION (what the post means, how common the view is). When the report is delivered, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- The team needs customers' own words for a questionnaire, an interview guide or message drafts.
- Complaints, workarounds and comparisons between brands are discussed in public groups, comment threads or reviews.
- The topic is hard to raise in an interview but is discussed freely online.
- A fast, low-cost exploratory step is needed before interviews or a survey.

Do not use it when:
- The decision needs a share or a size ("what percent of buyers..."). Online voices are not representative. Use S1-08.
- The relevant customers do not talk about the category online, or the only material is the brand's own page.
- The only available sources are private chats or closed groups where members expect privacy and permission cannot be obtained.
- The team plans to count keywords by software and call it insight. Automated sentiment tools handle sarcasm and cultural context poorly; the core analysis here is interpretive.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. The decision and research question (S0-02, S2-01), and the expectations written down beforehand (S2-11).
2. Category words, brand names, nicknames and misspellings in Uzbek (Latin and Cyrillic) and Russian.
3. Candidate sources: Telegram groups and channel comments, Instagram comments, marketplace and map reviews, forums.
4. Someone who reads the languages and slang of the community natively.
5. A spreadsheet for the post log and a document for field notes.
6. An ethics decision for each source (step 4), agreed with the user before collection.

## Procedure
1. Check fit. Ask the user: "Do we need wording and themes, or a number? Where do these customers actually talk about the category?" If a number is needed, return to the research-orchestrator skill and propose S1-08. If nobody can name a source, spend one hour searching before committing.
2. Entree (entry): choose the communities. List candidates and rate each on the criteria attributed to Kozinets: a focus that matches the research question; many posts and high traffic; real interaction between members, not only brand announcements; varied members; detailed and recent posts. Keep the best few. A small set of rich sources beats a large volume of thin ones.
3. Get familiar before collecting. Read each source for a period without saving anything. Note who posts, the tone, in-group words, emoji and memes, what gets answered and what is ignored, and the group rules. Write this up as the first field note.
4. Make the ethics decision per source and write it down. Kozinets (2002) recommended four things: disclose the researcher's presence and purpose to the community, keep members anonymous, seek and use feedback from members, and ask permission to use specific postings. Langer and Beckmann (2005) argued this is too strict for clearly public material and that anonymity may be enough. The Association of Internet Researchers (2020) advises judging case by case: people may expect privacy even in public spaces; verbatim quotes can be traced by a simple search; the more vulnerable the people, the greater the duty of care; platform terms should be checked. Working rules for this agency: Open reviews and public comment threads: observe without posting; no names, handles or profile links in any file that leaves the team. Groups that need joining or admin approval: ask the admin and state the purpose, or do not use the group. Never use private messages. Never create a false persona to draw out posts. If the researcher asks questions in the community, disclose who is asking and why. Material from minors, or on health, debt or family conflict: paraphrase only, or leave out.
5. Collect three kinds of data. Archival: existing posts copied with source, date, thread and language, no usernames. Elicited: answers to questions the researcher posts openly (optional). Field notes: the researcher's own observations and reflections, written each session. Save the surrounding thread, not single sentences, because meaning depends on context.
6. Decide when to stop. Collect while new themes keep appearing. Working rule: stop on a source when a full session of reading adds no new code, and record the number of posts and threads kept per source.
7. Classify the material before interpreting. Mark each item as on-topic or off-topic, and as experience (what happened to the poster), opinion, question, or advice to others. Mark suspected paid or seller posts. Experience posts carry the most weight.
8. Code and interpret. Code the posts (S4-01 or S4-04), keeping original wording. Build a glossary of customer terms with meanings. Group complaints by the stage of buying or use. Read each theme in its community context: who says it, who answers, what the group treats as normal. Compare sources: does the same theme appear in reviews and in group talk?
9. Treat counts with care. It is fine to say "11 threads in two groups raised this". It is not fine to convert that into a share of customers. If counting categories is the real aim, hand over to S4-21 with a defined sampling rule.
10. Check the reading. Where the community was told about the study, share a summary and ask members what is wrong or missing (member check; see S5-14). Otherwise check with two or three customers in interviews.
11. Triangulate and report. State which claims rest only on online material. Propose interviews (S1-04) or a survey (S1-08) for anything the client will spend money on. Then return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. A Tashkent juice brand wants to know how parents talk about packaged juice for children before a survey is written. Input: 3 sources chosen from 9 candidates (a parents' Telegram group joined with the admin's permission, comment threads under 2 public Instagram pages about children's food, reviews on a marketplace); 4 weeks; 240 items kept: 84 group messages, 96 comments, 60 reviews (84 + 96 + 60 = 240). Output (extract):

```
GLOSSARY (customer wording, original language)
"shakar suvi"        - juice seen as sugared water          FACT (wording)
"натуральный состав" - short ingredient list, no additives  FACT (wording)
"bolaga beradigan"   - juice judged safe enough for a child FACT (wording)
THEMES                             | Where seen                 | Type
Parents read the sugar line first  | group 14 threads,          | FACT (posts)
                                   | comments 9 threads         |
Small pack for school bag praised  | reviews 12, group 3        | FACT (posts)
Distrust of "100%" on the label    | group 6 threads            | FACT (posts)
Reading: "100%" may not work as proof for this group; composition
  wording may matter more than the claim.                   INTERP
Not known: how common this is among all buyers; older and rural
  buyers are absent from these sources.                     LIMIT
Hand-over: glossary to S3-01 for survey wording; theme 3 to S1-04.
```

## Common mistakes
- Treating the volume of posts as the size of a customer group. Scraping keywords without reading threads, so sarcasm and jokes are coded as praise.
- Skipping field notes, which leaves only a pile of quotes with no context.
- Studying only the brand's own page, where critical comments may be removed.
- Copying usernames, profile photos or traceable verbatim quotes into the client report. Entering a closed group under a false identity.
- Translating posts before coding and losing the original wording.
- Ignoring paid reviews and seller accounts.

## Quality checklist
- Is the aim language, themes and hypotheses, not a market share or percentage?
- Was each source rated on relevance, activity, interaction, variety of members and richness?
- Is there a written ethics decision for every source, and were closed groups used only with permission?
- Are field notes kept alongside the post log?
- Are posts stored with source, date and thread, and without names or handles?
- Were themes checked across more than one source or with customers?
- Does the report say who is missing from these online voices?

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Search in Uzbek Latin, Uzbek Cyrillic and Russian, including mixed-language posts and common misspellings. Keep every quote in the original language and script in the working files; translate only in the report, next to a paraphrase.
- Telegram groups and channel comments are a main candidate source, but many groups are semi-private. Apply the closed-group rule: ask the admin or leave it.
- Who is online differs by age and place. Treat the voices found as those of people who post, and say plainly that older and rural buyers are likely to be missing. Check current figures with S8-04 instead of assuming.
- Public praise and polite wording may hide criticism; complaints may move to private messages. Absence of complaints in a thread is not evidence of satisfaction.
- Report Tashkent-based and regional groups separately where the source makes the place clear.
- Kozinets's work and the ethics guidelines come from Western online communities. Treat their categories of member behaviour as hypotheses here.
