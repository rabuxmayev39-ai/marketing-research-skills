# Secondary (desk) research first (S1-03)

Secondary research means using data that someone else collected, usually for a different purpose: official statistics, company records, trade reports, published studies, media, competitor materials. The working principle in marketing research textbooks is to look at what exists before paying for new data. The standard reference is David Stewart and Michael Kamins, Secondary Research: Information Sources and Methods (Sage, 1993; first edition by Stewart, 1984), which treats the evaluation of a source as the core skill. The evaluation questions used in this skill come from free pages that were opened (listed under Sources); they follow the same line of questioning about purpose, collector, content, date, method and consistency.

Evidence grade: B. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. "Desk research first" is a professional rule of economy, not a tested theory. Rely on it as a default order of work. The weight of any single finding depends entirely on the source behind it, which is why every number must carry its source, date and a quality rating.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (a figure or statement exactly as the source gives it, with source and date) versus INTERPRETATION (your estimate, adjustment or conclusion). Never present a number from memory as a finding; search, open the source and log it. When the desk report is delivered, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- A project starts and the team needs background, market size, competitors, prices or population figures.
- The client is about to pay for a survey or interviews; check first what is already answered.
- Hypotheses or answer options are needed for later stages. Textbook course notes list secondary data analysis among exploratory methods.
- Primary results need a benchmark or a sanity check.

Do not use it as the only step when:
- The question is about this client's own customers' motives or reactions to a new idea. Existing data rarely hold that; plan primary research.
- Available sources fail the evaluation and no better ones exist. Say that the question cannot be answered from the desk.
- The definitions in the source do not match the question and cannot be adjusted (see step 6).

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. The decision and the research questions (S0-02, S2-01), turned into a list of information needs.
2. The client's internal data: sales by product, region and channel; CRM; price lists; past research; sales staff knowledge. Internal data come first because they are free and specific.
3. Definitions: the product category, geography, period and customer group, written precisely.
4. Languages to search in (Uzbek in Latin and Cyrillic script, Russian, English) and a time limit for the desk stage.
5. A source log (template below) opened before the first search.

## Procedure
1. Fix the information needs. For each research question write what fact would answer it and how exact it must be ("order of magnitude" or "within 10%"). Ask the user: "Which of these do you already have in your own records?"
2. Start inside the company. Collect internal figures and note their own limits (period, what is not recorded). Decide what remains open.
3. Search outward, from most to least accountable. Working order: official statistics and regulators; international organisations; industry associations and trade bodies; academic studies; paid market reports; company reports and websites; news media; social media and marketplaces. Search each open need in all agreed languages.
4. Log every source as you open it: title, publisher, URL, publication date, date the data refer to, date accessed, and the exact figure or statement with page or table. Working rule: nothing enters the report that is not in the log.
5. Evaluate each source with six questions before using it.
   - Purpose: why was the data collected and published? A commercial or political interest can tilt what is shown. Who: who collected it, and are they competent and accountable? Opened guidance ranks named agencies and recognised bodies above anonymous sites.
   - What: what exactly was measured, with what definition and unit? When: when were the data collected (not when the article appeared)? Sound data can still be too old.
   - How: what method, sample and coverage? Does the population studied match yours? Data with no description of procedure should be used with caution. Consistency: does it agree with other independent sources? Where it does not, look for the reason in definitions and methods before picking one.
   Rate each source (working rule): A = method documented, accountable collector; B = partly documented or older; C = no method shown, or interested party. C sources may suggest hypotheses but may not carry a conclusion.
6. Check measurement fit. An open research-methods text warns that the original team may have defined or measured a concept differently from what you need, and that a topic minor to them may be measured thinly. Compare category definition, geography, period and unit. If they differ, either adjust openly (and mark the result INTERPRETATION) or do not use the figure.
7. Trace figures to their origin. If an article cites a number, find the original publication. Working rule: several articles repeating one original count as one source.
8. Triangulate key numbers. For each number the decision depends on, look for at least two independent sources (working rule). Report the range, not one point, and explain the gap.
9. Write the gap list: which information needs are answered (with rating), partly answered, or open. Open and C-rated items become the brief for primary research. This list is the main product of the stage.
10. Report facts with source and date in every line, estimates marked as interpretation, and the gap list. Return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example

## Common mistakes
- Skipping the client's own records and searching the internet first.
- Quoting a number without the date the data refer to, or citing the article instead of the original.
- Counting three reposts of one figure as three confirmations.
- Using a figure whose category or geography differs from the question without saying so.
- Treating a seller's or advertiser's "market review" as neutral.

## Quality checklist
- Were internal data checked before external search?
- Does every figure in the report have source, data date and access date in the log?
- Were all six evaluation questions answered for each source used in a conclusion?
- Were key numbers checked against a second independent source, or marked single-source?
- Are definition mismatches stated and adjustments labelled as interpretation?
- Is there a gap list that feeds the primary research brief?

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Sources are thin for many categories and regions. Expect more "open" lines in the gap list than a Western textbook assumes, and plan primary research accordingly.
- Record source and date for every figure without exception. Figures can be revised and web pages moved; save a copy or screenshot with the access date.
- Search in Uzbek (Latin and Cyrillic), Russian and English; the same organisation may publish different detail in each. Keep titles and quoted wording in the original language in the log.
- Check whether a figure covers the whole country, Tashkent city or Tashkent region; do not apply Tashkent figures to regions or the reverse without marking it as an assumption.
- Digital-audience figures describe people who are online; they under-represent older and rural people, as Telegram-bot samples do. Figures borrowed from Russia, Kazakhstan or other markets are analogies. Label them as hypotheses, never as local facts.
