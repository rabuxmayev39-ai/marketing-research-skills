# Observation -> finding -> insight -> implication ladder (S5-02)

The ladder is a working discipline for research synthesis. It separates four kinds of statement: what was seen or said (observation), a pattern across observations (finding), an explanation of why the pattern exists and why it matters (insight), and what the business should consider doing about it (implication). It has no single author. It is a convention from UX research and advertising planning practice; versions with slightly different rung names are published by Nielsen Norman Group (data, findings, insights) and by research firms. Evidence grade: C, a practitioner tool with little formal testing. It makes reasoning visible and easier to check, but climbing the ladder does not make an insight true. Every rung above "observation" is an interpretation and should be tested with other data and other people.

Always answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label each statement as fact or interpretation, and return to the research-orchestrator skill when the ladder is complete.

## When to use it / When not to use it

Use it when:
- Fieldwork or desk research is analysed into clusters or themes (for example after S5-01) and the team must write conclusions.
- A draft report mixes quotes, patterns, explanations and advice in one list.
- A client asks "so what?" about a set of findings.

Do not use it when:
- Data have not yet been sorted. Do the sorting first.
- The client only needs a quick fix list (for example usability faults). Findings may be enough; do not dress them up as insights.

## What you need before starting (inputs)

- Sorted data: clusters, coded transcripts, or survey tables, with participant IDs.
- The project decision and research objectives (what the client must decide).
- Context that is not in the fieldwork: earlier research, sales data, category facts, business goals. Insights need this context.

## Procedure

1. Restate the decision. Ask the user: "What decision will this work feed, and who makes it?" Write it at the top. Everything on the ladder must relate to it.

2. Rung 1, observations (fact). List the raw points: a quote, a behaviour seen, a number from a table. Rules:
   - Use the participant's words or a plain description. No "because", no adjectives of judgement.
   - Attach a source ID to each.
   - Keep quotes in the original language.

3. Rung 2, findings (low interpretation). A finding is a pattern or summary across observations. It says what happened, not the deep reason.
   - Write each finding as one sentence with its base: "8 of 12 bakers ...", "In all regional interviews ...".
   - A reason that participants stated themselves is still part of the finding. Mark it "stated reason".
   - Test: could a careful reader of the data disagree? If yes, it is already more than a finding; move it up or cut it back.

4. Look for tension before climbing. For each finding ask:
   - Does it conflict with another finding, with what participants say about themselves, or with what the client believed?
   - What is the stated reason, and is there a reason they did not state?
   - Who does not fit the pattern?
   Findings with a contradiction or surprise are the best raw material for insights. Findings with none may simply stay findings.

5. Rung 3, insights (interpretation). An insight explains why the pattern exists, using more than one finding plus outside context, and changes how the team sees the problem.
   - Combine at least two findings, or one finding and one piece of context.
   - Ask "why?" several times past the stated reason. Stop when the answer is a motive, belief or constraint that the data can still support.
   - Write it in one or two sentences, about people, not about the brand.
   - Describe the problem, not the solution. If the sentence contains "should", it is an implication.
   - Write a rival explanation next to it and say what data would separate the two.
   - Rate support: strong (several sources, no counter-cases), medium, or weak (plausible, thin data).
   Not every project yields an insight. Say so if that is the case.

6. Rung 4, implications (judgement). An implication states what the insight means for the decision in step 1.
   - Write it as a direction, not a finished solution: "Communication should prove X before talking about Y."
   - Tie each implication to exactly one insight or finding by ID.
   - Add what would need to be tested before acting.
   - Keep implications separate from recommendations that depend on budget or politics; those belong to strategy work.

7. Check the chain in both directions.
   - Downward: for each implication ask "why?" and confirm the insight answers it; for each insight ask "how do we know?" and confirm the findings answer it; for each finding confirm the observations exist.
   - Upward: for each finding ask "so what?".
   Cut any rung that has no support below it. Flag any important finding that has nothing above it.

8. Triangulate. Check each insight against a different source (another method, another researcher's reading, sales or survey data). Record what was checked and what was not.

9. Present. Give the user one ladder per insight using the template, plus a list of "findings without an insight". Ask which insights they doubt and why. Then return to the research-orchestrator skill.

Decision rules:
- If the "insight" could be read straight from one quote, it is an observation.
- If it starts with a number, it is a finding.
- If it names the brand or a campaign idea, it is an implication or an idea.

## Worked example

INVENTED example for illustration only. A flour mill wants more small bakeries in regional towns to buy its flour. Decision: what to say and offer to bakery owners.

Observations (fact):
- O1 (P04): "Un yaxshi, lekin eski un bilan xamirim tanish" [The flour is good, but I know my dough with the old flour]
- O2 (P07): baked a small test batch at night, after the day's sales
- O3 (P02): "Один раз хлеб не поднялся - клиенты ушли к соседу" [Once the bread did not rise and customers went next door]
- O4 (sales data): trial bags were taken by 15 bakeries; 3 reordered

Findings:
- F1: 9 of 11 bakers describe a new flour in terms of how the dough behaves, not price or protein figures.
- F2: 6 of 11 tested new flour outside selling hours or in small batches.
- F3: Trial uptake is high but reorder is low (15 to 3).

Insight (interpretation, support: medium): For a small baker a new flour is not a purchase to compare but a risk to tomorrow's batch: one failed bake costs customers at once, while a better price pays back slowly. So bakers accept a free trial out of politeness and then stay with the flour whose behaviour they know.
Rival explanation: reorders are low because the distributor does not come back. Data needed: check delivery visits for the 12 non-reordering bakeries.

Implication (judgement): The offer should reduce the risk of the first full batch (for example a technologist present at the first bake, or a recipe adjustment card) before it talks about price. To test: pilot with 10 bakeries and compare reorders.

## Common mistakes

- Calling every finding an insight.
- Jumping from a quote straight to a recommendation.
- Adding the participants' own stated reason and calling the result an insight.
- Insights written as solutions or slogans.
- No rival explanation, so the first plausible story wins.
- Implications with no line back to evidence.

## Quality checklist

- Is the client decision written at the top?
- Does every observation carry a source ID and stay free of interpretation?
- Does every finding state its base (how many, who)?
- Does every insight rest on two or more findings or on a finding plus context?
- Is each insight problem-focused and free of "should"?
- Is there a rival explanation and a support rating for each insight?
- Does each implication trace to one insight and name what to test?
- Are facts and interpretations labelled?


## Notes for Uzbekistan

- Keep rung-1 quotes in Uzbek or Russian as spoken. Write rungs 2 to 4 in the team's working language and check that the translation did not add a reason that was not said.
- Where participants tend to give polite, agreeing answers, treat approval statements as weak observations and behaviour (what they bought, reordered, did) as strong ones. Note which is which.
- Write the base of each finding by place (Tashkent, regional city, village) when the sample mixes them. Do not merge them into one pattern unless it holds in each.
