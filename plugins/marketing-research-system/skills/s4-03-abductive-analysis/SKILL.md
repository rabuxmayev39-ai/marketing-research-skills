# Abductive analysis: working from surprises (S4-03)

Abductive analysis is an approach to qualitative analysis that builds new explanations from findings that existing explanations did not predict. Stefan Timmermans and Iddo Tavory proposed it in Sociological Theory (2012), drawing on the philosopher Charles S. Peirce, for whom abduction is the step of forming a hypothesis that would make a surprising fact unsurprising. Their argument against classic grounded theory is that nobody observes without prior ideas, so the analyst should know many theories well and look for what none of them explains. For a marketing team it is the method for the finding that "should not be there".

Evidence grade: A. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. The approach is published and widely discussed in peer-reviewed journals and has been developed further by other authors. Weight it correctly: abduction is the most conjectural form of inference. Its product is a plausible hypothesis that fits the data better than rivals. It becomes a finding only after it has been checked against the full dataset and, where possible, new data.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (observations and quotes with IDs) versus INTERPRETATION (expectations, candidate explanations, the chosen hypothesis). When the hypothesis and its test plan are written, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- A result contradicts the brief, the client's belief, an earlier study or a standard marketing model.
- Coding has produced many patterns but no organising explanation.
- The team can name what it expected, so that the gap between expectation and data can be stated.

Do not use it when:
- Nothing in the data is surprising and a description of patterns is enough (S4-01).
- The team knows no relevant theories or prior findings and has no time to read any. Without expectations there are no surprises, only novelties.
- A fixed hypothesis must be tested on a fixed frame (S4-21 or a survey), or the "surprise" rests on one remark that cannot be traced in the data.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. The dataset in a written, re-readable form: transcripts, field notes, coded extracts, memos.
2. A written list of expectations: what the client, the team and the literature predicted, each with its source.
3. Three or more theories, models or earlier findings relevant to the topic. They can come from academic work, the methodology index, or earlier agency projects.
4. A colleague or client contact willing to argue with the explanation, and the option to go back to the data and ideally collect a little more.

## Procedure
1. State expectations before looking for surprises. Ask the user: "What did you, the client and the textbooks expect to find, and why?" Write each as "We expected X because of Y." The source's position is informed theoretical agnosticism: know several theories, be committed to none.
2. Find the anomalies. Go through the coded data and list observations that an expectation did not predict. Separate two things, following Peirce's distinction as the paper presents it: a novelty is merely new; an anomaly conflicts with an expectation. Only anomalies go forward. Write each as: "Expected X (source). Observed Y (FACT, IDs)."
3. Check that the surprise is real (working rule). How many respondents and which segments show it? Could it come from the question wording, the interviewer, translation, or a polite answer? Drop or park anomalies that rest on one unclear remark.
4. Revisit the phenomenon. Reread the full transcripts around each anomaly, not only the coded extract, and reread them again after step 6. The paper's point is that an observation holds more than a first reading shows, and that a later reading with a different idea in mind picks up what was passed over.
5. Defamiliarise. Make the familiar strange so that what was taken for granted becomes visible. Practical moves in the spirit of the source: work from the written transcript, not memory; lay comparable passages side by side; list what respondents did in order; mark inconsistencies inside one account; ask "what would an outsider find odd here?"
6. Alternative casing. Take the anomaly and a small set of extracts and ask of each theory in turn: "What is this a case of, if this theory is right?" Write one candidate explanation per theory, plus one that comes from no theory. For each, list which extracts it accounts for and which it cannot. Keep several casings open at once; do not settle early. Aim for at least three (working rule).
7. Form the abductive hypothesis. Choose or combine the explanation that makes the anomaly follow as a matter of course. State it in Peirce's form: "Surprising fact Y is observed. If H were true, Y would be expected. So H is worth testing." Say what existing idea it modifies or replaces.
8. Work out consequences (deduction). If H is true, what else must appear in the data, and what must not? Write three to five checkable implications before looking (the count is a working rule).
9. Test against data (induction). Go through the whole dataset for each implication. Search actively for negative cases and rival explanations. Then decide: keep H, narrow it to stated conditions, or discard it and return to step 6. If the dataset cannot settle it, specify what new data would.
10. Put it to a community of inquiry. Present the anomaly, the rival explanations and the test result to colleagues or the client and ask for objections. The source treats disagreement among people with different standards as productive. Record objections and what changed.
11. Report the hypothesis with its status (supported in this dataset, partly supported, untested), the rivals rejected and why, and the next test. Then return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. A juice brand cut its price in small Tashkent shops. Interviews with 15 shop owners and 10 buyers followed.

Input:
```
Expected: lower price -> more purchases (client; standard price logic)
Observed: 9 of 15 owners report slower sales after the cut.             FACT
  "Arzonlashgandan keyin mehmonga olmay qo'yishdi." (owner O4)          FACT
  "В гости такое уже не понесёшь." (buyer B7)                           FACT
```
Output:
```
Anomaly real? 9 owners, 6 buyers, both districts; not one interviewer.  FACT
Casing 1, price as quality signal: cheap = worse juice. Explains the
  drop; does not explain why home use continued.                        INTERP
Casing 2, gift and hospitality use: the juice was bought to bring when
  visiting; a visibly cheap item cannot be given. Explains both.        INTERP
Casing 3, distributor effect: agents pushed it less after margin fell.
  Explains owners' reports; does not explain buyers' words.             INTERP
Hypothesis H: the cut removed the product from the "bring to hosts"
  role while leaving home use unchanged.                                INTERP
Implications: (a) large packs fell more than small; (b) drop strongest
  before holidays; (c) buyers name a replacement gift item.
Test in data: (c) found in 5 of 6 buyers; (a), (b) need sales data.
Negative case: O11, sales rose - shop beside a school.                  FACT
Status: partly supported; next test = sales by pack size and week.
```

## Common mistakes
- Calling any unexpected remark a surprise without stating what was expected and why.
- Arriving with one favourite theory and fitting the data to it, or claiming to have no prior ideas at all.
- Stopping at the clever hypothesis and skipping steps 8-9.
- Considering only one explanation, so that no rival was ever rejected.
- Reading only coded extracts and never returning to the full account.
- Building the hypothesis alone and reporting it as a settled fact about the market.

## Quality checklist
- Are expectations written down with their sources, before the anomalies?
- Is each anomaly stated as expected versus observed, with respondent IDs?
- Was each anomaly checked for artefacts of method, translation or politeness?
- Were at least three candidate explanations written, with what each cannot explain?
- Were checkable implications written before testing?
- Were negative cases searched for and reported?
- Did someone outside the analysis challenge the explanation?
- Is the status of the hypothesis and the next test stated?

## Works with
- S4-01 thematic analysis and S4-04 coding cycles: supply the coded data in which anomalies are found; versus codes often point to them.
- S4-02 grounded theory coding: its line-by-line coding, memos and constant comparison are kept; the theory-free stance is not.
- S4-05 matrix displays: a respondent-by-theme matrix makes exceptions and empty cells visible. S4-07 negative case analysis: used in step 9.
- S2-07 triangulation: check the anomaly in a second data source before building on it. S5-03 insight tests and S5-05 what would have to be true: turn the hypothesis into conditions and tests.
- S5-14 member checking: ask respondents whether the explanation fits, without treating agreement as proof.

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Before treating an answer as an anomaly, rule out politeness: a respondent who agrees with everything, or praises the product to the interviewer, is not evidence against an expectation. Look for described behaviour.
- Check the original wording. A surprise that exists only in the Russian or English translation of an Uzbek answer (or the reverse) is a translation artefact. Keep quotes in the original language in the note.
- Compare Tashkent and regional respondents before explaining: a "surprise" may be a difference between places that the expectation ignored.
- If the data come from a Telegram-bot survey, a surprising pattern may reflect who answers such bots (older and rural people are likely to be missing), not the market.
- The expectations themselves usually come from theory tested on Western samples. A mismatch may show that the theory does not transfer. Record that as one of the rival explanations; do not assume it. Team members' local experience is a legitimate lens in step 6 if written down and tested like the others.
