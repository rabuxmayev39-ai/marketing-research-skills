# Write expectations down before fieldwork (S2-11)
Before the first interview or the first survey response, the team writes what it expects to find, dates the list and locks it. After fieldwork the results are compared with the list line by line. Two lines of work stand behind the practice. Nosek, Ebersole, DeHaven and Mellor (2018) describe preregistration: stating the research questions and the analysis plan before outcomes are known, so that results that were predicted can be told apart from results explained afterwards. They note that ordinary reasoning biases, hindsight bias among them, make this mix-up easy. Timmermans and Tavory (2012) argue that new ideas in qualitative research come from abduction, which they describe as forming hypotheses from surprising evidence, and that what a researcher can find surprising depends on the theories and position the researcher starts from. This skill joins the two: a finding can only be recognised as a surprise against a recorded expectation. Evidence grade: A. Both sources are peer-reviewed and preregistration is widely used in science. The grade covers the logic of separating prediction from after-the-fact explanation. The short commercial form used here is this skill's own adaptation and has not been tested on its own. The list does not stop bias; it only makes bias visible if someone checks results against it.

Working rules for the assistant:
- Answer in the language the user writes in (Uzbek, Russian or English).
- Label every statement as FACT (what was written in advance, what the data show) or INTERPRETATION (your reading).
- Never rewrite or tidy an expectation after fieldwork has started. Add dated notes instead.
- When the work is finished, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- A study is about to start: interviews, groups, observation, a survey, a test or mystery shopping.
- The client or the team already "knows" the answer, and the study risks only confirming it.
- Several people will analyse the data and need a common reference for what counts as new.
Do not use it when:
- Fieldwork is finished and results are known. A list written now is an after-the-fact explanation; label it as such and do not present it as a prediction.
- The task is the hypothesis tree itself (S0-03) or the facts and assumptions register (S0-13). This skill takes those as input and adds expected answers.
- A formal confirmatory test with a fixed analysis plan is needed (S1-09, S7-02). Use a full preregistration template for that and keep this list as a supplement.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
- The research brief and question hierarchy (S2-01), the hypotheses (S0-03) and the assumptions register (S0-13).
- The interview guide or questionnaire draft, so that expectations can be tied to specific questions.
- Names of everyone who will write expectations: client decision owner, account lead, researchers, interviewers.
- A place where a file can be saved with a date and made read-only (shared drive, pinned message, e-mail to the whole team).

## Procedure
1. Fix the moment. The list must be complete before any outcome is seen: before the first interview, before survey data arrive, before anyone opens a results file. If some data already exist, write down exactly what has already been seen and by whom. Nosek and colleagues treat existing data as a case that weakens the prediction and needs to be disclosed, and the Center for Open Science asks registrants to state how much of the data they had accessed.
2. Collect expectations separately. Ask each person to write alone, before any group talk, so that the senior voice does not set everyone's answer (working rule). Ask the user for the client's own expectations as well; they are often the most useful line to test.
3. Use three kinds of entries. Hypotheses: statements about the market that could be wrong ("small bakeries choose flour mainly by price"). Expected answers: for each key question in the guide or questionnaire, what the team thinks it will hear, or the number or range it expects ("30 to 40 percent will name our brand unaided"). Expected absences: things the team thinks nobody will mention.
4. Make each entry checkable. For every line record: the statement; whose it is; where it comes from (previous study, sales data, client belief, theory, guess); confidence (high, medium, low); what result would support it; what result would contradict it; and which decision changes if it is wrong. If nobody can say what would contradict a line, rewrite it until someone can. For survey numbers, write a range and the base it refers to.
5. Record the theories in use. Timmermans and Tavory hold that surprises appear in the light of the theories a researcher already carries. List the two or three frameworks the team is reading the market through (for example S0-11 JTBD or S6-03 penetration) so that later readers know what "expected" meant.
6. Fix the analysis choices that can be bent later (working rule, taken from the preregistration idea of stating the analysis plan in advance): which subgroups will be compared, what counts as a "theme" (for example, raised unprompted by at least a stated number of people), which survey question is the main measure, and which cases will be excluded.
7. Lock the list. Save it with date, time and authors, make it read-only, and send it to the team and the client. The Open Science Framework describes a registration as time-stamped and read-only; a dated PDF in the project folder serves the same purpose here. Do not keep a private editable copy as the master.
8. Handle changes openly. If the plan changes during fieldwork (new segment, changed guide), add a dated amendment that says what changed and why. The Center for Open Science describes a preregistration as "a plan, not a prison": changes are allowed when they are documented, and anything decided after results were seen is reported as exploratory.
9. Name the checker. Assign one person, preferably someone who did not conduct the interviews, to compare results with the list. Set the date of the check in the project plan. Without this step the list has no effect.
10. Compare after fieldwork. Give every entry one status, with evidence (count, base, quotes). Supported: the pre-stated supporting result occurred. Contradicted: the pre-stated contradicting result occurred. Mixed: differs by segment; say which. Not tested: the study produced no relevant evidence; do not count this as support. Then add a separate list of findings nobody expected at all.
11. Work on the surprises. Contradicted, mixed and unexpected items go to abductive analysis (S4-03) and negative case analysis (S4-07). Timmermans and Tavory name three practices for this stage: revisiting the same material, defamiliarisation (making the familiar look strange) and alternative casing (trying different theoretical framings on the same case). Check first that the surprise is not an artefact of the sample or the question wording (S2-13).
12. Report honestly. In the report, mark which conclusions were predicted in advance and which were found afterwards. A conclusion found afterwards is a hypothesis for the next study, not a confirmed result. Supported expectations are also findings; do not hide them to make the report look fresh. Return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE for illustration. No real data.
Input: a flour mill plans 12 interviews with owners of small bakeries in Tashkent and two regions. Before the first interview, the client's sales director, the account lead and two researchers each write expectations. The merged list has 8 entries and is saved as a read-only PDF on 3 March, 18:40.
Extract of the locked list:

| # | Expectation | Owner | Confidence | Contradicted if |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Price per sack is the first reason for choosing a supplier | Sales director | High | Fewer than half name price first, unprompted |
| 2 | Bakers switch mills at least once a year | Account lead | Medium | Most have used the same mill for 2+ years |
| 3 | Nobody will mention delivery timing | Researcher | Low | Three or more raise it unprompted |

Output after fieldwork (checker: the second researcher, who did no interviews):

| Status | Entries | Count |
|---|---|---|
| Supported | 2, 5, 8 | 3 |
| Contradicted | 1, 3 | 2 |
| Mixed | 4 | 1 |
| Not tested | 6, 7 | 2 |

FACT: 3 + 2 + 1 + 2 = 8 entries, all accounted for. Entry 1: 4 of 12 owners named price first; 7 of 12 named consistency of the flour between batches first; 1 named credit terms (4 + 7 + 1 = 12). Entry 3: 5 of 12 raised delivery timing unprompted. Unexpected: 3 owners test each new sack with a small trial dough before using it. INTERPRETATION: the high-confidence belief about price did not hold in this sample; batch consistency goes to S4-03 as the main surprise. Limit: 12 purposively chosen bakeries cannot give a market share of opinions; the result is a reason to test the price belief in a survey, with a new expectation written before that survey.

## Common mistakes
- Writing the list after the first interviews "because we were busy", and calling it a prediction.
- One merged list written in a meeting, which records only the most senior opinion.
- Vague entries that no result could contradict ("quality matters").
- Editing the list during fieldwork instead of adding dated amendments; no named checker, so the list is never opened again.
- Counting "not tested" as "supported", or reporting only the surprises.
- Treating every surprise as an insight without checking sample and wording first.

## Quality checklist
- Was the list completed and locked before any results were seen, with date and authors? (yes/no)
- Did each person, including the client, write separately first? (yes/no)
- Does every entry state what would contradict it? (yes/no)
- Are expected answers tied to named questions, with ranges and bases for numbers? (yes/no)
- Are amendments dated and explained, with the original left unchanged? (yes/no)
- Did a named person compare every entry with the results and give each one a status? (yes/no)
- Does the report separate predicted conclusions from those found afterwards? (yes/no)

## Works with
- S0-03 hypothesis-driven problem solving and S0-13 facts, assumptions, unknowns register: supply the entries. S2-01 research brief: the list is attached to it.
- S1-04, S1-05, S1-07, S1-08, S1-09, S1-13: every fieldwork method starts after this list is locked.
- S4-03 abductive analysis and S4-07 negative case analysis: receive the surprises. S3-11 interview snapshot: note surprises against the list after each interview.
- S2-07 triangulation and S2-08 trustworthiness criteria: the locked list is part of the audit trail. S2-13 total survey error: artefact check.
- S5-03 insight tests and S5-05 what would have to be true: use the predicted versus found-afterwards labels. S7-04 project retrospective: review how well the team predicted.

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Let each person write in the language they think in, Uzbek or Russian, and keep the original wording in the locked file. Translate only in a copy.
- Polite or agreeing habits also work inside teams and with clients. Collect the client's and the director's expectations in writing and separately, so that juniors do not simply copy them.
- Respondents' polite agreement can make an expectation look supported. Define "supported" by unprompted mentions or described behaviour, not by "yes" to a leading question (S3-07).
- Write separate expectations for Tashkent and for regions when both are in the sample; a single line for "the market" hides the difference.
- For Telegram-bot surveys, write the expected result for the people the bot reaches, and note that it misses older and rural people.
- Hindsight bias and preregistration were studied mostly on Western researchers and academic studies. Treat the transfer to a small commercial team as a working hypothesis.
