# Positioning statement (S6-02)

A positioning statement is a short internal record of how a product should be understood: what kind of thing it is, whom it is for, what buyers would otherwise choose, what it does better, and why anyone should believe that. The idea of positioning was made popular by Al Ries and Jack Trout, first in trade articles (1969, and a 1972 Advertising Age series) and then in the book Positioning (1981). Their core point is that a position exists in the buyer's mind, not in the marketplace, and that a new position has to connect to what is already in that mind. April Dunford (Obviously Awesome, 2019) turned this into a working sequence of five linked components: competitive alternatives, unique attributes, value, the customers who care most, and market category. This skill follows her sequence and adds a proof step.

Evidence grade: C. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. The method comes from practitioners' experience, mostly in advertising and business software, and has not been tested in controlled research. Three cautions follow. It is only as good as the claimed difference: if the unique value is not real or cannot be shown, the statement is wishful. Dunford herself criticises the classic fill-in-the-blanks statement: she says it assumes there is one right answer, produces an awkward sentence nobody uses, and gives a false sense of being finished. And researchers at the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute argue that perceived differences between competing brands play a smaller part in buying than positioning theory assumes, while Mark Ritson argues that a relative difference (more of something valued than rivals have) is both realistic and useful. Treat the output as a hypothesis to test with buyers.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (what customers said, what the product does, measured proof) versus INTERPRETATION (the chosen category, the claimed value). The statement is an internal document, not advertising copy. When it is written and tested, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- A target has been chosen (S6-01) and the team needs one agreed description of the offer before writing briefs or messages.
- Sales staff, the website and advertising describe the product in different ways.
- Buyers compare the product with the wrong alternatives, or ask "what is this?".

Do not use it when:
- There are no customers and no evidence yet on why anyone buys. Do the interviews first (S1-07, S1-04).
- The team wants a slogan. A slogan is a creative output that comes later (S6-18).
- The product has no demonstrable difference. Fix the offer, or compete on being easy to think of and easy to buy (S6-03), and say so honestly.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. Evidence from recent buyers: why they bought, what they used before, what else they considered (S1-07, S0-12).
2. A list of the product's features, terms, service and business-model facts.
3. Lost-deal or lost-customer reasons, if the client records them.
4. Any measured proof: test results, delivery times, defect or return rates, guarantees, named customer cases.
5. The target decision from S6-01.
6. Working rule: a small group from sales, production and management in one session, because a positioning change affects price, channels and service, not only marketing.

## Procedure
1. List the competitive alternatives. Ask: what would these customers do if this product did not exist? Include doing nothing and makeshift solutions, not only rival brands. Dunford warns against "phantom competitors": firms that could compete but that the client never actually meets or loses to. Take the list from buyer evidence, not from the client's fears. Record how many buyers named each alternative.
2. List the unique attributes: everything the product or company has that those alternatives lack (features, process, terms, location, skills). Do not judge yet whether each is good or bad.
3. Turn attributes into value. For each attribute ask "so what does this let the customer do?" until the answer is an outcome the customer would recognise. Group the outcomes into one to three value themes. Dunford's point is that customers buy outcomes, not features.
4. Attach proof to each value theme (Working rule; this step is this skill's addition). Proof is a measured fact, a demonstration, a guarantee or a named case. Decision rule: a value theme with no proof is either dropped or marked "claim to build", and it cannot be the lead theme.
5. Identify who cares most. Describe the characteristics that make a customer care a lot about the value themes, going beyond industry, size and location. Check the result against the S6-01 target. Working rule: if the best-fit group is a small fraction of category buyers, use it to sharpen the offer and the sales pitch, but do not use it to narrow advertising reach.
6. Choose the market category: the frame of reference that makes the value obvious to those customers. Dunford's advice is to start in an existing category, narrowed with a qualifier if needed, because creating a new category requires a long and costly effort to educate buyers.
7. Write the positioning record (the components table in the template) and, under it, one plain sentence. A widely used pattern is: for [customers] who [situation], [product] is a [category] that [value], unlike [alternative], because [proof]. Its exact origin was not verified for this skill.
8. Run three desk tests (Working rules). Substitution: put a competitor's name into the sentence; if it is still true, it is not a difference. Proof: could each claim be shown to a sceptical buyer today? Plain words: would a customer use these words, in Uzbek or Russian, to describe the product?
9. Test with real prospects. Dunford's guidance is to try a candidate positioning with prospects, keep it if it works and redo it if it does not. Working rule: show the sentence to 10 to 20 prospects, ask them to say back what the product is and why it is better, and count how many get it right; then check with S5-03 and S9-06.
10. Date the record and name a review trigger. Dunford notes that positioning weakens as markets change; revisit when unexpected competitors start appearing in deals.
11. Hand the record to S6-18 and S9-02. Return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. Input: a Tashkent workshop that makes seating for cafes and restaurants. 12 switch interviews with cafe owners who bought in the last year; order records for 150 orders.

```
ALTERNATIVES (12 buyers, main alternative considered)                 FACT
Imported furniture ordered through a dealer: 5 | Local carpenter: 4 |
Keep and repair the old furniture: 3
UNIQUE ATTRIBUTES: own metal and upholstery shop on one site; fabric stock
  held locally; written 24-month frame warranty.                      FACT
VALUE THEMES                                               INTERPRETATION
1. Open on the planned date: delivery in 20 days against 60 for imports.
2. Seating that survives daily commercial use.
PROOF                                                                 FACT
Theme 1: delivery records, 20 days average. Theme 2: 3 warranty claims in
  150 orders (2.0%); warranty document.
WHO CARES MOST: owners opening or refitting a venue with a fixed opening date.
CATEGORY: commercial seating for cafes and restaurants (not "designer furniture").
STATEMENT: For owners fitting out a cafe to a fixed opening date, [brand] is
  commercial seating made in Tashkent that arrives in 20 days and is built
  for daily use, unlike imported furniture that takes about 60 days, because
  frames, upholstery and fabric stock are on one site with a 24-month warranty.
TEST: 20 prospects read it; 14 restated the value correctly (70%).    FACT
```
Arithmetic check: 5 + 4 + 3 = 12; 60 - 20 = 40 days faster, and 20 / 60 = one third of the import time; 3 / 150 = 0.02, or 2.0%; 14 / 20 = 0.70, or 70%.

## Common mistakes
- Starting from features or from the category the product was first built for, and never asking what buyers would do otherwise.
- Naming competitors the client worries about but buyers never mention.
- Claiming a value that has no proof, or that every competitor could claim.
- Inventing a new category when an existing one with a qualifier would be understood at once.
- Treating the statement as ad copy, or filing it and never using it.
- Narrowing the audience so far that most category buyers are excluded from communication.

## Quality checklist
- Does every alternative come from buyer evidence, with a count?
- Is each value theme an outcome for the customer, not a feature?
- Does each value theme have at least one piece of proof labelled FACT?
- Does the sentence fail the substitution test (it is not true of a named competitor)?
- Is the category one that customers already use and understand?
- Was the statement tested with prospects and the result recorded?
- Is the record dated, with a review trigger?

## Output template
```
POSITIONING RECORD - [brand] - [date] - review when: ...
1. COMPETITIVE ALTERNATIVES (FACT): alternative | buyers naming it | source
2. UNIQUE ATTRIBUTES (FACT): ...
3. VALUE THEMES (INTERPRETATION): theme | attributes behind it
4. PROOF (FACT): theme | proof | status: shown / claim to build
5. CUSTOMERS WHO CARE MOST: characteristics ... | share of category buyers ...%
6. MARKET CATEGORY: ... | why this frame: ...
STATEMENT: For ... who ..., [brand] is a ... that ..., unlike ..., because ...
DESK TESTS: substitution pass/fail | proof pass/fail | plain words pass/fail
PROSPECT TEST: n = ... | restated correctly ... (...%) | changes made: ...
```

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Write the statement in the language the team sells in, and check it in both Uzbek and Russian (S3-03); category names often do not translate one to one. Keep customers' own phrases in the original language.
- In the prospect test, polite agreement is likely. Do not ask "is this clear?"; ask the person to say back what the product is and what it is compared with.
- Buyers in Tashkent and in the regions may compare the product with different alternatives. Collect the alternatives separately before assuming one list.
- Proof that depends on documents (warranty, certificates, delivery terms) only works if buyers trust that the document will be honoured. Treat this as a question to ask in interviews, not as a known fact.
- The method was developed mainly for North American business software and advertising. Treat its transfer to local consumer goods as a hypothesis.
