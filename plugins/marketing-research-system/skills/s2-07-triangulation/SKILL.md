# Triangulation (S2-07)

Triangulation means examining the same question from more than one angle and comparing the results. The four classic types are credited to Norman Denzin (The Research Act, 1978) and were taken up by Michael Quinn Patton (1999): method triangulation (two or more ways of collecting data), data source triangulation (different people, places or times), investigator or analyst triangulation (two or more people analysing the same material), and theory triangulation (reading the same data through different explanations). Carter and colleagues (2014) summarise it as using several methods or sources to build a fuller understanding and to test whether information converges. In this agency it is a rule: no finding goes into a strategy on the strength of one method, one source or one analyst.

Evidence grade: B. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. Triangulation is a standard credibility technique in qualitative research and is listed under credibility in Lincoln and Guba's criteria (S2-08). It raises confidence; it does not prove a finding. Sources can agree and still be wrong for the same reason, so weight agreement by how independent the checks really are.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (what each source shows, with its reference) versus INTERPRETATION (whether the sources agree and why). When the triangulation table is delivered, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- A finding will drive positioning, pricing, a product change or a media budget.
- A finding rests on one method (only interviews, only a survey) or was coded by one person.
- A result surprised the team, or pleased the client suspiciously well. Designing a study: plan the second angle before fieldwork, when it is still cheap.

Do not use it when:
- The finding is minor and nothing depends on it. An opened textbook chapter notes triangulation costs time and resources and is sometimes unnecessary.
- The only available "second source" shares the first one's weakness (same respondents, same question, same recruiter). That is repetition, not triangulation.
- The team would use it to pick whichever source agrees with the client. Disagreement must be reported.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. The list of findings, each with its evidence: respondent IDs, quotes, tables (from S4-01, S4-05, S4-10 or similar).
2. Which findings the recommendation depends on.
3. An inventory of other evidence already available: sales and CRM data, complaint logs, desk research (S1-03), observation notes, earlier studies, the client's staff.
4. A second analyst who has not seen the first analyst's conclusions. Time and budget for any extra data collection.

## Procedure
1. Select the findings to check. Ask the user: "Which three to five findings would change the recommendation if they were wrong?" Triangulate those; note the others as single-source.
2. Describe the first leg for each finding: method, who the sources were, who collected and who analysed, and its main weakness (for example: stated reasons, polite answers, small sample, one coder).
3. Choose the second leg so that it does not share that weakness. Options, as defined in the opened sources:
   - Method: add a different way of collecting data, for example observation or behavioural records beside interviews, or a survey beside interviews.
   - Data source: compare people in different roles, different sites, or different times. Korstjens and Moser (2018) describe data triangulation across time, space and person.
   - Investigator: two or more people code and interpret the same material. In the example they report, two researchers analysed the first six interviews independently, compared interpretations and discussed differences.
   - Theory: have people with different perspectives or disciplines interpret the same data, or test the finding against at least two rival explanations (S4-03, S5-05).
   - A University of Florida extension guide adds a fifth, environmental triangulation: repeat at a different place, time of day or season when those could affect the result.
4. Check independence (this skill's working rule, addressing the limit named above). For each pair of legs ask: same respondents? same interviewer or recruiter? same question wording? both based on what people say about themselves? both supplied by the client? If two or more answers are yes, treat the pair as one leg and look for another.
5. Write the prediction before looking: "If the finding is true, the second leg should show ...; if false, it should show ..." (S2-11). This stops the team from reading any result as support.
6. Collect or extract the second-leg evidence. Keep the analyst of the second leg blind to the first conclusion where possible.
7. Compare and classify each finding:
   - CONVERGE: the legs point the same way.
   - COMPLEMENT: the second leg adds a condition or detail without contradicting.
   - DIVERGE: the legs disagree.
   The University of Florida guide reports Patton's position that the goal is not to arrive at consistency: inconsistencies are likely and are a chance to find deeper meaning, not a reason to discard evidence.
8. Explain every divergence. Working questions: Do the legs measure different things (what people say versus what they do, S4-08)? Different people? Different moments? Is one leg weaker? Write the explanation as INTERPRETATION and decide whether more data are needed.
9. Assign a confidence label (working rule): HIGH = two or more independent legs converge and no unexplained divergence; MEDIUM = legs converge but are partly dependent, or complement only; LOW = single leg, or divergence not explained. Only HIGH and MEDIUM findings may carry a recommendation, and MEDIUM ones must state the condition.
10. Report the table with all legs, including those that disagreed. Return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. Input: a flour mill selling to bakeries. Interviews with 12 bakers who stopped ordering produced the finding "bakers leave because quality differs from batch to batch". The mill plans to invest in quality control on this basis.

```
FINDING: lost bakers left because of batch-to-batch inconsistency.
LEG 1 (method: interviews; source: 12 lost bakers; analyst A)
  Analyst A coded "batch inconsistency" in 9 of 12 transcripts.              FACT
  Weakness: stated reasons, one coder, recruited through the mill's sales reps.
PREDICTION: if true, complaint records should show batch complaints before
  customers left, and analyst B should find the theme independently.
LEG 2 (investigator): analyst B, blind, coded the theme in 7 of 12; all 7 are
  among A's 9. Disputed transcripts: 2.                                       FACT
LEG 3 (method + source: behavioural records): of 40 bakeries lost in 12 months,
  14 had a logged batch complaint in the 3 months before the last order = 35%;
  26 had none = 65%.                                                          FACT
INDEPENDENCE: legs 1 and 2 share respondents and recruiter -> count as one leg.
  Leg 3 is independent (records, all lost customers).
CLASSIFICATION: COMPLEMENT. Inconsistency is real for a part of lost bakers;
  records do not support it as the main reason for most.                      INTERPRETATION
  Possible reasons for the gap: complaints go unlogged; interviewees named a
  respectable reason. Next step: ask about price and delivery (S3-06).        INTERPRETATION
CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM. Recommendation limited: fix logging, test before investing.
```
Arithmetic check: 9 - 7 = 2 disputed transcripts. 14 / 40 = 0.35 = 35%. 40 - 14 = 26; 26 / 40 = 0.65 = 65%. 35% + 65% = 100%.

## Common mistakes
- Counting two interviews with similar people as two sources.
- Treating agreement between two self-report methods (interviews and a survey with the same wording) as proof; both can carry the same polite or agreeing answers.
- Dropping or hiding the leg that disagreed. Averaging conflicting results instead of explaining them.
- Looking at the second source first and writing the prediction afterwards. A "second analyst" who has already read the first analyst's summary.
- Triangulating everything, so that nothing is checked properly.

## Quality checklist
- Was each strategy-driving finding checked with at least one additional leg?
- Does the second leg have a different main weakness from the first?
- Was the independence check done and recorded?
- Was a prediction written before the second leg was examined?
- Is every finding classified as converge, complement or diverge? Is each divergence explained or flagged for further data?
- Does the report show all legs, including disagreeing ones, with a confidence label?

## Output template
```
TRIANGULATION TABLE - [project] - [date]
Finding | Depends-on decision | Leg 1 (method, source, analyst, weakness)
        | Prediction if true / if false (written on: date)
        | Leg 2 type (method / source / investigator / theory / environment) and evidence [FACT]
        | Independence check (shared respondents? interviewer? wording? self-report? client-supplied?)
        | Result: CONVERGE / COMPLEMENT / DIVERGE
        | Explanation of any gap [INTERPRETATION]
        | Confidence: HIGH / MEDIUM / LOW | Condition attached to recommendation
Single-source findings not checked: ... Further data needed: ...
```

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Polite and agreeing answers can appear in interviews, groups and surveys alike, so two self-report methods may agree for the same reason. Prefer a behavioural or observational second leg: sales records, receipts, shelf checks, order history.
- If the first leg was collected in Tashkent, a regional leg is a real data-source check; do not assume transfer. A Telegram-bot survey as second leg misses older and rural people; state this next to any convergence it shows.
- Keep quotes and coded extracts in Uzbek or Russian for both analysts. Where possible the second analyst should read the original language, not a translation made by the first.
- Client-supplied records (CRM, complaint logs) may be incomplete; ask how they are filled in before treating them as an independent leg.
- The method's literature comes mostly from Western academic and health research. The logic applies anywhere; which sources are independent in a local market is a judgement to record.
