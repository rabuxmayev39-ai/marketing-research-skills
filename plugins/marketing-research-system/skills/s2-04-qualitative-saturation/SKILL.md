# Qualitative sample size and saturation (S2-04)

Saturation is the point in a qualitative study where further interviews or groups add little or no new information for the study's objectives. For decades it was claimed without evidence; this skill uses the studies that tested it on real data. Greg Guest, Arwen Bunce and Laura Johnson (2006) coded 60 interviews and found that most themes had appeared within the first twelve. Monique Hennink and Bonnie Kaiser (2022) reviewed 23 such tests systematically. Guest, Emily Namey and Mario Chen (2020) proposed a simple way to assess and report saturation with three settings: base size, run length and new information threshold. Hennink, Kaiser and colleagues also separated code saturation (the range of issues has been heard) from meaning saturation (the issues are understood).

Evidence grade: A. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. The numbers below come from peer-reviewed tests, so they are a sound starting range for planning. They are not a guarantee: most tests in the review used fairly homogeneous samples and narrow objectives, the studies opened for this skill were all on health topics, and Guest and colleagues state that meeting a threshold does not prove that later interviews would have added nothing. Use the range to plan and the stopping rule to check, per segment.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (counts of new themes per interview, the settings used) versus INTERPRETATION (the judgement that enough has been heard). Do not turn interview counts into percentages of a market. When the plan or the saturation report is delivered, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- Planning how many in-depth interviews or focus groups to budget for each segment.
- Fieldwork is running and the team must decide whether to stop, continue or add a segment.
- A report needs a defensible statement of why the sample was enough.

Do not use it when:
- The analysis is an in-depth study of a few cases or a narrative or interpretive design; Guest and colleagues say their method is built for applied inductive thematic analysis of narrow questions and is untested elsewhere. Reason with S2-05.
- The goal is to count how common something is. Use S1-08 with S2-03.
- Interviews cannot be coded as they come in. Without ongoing coding there is nothing to measure; plan from the range and S2-05 and say saturation was not assessed.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. Research questions and their breadth (S2-01).
2. The segments that must be understood separately and how similar people are inside each (S2-02, S2-06).
3. The interview or group guide, so the same topics are covered in each session.
4. A way to code each transcript or snapshot soon after the session (S3-11, S4-01) and a running codebook.
5. Budget and time limits, so a provisional maximum can be set.

## Procedure
1. Fix the unit: one homogeneous segment. Working rule: plan and assess saturation separately for each segment whose views are expected to differ (for example buyers and non-buyers). Pooling different segments hides the fact that one of them has barely been heard.
2. Choose which saturation is needed. Code saturation is enough to list the range of issues. Meaning saturation is needed to explain them. In Hennink, Kaiser and Marconi's study of 25 interviews, code saturation came at 9 interviews, while meaning saturation needed 16 to 24, and abstract, conceptual codes took longer than concrete ones.
3. Set the planning range for interviews. Hennink and Kaiser's review found saturation at 9 to 17 interviews in the empirical tests, mostly with homogeneous populations and narrowly defined objectives. Guest, Bunce and Johnson found saturation within the first twelve interviews, with the basic elements of the broad themes present by six. Working rule: budget 12 per homogeneous segment as a default inside that range, with a provisional maximum of 17, and write both in the brief.
4. Adjust upward when the conditions of those tests are not met. The review's outliers needed more: multi-country studies, meta-themes across sites, and meaning saturation. Hennink and colleagues list the parameters to weigh together: study purpose, how diverse the population is, sampling strategy, data quality, type of codes, complexity of the codebook, and the saturation goal. In Guest, Namey and Chen's tests the most heterogeneous dataset needed more interviews and still captured a smaller share of themes.
5. Set the planning range for focus groups. The review found 4 to 8 groups. Hennink, Kaiser and Weber's study of 10 groups found that four identified the range of issues, that one group per stratum was enough to identify issues, that two per stratum gave fuller understanding, and that more than two added little. Working rule: plan two groups per stratum and limit the number of strata (S1-05).
6. Fix the stopping rule before fieldwork, using Guest, Namey and Chen's three settings. Base size: the first interviews whose unique themes form the denominator (they suggest 4). Run length: the number of later interviews checked for new themes (2 or 3). New information threshold: the largest share of new themes accepted (5% or less, or 0%). Longer runs and stricter thresholds are more conservative. State the choice in the plan, not after the result is known.
7. Log during fieldwork. After each interview, code it and record how many themes are new, meaning not present in any earlier interview. Keep themes at one consistent level of detail; the authors tested only single-level codebooks and note that the level chosen is a judgement.
8. Compute after each interview past the base: saturation ratio = new themes in the current run / unique themes in the base. Runs overlap and move forward one interview at a time (5-6, then 6-7, then 7-8 with run length 2). Spreadsheet: =SUM(new_in_run)/base_themes. When the ratio is at or under the threshold, saturation is attributed to the last interview before that run and written as that number "+" the run length, for example 6+2.
9. Check meaning for the themes the decision rests on. For each key theme ask whether the latest interviews added a new dimension, condition or contradiction (S4-07). Hennink's phrasing of the difference is between having heard it all and understanding it all. If key themes are still deepening, continue with targeted recruitment even if the ratio is under the threshold.
10. Decide and record: stop, continue to the provisional maximum, or report that saturation was not reached. Sim and colleagues argue that sample size cannot be fully fixed in advance in inductive work, so present the planned number to the client as provisional.
11. Report the base size, run length, threshold, the result per segment, which type of saturation is claimed, and where it was not reached. A review of published interview studies found saturation was the most common justification and was rarely backed by a described procedure; do not repeat that. Return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. Input: a Tashkent housing developer wants to understand how buyers chose an apartment. Two segments: bought to live in (A) and bought as an investment (B). Narrow question, fairly similar people inside each segment.

```
PLAN  12 interviews per segment = 24; provisional maximum 17 per segment = 34.
Stopping rule set in advance: base 4, run 2, threshold <= 5%, code saturation;
  meaning check on key themes.
SEGMENT A THEME LOG                                                        FACT
Interviews 1-4 (base): 30 unique themes
Interview:      5    6    7    8
New themes:     3    2    1    0
Run 5-6: (3 + 2) / 30 = 5 / 30 = 16.7%   above threshold, continue
Run 6-7: (2 + 1) / 30 = 3 / 30 = 10.0%   above threshold, continue
Run 7-8: (1 + 0) / 30 = 1 / 30 =  3.3%   at or under 5% -> code saturation 6+2
Themes found so far: 30 + 3 + 2 + 1 + 0 = 36
MEANING CHECK
"Ishonch / доверие к застройщику": interviews 7 and 8 each added a new
  condition (who recommended; stage of construction).                     FACT
Decision: range of issues heard in A; trust not yet understood. Do 4 more A
  interviews with recent buyers of unfinished buildings; total A = 12.  INTERPRETATION
Segment B: assessed separately with the same rule.
```
Arithmetic check: 12 x 2 = 24; 17 x 2 = 34; 3 + 2 = 5, 5 / 30 = 0.167; 2 + 1 = 3, 3 / 30 = 0.100; 1 + 0 = 1, 1 / 30 = 0.033; 30 + 6 = 36; 8 done + 4 more = 12.

## Common mistakes
- Writing "saturation was reached" with no rule, no log and no numbers.
- Applying one total (for example 12) to a study with four different segments, three interviews each.
- Choosing the base, run and threshold after seeing which setting gives the wanted answer.
- Stopping on code saturation when the client needs explanation of a complex theme.
- Counting a theme as "not new" by loosening its definition until everything fits.
- Treating the published range as valid for broad questions, mixed populations or several countries.
- Weak interviews: short, agreeable answers saturate quickly because little is said.

## Quality checklist
- Is the sample planned and assessed per homogeneous segment?
- Is the planned number inside or justified against the published ranges, with a provisional maximum?
- Were base size, run length and threshold fixed before fieldwork?
- Is there a theme log showing new themes per interview?
- Is the type of saturation claimed (code or meaning) stated?
- Were key themes checked for new dimensions in the latest interviews?
- Does the report say where saturation was not reached and who was not interviewed?

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Code in the original language, Uzbek or Russian, and keep theme names and quotes in that language; merging translated labels too early makes different ideas look like the same theme.
- Polite or agreeing answers produce thin transcripts that seem to saturate fast. Judge data quality first (S1-04) and treat early saturation with general praise as a warning.
- Tashkent and regions, and Uzbek-speaking and Russian-speaking respondents, may need to be separate segments; decide this from the research question, not by habit.
- Recruitment through referrals and Telegram reaches similar people, which speeds apparent saturation and misses older and rural people. Record how each respondent was found.
- The tests opened for this skill used health topics in the United States and Africa. Using 9 to 17 for marketing topics here is a transfer to treat as a hypothesis and check with the stopping rule.
