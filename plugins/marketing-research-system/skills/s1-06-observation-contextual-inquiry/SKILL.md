# Observation and contextual inquiry (S1-06)

Observation means watching what people do where they normally do it, and recording it systematically. Two sources shape this skill. James Spradley's Participant Observation (1980) comes from ethnography: the researcher spends time in a social situation, starts with wide descriptive observation, then narrows to focused and then selective observation, and records what is seen along nine dimensions (space, actor, activity, object, act, event, time, goal, feeling). Karen Holtzblatt and Hugh Beyer's Contextual Design adds contextual inquiry, a shorter field interview developed for product design: the researcher watches one person do their real work and asks about it as it happens, guided by four principles (context, partnership, interpretation, focus) and a master and apprentice relationship in which the person observed is the expert. In marketing research the two are used to study shopping, selling, cooking, ordering, installing and using.

Evidence grade: B. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. Both are established professional methods with long use in anthropology and design. They give direct evidence of behaviour in a few settings. They are slow, cover few cases, and the observer's presence can change what people do. Put weight on what was seen and written down at the time; treat explanations and any generalisation as interpretation.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (what was seen or said, with place, time and observer) versus INTERPRETATION (what it means, why it happened). The assistant helps plan, prepare sheets and analyse notes; people observe. When the plan or the field report is delivered, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- People cannot describe what they do accurately because it is habitual or detailed. The designers of contextual inquiry built it because surveys and interviews depend on recall and summary and miss steps and reasons.
- The decision depends on the real setting: shelf, counter, kitchen, workshop, phone screen, delivery.
- You are early in a project and do not yet know what to ask; observation shows which questions are relevant.
- You suspect a gap between what people say and what they do (S1-23, S4-08).

Do not use it when:
- The behaviour is rare, private or spread over months; you will not be there when it happens. Use S1-04, S1-16 or S1-17.
- The question is about attitudes, images or reasons for a past decision.
- Numbers for a market are needed (a few sites are not a sample), or time is very short. An open textbook lists heavy time and resource needs and access through gatekeepers as the main obstacles.
- The task is simple and narrow; Nielsen Norman Group advises that contextual inquiry suits complex processes and experienced users, not small targeted tasks.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. The decision, research questions and a one-sentence focus for the observation.
2. A list of settings and people, and who controls access (owner, manager, family head).
3. Permission and consent plan: who is told, what is recorded, photos or not (S2-10).
4. Observers, preferably two for the first visits, with an observation sheet, and time for visits, same-day write-up and analysis.

## Procedure
1. Check fit. Ask the user: "Can we be present when this happens, and would people let us watch?" and "Do we need what they do, or what they think?" If the behaviour cannot be witnessed, switch to S1-04 or S1-17.
2. Choose the form. Plain observation (watch, little or no interaction): for public settings such as shops, markets and queues, or when people cannot be interrupted. Participant observation (join in, over repeated visits): for understanding a setting's routines and rules from inside. Contextual inquiry (watch one person work and ask as they go): for a process, tool or job done by one person at a time, such as ordering, baking or serving a customer.
3. Decide how open to be. Overt observation, where everyone knows, has the lowest ethical risk but people may adjust their behaviour. Covert observation shows natural behaviour but carries the highest ethical risk and needs strong justification. Working rule for this agency: be overt in private and business settings; in public commercial settings observe only public behaviour and record nothing that identifies a person.
4. Write the focus. Holtzblatt and Beyer's principle: know what you are there to learn so it guides what you notice, and stay aware of your own assumptions. Write down what you expect to see before the first visit (S2-11).
5. Choose sites and times. Vary what could matter: busy and quiet hours, weekdays and weekends, different types of setting. Course material based on Contextual Design suggests 15 to 20 users across about five sites for a full design project and adds that any number is better than none. Working rule for small marketing projects: begin with four to six sessions, then review.
6. Prepare the observation sheet with Spradley's nine dimensions as headings: space (layout), actors (who is there), activities, objects, acts (single actions), events, time (sequence and duration), goals, feelings shown. Add mapping, counting and listening: sketch the place, count people and occurrences, write down talk as close to verbatim as possible.
7. Observe in three passes (Spradley). First descriptive: record everything broadly, "what is going on here?". Then focused: concentrate on the parts tied to the research focus. Then selective: look for specific contrasts and details. Keep some descriptive observation going to the end.
8. If running contextual inquiry, use four parts (Nielsen Norman Group's description). Primer: build rapport, explain the purpose and confidentiality, get consent, ask for an overview of the work. Transition: say clearly that you will now watch while they work and will interrupt with questions. Contextual interview: watch, stop to ask about anything unclear, and offer your interpretation for correction; do not do this too often, since it can change how they work. Wrap-up: summarise what you understood and let the person correct it. Sessions run from an hour or two upward.
9. Hold the apprentice stance. The person shows; you learn. Do not act as questioner, teacher or guest. Do not criticise or correct. When the person starts to summarise ("usually we..."), ask them to show the last real case. Ask them beforehand not to tidy up, and to keep real orders, lists and papers to hand.
10. Write field notes. Short notes during the visit, written unobtrusively; full notes the same day before details fade: what happened in order, verbatim phrases, counts, a map, and artefacts collected or photographed with permission. Keep observer comments and feelings in a separate column or reflexive diary. Note where your presence seemed to change behaviour.
11. Analyse and report. Compare across sites: sequences, workarounds, breakdowns, things people do but did not mention, and things they said but did not do. Check against what you expected to see. Take notes into S5-01 or S4-01, and say-do gaps into S4-08. Report observations as FACT with site and time, readings as INTERPRETATION, and describe spread in words. Return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. Input: a flour mill wants to know how small bakeries actually handle and judge flour, because owners in interviews say only "quality matters". Baking is habitual, detailed work, so contextual inquiry fits.

```
PLAN
Focus: how flour is received, stored, tested and used in one working shift.
Sites: 6 bakeries (3 Tashkent, 3 regional towns), 1 baker each, overt, owner's consent.
Session: 3 hours during a real shift = 18 hours of observation in total.
  Primer 20 min | transition 5 | contextual interview 140 | wrap-up 15 = 180 min.
Expected beforehand: bakers choose by price and brand name on the sack.
FIELD NOTE EXTRACT - site B4, regional, 05:10-08:10, observer N.
Space    | sacks stacked by the oven wall; no pallet                         FACT
Objects  | two brands of flour open at once; unmarked scoop                  FACT
Acts     | baker rubs flour between fingers, then mixes a fist-sized test    FACT
         | dough before the main batch; adds water "ko'z bilan"              
Time     | test dough takes about 6 minutes; done for every new sack         FACT
Said     | "Har qop har xil chiqadi" (while testing)                         FACT
Observer | he blends two brands; not mentioned in the primer.                INTERP
Presence | slowed down and explained more in the first 30 minutes.           FACT
ACROSS SITES (reading)
5 of 6 bakers made a test dough for each new sack; 4 of 6 blended brands.    FACT
Bakers manage variation between sacks themselves; consistency between
  batches may matter more than the brand name. Expectation not supported.    INTERP
Next: S1-04 interviews on blending; S1-08 to measure how common it is.
```
Arithmetic check: 6 sites x 3 hours = 18 hours; 20 + 5 + 140 + 15 = 180 minutes = 3 hours.

## Common mistakes
- Sitting in an office and asking people to describe their work (that is an interview, not observation), or letting the session turn into a list of complaints or a summary of "what we usually do".
- Arriving without a focus and recording everything, or arriving with a fixed checklist and missing surprises.
- Writing interpretations ("she was confused") in place of what was seen ("she picked up three packs and put all back").
- Writing notes days later; observing one site at one hour and generalising.
- Correcting, advising or selling during the visit; ignoring the effect of the observer, or observing covertly in a private setting without consent.

## Quality checklist
- Can the behaviour actually be witnessed in the planned visits?
- Is there a written focus and a written expectation before fieldwork?
- Do sites and times vary on what could matter, and were consent and the level of openness decided in advance?
- Do notes cover the nine dimensions and separate what was seen from the observer's comments?
- Were full notes written the same day?
- In contextual inquiry, were interpretations checked with the person in the wrap-up?
- Does the report mark observer effects and avoid generalising from few sites?

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Write notes in the language you hear, Uzbek or Russian, and keep verbatim phrases in the original; add translations only in the report.
- A visitor is a guest. Hospitality can stop normal work and polite hosts may show the "proper" way of doing things. Explain that you want to see an ordinary day, allow extra time before real work resumes, and note what happened before and after the tea.
- Get permission from the person who actually decides (owner, family elder), not only from the person observed, and agree whether photos are acceptable. Bazaars, small shops and workshops in the regions may work very differently from Tashkent supermarkets and offices; include both if the decision covers both.
- Match the observer to the setting where it matters (for example a woman observer for a home kitchen) and record who observed. Spradley's and Holtzblatt and Beyer's methods were developed in US settings. The procedure transfers; any expectation about how people behave when watched here is a hypothesis.
