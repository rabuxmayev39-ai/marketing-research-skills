# SWOT, use with caution (S0-08)

SWOT sorts what is known about a business into four boxes: strengths and weaknesses (inside the company, judged against competitors) and opportunities and threats (outside it). Historical research by Puyt, Lie and Wilderom traces it to Robert Franklin Stewart's planning group at the Stanford Research Institute, which published a method called SOFT in 1965; the label became SWOT about 1967. In that original process each manager listed 8 to 10 key issues for their unit, backed each with evidence, sorted them into four classes (keep what is satisfactory, open opportunities, fix faults, counter threats), and discussed them in groups before proposals went to a planning committee. What most teams do today, a brainstorm onto four boxes, keeps the boxes and drops the evidence and the follow-through.

Evidence grade: C. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. SWOT is everywhere, but the best-known empirical look at its use is negative. Hill and Westbrook (1997) reviewed 50 companies taking part in a British government manufacturing-planning scheme; more than 20 had used a SWOT, run with 14 consulting firms. According to the article's abstract, the lists were long (over 40 factors on average), the wording was vague, nothing was prioritised or verified, and in no case was the output used in the later stages of the strategy work. The authors concluded that its continued use should be questioned. That is one study of one setting, so it does not prove SWOT can never help, but no study showing that SWOT improves decisions was found among the sources opened. This skill therefore allows SWOT only as a summary of analysis already done, and never as the main method.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT versus INTERPRETATION; every item on the slide must point to its evidence. If the user asks for "a SWOT" as the analysis itself, explain the limit politely and propose S0-07 plus a diagnostic method, then offer the summary slide afterwards. When done, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- Real analysis exists (S0-07, S0-06, S0-10 or research findings) and a one-slide recap is needed.
- A client, bank, tender or grant form demands a SWOT by name.
- A team wants to check that a finished diagnosis has not ignored an outside threat.

Do not use it when:
- It would be the first or only analysis. An empty SWOT filled in a meeting produces the vague lists the 1997 study describes.
- A choice between strategies is needed. SWOT does not rank or choose; use S5-05 or S6-08.
- Items cannot be tied to evidence. Then the honest output is a list of unknowns (S0-13).

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. The decision and the unit of analysis: one brand or line in one market.
2. Finished analysis with sources: situation analysis (S0-07), context scan (S0-09), competitor and customer facts.
3. Named competitors, because a strength exists only in comparison with someone.
4. The diagnosis or strategy the slide is meant to summarise, if one exists.

## Procedure
1. Gate check. Ask: which analysis is this SWOT summarising? If the answer is "none", stop and route to S0-07 and S1-03. Working rule: no evidence file, no SWOT.
2. Collect candidate items from the existing analysis only. If the team insists on a brainstorm, keep its output as a raw list and treat every line as an assumption until checked.
3. Apply the evidence test to each candidate. Keep it only if it (a) names something specific, (b) has a number or a dated source, and (c) for strengths and weaknesses, is stated against a named competitor or a customer requirement. This answers the faults reported by Hill and Westbrook, vague wording and no verification. "Good quality" fails; "9 of 10 deliveries within 24 hours against two days for rival X" passes.
4. Sort correctly. Inside the company and under its control: strength or weakness. Outside and not under its control: opportunity or threat. A plan ("open a branch") is not an opportunity; the opportunity is the outside condition that makes the plan worthwhile.
5. Prioritise. Working rule: score each surviving item for impact on the decision (1 to 3) and certainty of the evidence (1 to 3), multiply, and keep at most two or three per box, eight to twelve in all. The cap echoes the 8 to 10 issues per manager in the original SOFT process and counters the 40-item lists in the 1997 study.
6. Add "so what" to every kept item: one line on what it implies for the decision. An item with no implication is removed.
7. Link to action, since unused output was the gravest fault found. For each kept item name where it goes next: a hypothesis to test, a choice in the strategy (S6-08), a risk to monitor, or a research gap. Working rule: pair items across boxes (which strength meets which opportunity; which weakness is exposed to which threat) and write two to four such pairs as INTERPRETATION.
8. Label the slide "summary of analysis, not an analysis", list the sources under it, and return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. Input: a housing developer in Tashkent. A management workshop produced 37 SWOT lines. The agency already has a 5C file and a sales-funnel analysis.

```
Raw list: 37 lines = 12 S + 7 W + 11 O + 7 T (e.g. "strong team", "good location")
Evidence test: 23 lines removed (vague or no evidence) -> 14 left = 4 S + 4 W + 3 O + 3 T
Priority (impact x certainty): top 2 per box kept -> 8 lines on the slide

S1 10 of last 12 buildings handed over on or before the contract date (83%);
   rival X: delays reported in buyer chats (S1-14 file)        score 3 x 3 = 9  FACT
   So what: on-time handover is provable -> candidate proof point (S6-02)
W1 18 contracts from 300 site visits in Q3 = 6% visit-to-contract;
   own target was 10%                                           score 3 x 3 = 9  FACT
   So what: the loss is after the visit -> study the visit (S1-13)
O1 Buyers in 14 interviews asked for instalment plans of 36+ months;
   2 of 5 rivals offer them                                     score 3 x 2 = 6  FACT + INTERPRETATION
T1 A rival announced a project 1 km away with 400 flats         score 2 x 3 = 6  FACT
PAIR (INTERPRETATION): S1 + O1 -> test "on-time handover + longer instalments"
  as an offer; W1 + T1 -> fix the site visit before the rival opens sales.
Removed examples: "strong brand" (no measure), "growing market" (no source).
```
Arithmetic check: 12 + 7 + 11 + 7 = 37; 37 - 23 = 14; 4 + 4 + 3 + 3 = 14; 2 x 4 boxes = 8; 14 - 8 = 6 lines kept in the appendix; 10 / 12 = 0.833; 18 / 300 = 0.06; 3 x 3 = 9; 3 x 2 = 6; 2 x 3 = 6; 2 / 5 = 0.40.

## Common mistakes
- Starting a project with a SWOT workshop and calling the result an analysis.
- Long lists with no order of importance.
- Items nobody could check or disagree with: "experienced team", "high competition".
- Strengths judged against nothing, when rivals have the same.
- Putting the company's own plans in the opportunities box.
- The same fact in two boxes with no explanation.
- A finished slide that feeds no decision, test or monitoring.

## Quality checklist
- Does the slide name the analysis it summarises, with sources?
- Does every item carry a number or a dated source?
- Is every strength or weakness stated against a named competitor or customer requirement?
- Are opportunities and threats outside conditions, not plans?
- Are there twelve items or fewer, in order of priority?
- Does each item have a "so what" and a next step?
- Is the slide labelled as a summary and not as the analysis?

## Output template
```
SWOT SUMMARY - [brand / line] - [market] - [date]
Summary of: [S0-07 file, research report ...] | not an analysis in itself
Decision this serves: ...
STRENGTHS (inside, vs [competitor])   item | evidence, source, date | score | so what | next step
WEAKNESSES (inside, vs [competitor])  ...
OPPORTUNITIES (outside)               ...
THREATS (outside)                     ...
PAIRS (INTERPRETATION): S.. + O.. -> ... | W.. + T.. -> ...
Removed for lack of evidence: ... lines (kept in appendix as assumptions)
```

## Works with
- S0-07 3C and 5C situation analysis, S0-09 PESTEL, S0-10 five forces, S0-06 funnel diagnosis: the real analysis that must come first and that fills the boxes.
- S1-03 desk research first and S0-12 client and stakeholder interviews: where to go when the gate check fails. S0-13 facts, assumptions, unknowns register: home for the removed lines.
- S0-01 strategy kernel and S0-03 issue tree: diagnosis, which SWOT cannot do. S5-05 what would have to be true and S6-08 strategy choice cascade: choosing.
- S2-07 triangulation: checking a key item against a second source. S5-13 pyramid storyline: placing the slide in a report.

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Clients, banks and tender documents may ask for a SWOT by name. Deliver the short evidence-backed version and say plainly what it rests on; do not refuse the format.
- In a workshop with the owner present, staff may list strengths readily and stay quiet on weaknesses. Practical caution: collect weaknesses in writing beforehand or one to one (S0-12).
- Keep items in the language they were collected in, Uzbek or Russian, and translate the final slide (S3-03).
- Do not write market "facts" into opportunities or threats from memory. Each needs a current source; legal or regulatory items go to S0-09 and, where they matter, to a lawyer.
- The 1997 evidence comes from firms in a British manufacturing scheme; that the same faults appear here is a reasonable hypothesis, not a verified fact.
