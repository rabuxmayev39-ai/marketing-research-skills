# Needs-based segmentation and target selection (S5-09)

Needs-based segmentation groups buyers by what they need from a category and how they behave in it, instead of by age, income or personality type. Daniel Yankelovich introduced non-demographic segmentation in Harvard Business Review in 1964; in 2006 he and David Meer argued in "Rediscovering Market Segmentation" that the practice had drifted into producing lifestyle characters for advertising that say little about what people will buy. Their remedy: tie each segmentation to a specific business decision, base it on actual and likely buying behaviour, keep it understandable for senior management, and redo it when the market changes. They also proposed a "gravity of decision spectrum", a tool for judging how much importance buyers place on the product or category. Evidence grade: B. This is an established professional standard described by experienced practitioners, not a tested theory. There is also a serious counter-finding: research from the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute reports that competing brands in a category sell to very similar kinds of buyers and share customers roughly in line with brand size. So put weight on segmentation for deciding what to offer and how to serve different needs, and much less weight on it as a reason to restrict reach to a narrow group.

Working rules for the assistant:
- Answer in the language the user writes in (Uzbek, Russian or English).
- Label every statement as FACT (observed in data) or INTERPRETATION (your inference).

## When to use it / When not to use it

Use it when:
- A concrete decision depends on buyer differences: which product version to make, which channel to open, how to price tiers, which accounts sales should visit first.
- Research shows buyers want clearly different things from the same category.
- The team has, or can collect, data on needs and behaviour (not only demographics).

Do not use it when:
- No decision is attached ("we just want to know our segments"). Ask for the decision first.
- The purpose is to choose a narrow advertising audience for a mass-market brand. The Ehrenberg-Bass evidence points towards reaching all category buyers; see S6-03.
- The only data are demographics or the team's opinions. The output would be invented types.
- The sample is too small to separate groups. With a handful of interviews, report candidate needs, not segments.

## What you need before starting (inputs)

- The business decision the segmentation must inform, and who will make it.
- Category definition: what buyers choose between.
- Qualitative data on needs: interviews (S1-07, S1-15), observation, sales team knowledge.
- Behavioural data if available: purchase frequency, basket, channel, brands used, switching.
- Profit or revenue data by customer type if available.
- For quantitative segmentation: a survey with need statements and behaviour questions, and enough respondents to analyse.

## Procedure

1. Fix the decision. Ask: "Which decision will change depending on what we find?" Write it down. Yankelovich and Meer's first requirement is that segmentation serves a specific strategic decision. If two different decisions exist (for example product range and sales coverage), they may need two different segmentations.

2. Judge how weighty the purchase is for buyers. Ask how much importance buyers give this product or category: a routine pick, a considered choice, or a major commitment. This is a simplified use of the gravity-of-decision idea. Decision rule: for routine purchases, base segments mainly on behaviour (what, where, how often, price sensitivity); for weightier purchases, add needs, concerns and decision process. State that this three-way split is the agency's working simplification.

3. Identify who drives profit. With the client's data, find which customers bring most revenue or margin and what distinguishes them in behaviour. If no data exist, record the sales team's view as an assumption to verify.

4. List candidate segmentation bases from the qualitative data. Extract needs, desired outcomes, usage situations and barriers. Write each as a statement in the buyer's words. Sort into: needs (what they want from the category), behaviour (what they do), and descriptors (who they are, where they are). Rule: needs and behaviour define segments; descriptors only help find and reach them afterwards.

5. Form the segments.
   - Qualitative route (interviews only): group respondents who share the same main need and behaviour pattern. Produce three to six candidate segments and label them "hypothetical". Do not give sizes.
   - Quantitative route (survey): run cluster analysis (S4-11) or latent class segmentation (S4-12) on need and behaviour variables. Compare solutions with different numbers of segments. Choose the one that is stable, readable and useful for the decision.
   Name each segment by its need, not by a cute character name.

6. Profile each segment. For each write: the defining need, typical behaviour, current solutions and brands, barriers, descriptors, and size and value if measured. Mark each line FACT or INTERPRETATION.

7. Test the segments against five questions drawn from the 2006 article's advice:
   - Does the segmentation bear on the decision from step 1?
   - Does it separate more profitable from less profitable customers?
   - Is it based on actual or likely buying behaviour, not on personality?
   - Can senior management understand it and recognise the segments?
   - Can it be updated when the market changes?
   Add two practical checks: can each segment be found and reached, and would the company do something different for it? If a segment fails the last check, merge it.

8. Run the similar-buyers check. Before recommending a narrow target, compare who actually buys the client's brand with who buys competitors, if data exist. The Ehrenberg-Bass finding is that user profiles of rival brands seldom differ and that brands share buyers with others in proportion to size. Decision rules:
   - If the segments differ in what they need (different product, service or price), use them to design offers.
   - If they differ only in description and all buy the same things, do not narrow reach; treat the category's buyers as one market for communication.
   - Never recommend excluding buyers from reach only because they are outside the "ideal" segment, unless serving them is unprofitable or impossible.

9. Select targets. Score each segment on: size and value, fit with what the company can deliver, how well current options serve it, cost to reach, and risk. Present the scores and reasoning. Separate two decisions: (a) which segments to design offers for; (b) how broad communication reach should be. They can have different answers.

10. State what changes per target. For each chosen segment list the concrete differences in product, price, channel, service and message. If nothing differs, the segment is not needed.

11. Set a review trigger. Name the events that should prompt a redo (new competitor, price shock, new channel, a set period). The 2006 article stresses that buyer attitudes and behaviour shift and segments must be redrawn.

12. Report with the output template and return to "research-orchestrator".

## Worked example

INVENTED EXAMPLE for illustration. No real data.

Input: a flour mill selling to bakeries must decide which products and service terms to offer and where its three sales agents should go first. Data: 24 interviews with bakery owners in Tashkent and two regions, plus the mill's sales records.

Weight of purchase: considered, repeated purchase with real consequences for the buyer's own product.

Candidate segments (qualitative, hypothetical, no sizes):

| Segment (named by need) | Defining need | Behaviour (FACT from interviews and records) | What would differ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Consistency first | Same dough result every batch | Buys one grade, weekly, rarely switches | Batch quality certificate, fixed grade |
| Cash-flow first | Pay after selling the bread | Small frequent orders, switches for deferred payment | 7-day payment terms, small bags |
| Range builders | Flour for new product types | Buys several grades, asks for advice | Specialty grades, technologist visit |

Test: all three bear on the product and terms decision; sales records show "consistency first" buyers have the highest repeat rate (FACT); management recognised the groups (FACT, review meeting). Similar-buyers check: the same bakeries also buy from two competing mills (FACT, 15 of 24). INTERPRETATION: segments are useful for designing terms and products; they are not grounds for limiting which bakeries the agents and advertising reach.

Target decision: design offers for "consistency first" and "cash-flow first"; keep sales coverage broad across all bakeries in the delivery area. Next step: survey to size the segments (S4-11).

## Common mistakes

- Segmenting by demographics or personality and calling it needs-based.
- Building segments with no decision attached.
- Giving percentage sizes from qualitative interviews.
- Creating more segments than the company can treat differently.
- Naming segments with character names that hide the need.
- Using the segmentation to cut reach to a narrow audience without checking whether buyers of rival brands actually differ.
- Mixing the definition of a segment (needs, behaviour) with its description (age, region).
- Treating the segmentation as permanent.

## Quality checklist

- Is one specific business decision stated at the top? (yes/no)
- Are segments defined by needs and behaviour, with demographics only as descriptors? (yes/no)
- Is every segment profile backed by cited data, with facts and interpretations labelled? (yes/no)
- Are sizes given only where a quantitative sample supports them? (yes/no)
- Would the company do something different for each segment? (yes/no)
- Was the similar-buyers check done or explicitly marked as not possible? (yes/no)
- Are "offer design" targets and "communication reach" decided separately? (yes/no)
- Is a review trigger set? (yes/no)

## Notes for Uzbekistan

- Write need statements in the buyers' own words in Uzbek or Russian, and keep the original wording in the segment profiles.
- Polite, agreeing answers inflate agreement with need statements in surveys. Prefer forced choices, rankings and reported behaviour over agree/disagree scales.
- Tashkent and the regions can differ in channels, prices and available alternatives. Check whether "region" is a real need difference or only a descriptor before making it a segment.
- For business buyers, check who decides and who pays; the segment unit may be the business, not the person.
- The source articles and the Ehrenberg-Bass findings rest mainly on Western market data. Treat both the segmentation advice and the similar-buyers pattern as hypotheses to check against local data.
