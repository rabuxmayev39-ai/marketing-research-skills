# Facts, assumptions, unknowns register (S0-13)

The register is a running table that sorts every statement about the client's market, customers and business into three kinds: facts (supported by a source someone can show), assumptions (believed, but not adequately supported) and unknowns (questions nobody on the team can answer). Assumptions are then ranked by two things: how much the decision depends on them and how weak the evidence for them is. The important and weakly supported ones define what the research has to cover. The ranking step comes from assumptions mapping, a team exercise developed by David J. Bland and published in Bland and Osterwalder's book Testing Business Ideas (2019); Strategyzer, the authors' company, describes it as adapted from Lean UX and design thinking practice. The three-way split into facts, assumptions and unknowns and the use of the map as a standing register for marketing projects are this skill's own adaptation (Working rule).

Evidence grade: C. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. Assumptions mapping is a workshop tool from product development practice. No study testing whether teams that use it make better decisions was found among the sources opened for this skill; the pages opened are by the method's author and his company or report his teaching, so they are not independent. Its limits: placing a note on the map is a group judgement and can follow the most senior voice (the Strategyzer page itself warns that position should rest on observable evidence, not on who is loudest or best paid); the tool was designed for new business ideas, not for diagnosing an existing brand; and the register goes stale unless it is updated at every stage. Use it to organise what the team knows and to argue for research priorities; a high position on the map shows what to test first and proves nothing by itself.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT versus INTERPRETATION in every row; in this register an assumption is an interpretation waiting for evidence. Never move a row to "fact" without a source and date. When the register and the research priorities are written, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- A project starts and the brief mixes data, beliefs and guesses in one text.
- The team must decide what the research should cover and what can be left out.
- A stage ends (desk research, interviews, survey) and findings must be booked against earlier beliefs.
- The client and the agency disagree and nobody can say on what evidence.

Do not use it when:
- A single fact can be checked in five minutes; check it instead of mapping it.
- The team needs the logical structure of the problem; use S0-03 and attach this register to it.
- It would replace fieldwork. The register shows gaps; it does not fill them.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. The brief, client messages and notes from client interviews (S0-12).
2. Desk research found so far, with sources and dates (S1-03).
3. The decision the project serves (S0-02).
4. The people who hold the beliefs: client decision-maker, sales, marketing, the agency team. Bland advises a mixed team so that customer, business and delivery knowledge are all present.
5. A shared table (spreadsheet or board) that will stay alive through the whole project.

## Procedure
1. Collect statements. Go through the brief, interview notes and data, and write each claim as one short sentence per row. The sources insist on one statement per note with no bullet lists inside it, because compound statements cannot be ranked.
2. Add what is missing by asking the question the Strategyzer page uses: what must be true for this plan to work? The sources group the answers under desirability (do customers want it), feasibility (can the company deliver it) and viability (does it make enough money); the Strategyzer page adds adaptability (can it survive change). Working rule for marketing projects: label rows customer, company, money and environment.
3. Classify each row (Working rule). FACT: a source someone can show, with a date, relevant to this market and segment. ASSUMPTION: believed by someone, but resting on opinion, memory, one case, an old study or another market. UNKNOWN: a question with no proposed answer. When in doubt, choose assumption.
4. Rewrite each assumption as a testable belief. Strategyzer suggests starting with "We believe that..." and making the statement precise and separate from others; the wording also reminds the team that it may be wrong. Record who holds the belief.
5. Rate importance. The test in the source: if this were proved wrong, would the idea fail? For a marketing project ask: would the decision change? Working rule: score 3 = the decision would change, 2 = the plan would be adjusted, 1 = no real effect.
6. Rate evidence. The source's axis runs from "have evidence" to "no evidence", and the evidence may be qualitative or quantitative. Working rule: score 3 = none or opinion only, 2 = weak (one case, old, another market, stated intention), 1 = solid behavioural data for this market.
7. Rank (Working rule). Priority = importance x evidence score, range 1 to 9. Sort from high to low. Compare neighbours, as the source advises: is this one more important than that one, and does it have less evidence?
8. Decide what happens to each group. The published guidance is to test first the beliefs that are important and lack evidence. Bland's teaching, as reported by Mural, names the other areas too: important and known ones go into the plan; unimportant unknown ones are for open exploration; unimportant known ones are deferred, and he warns that work there only looks like progress. Working rule: priority 6 or 9 goes into the research brief; 3 or 4 is checked by desk research or noted; 1 or 2 is parked.
9. Convert unknowns. For each unknown that matters, either turn it into an assumption (someone states an expected answer, recorded under S2-11) or into an exploratory research question (S1-01).
10. Assign a method and an owner to each priority row (S2-01): desk research, interviews, survey, test. State what evidence would move the row to fact or refute it.
11. Update at every stage gate. After each stage change the status of rows (confirmed, refuted, still open), add the source, and add the new assumptions the findings created. Keep refuted rows visible; do not delete them. Return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. A juice brand wants to launch a 200 ml carton for schoolchildren. Twelve statements were collected from the brief and two client interviews.

```
REGISTER SUMMARY: 4 facts + 6 assumptions + 2 unknowns = 12 rows
FACTS (source shown)
  F1 200 ml cartons were 18% of our litres in 2025 (sales ledger)
  F2 Filling line has 30% free capacity (production report, May)
  F3 Two rival brands sell 200 ml packs in chains (store check, 14 May)
  F4 Our price per carton to distributors: 3,200 soum (price list)
ASSUMPTIONS ("We believe that...")            Imp  Evid  Priority
  A1 mothers choose the lunchbox drink          3    3      9
  A2 "no added sugar" is the main reason        3    3      9
     mothers would pick our carton
  A3 small shops near schools will stock it     3    2      6
  A4 a price of 4,500 soum is acceptable        2    3      6
  A5 children ask for the brand by name         2    2      4
  A6 our current design is recognised           1    2      2
UNKNOWNS
  U1 What do children drink at school now?
  U2 Are there school rules on drinks sold nearby?
DECISION: research brief covers A1, A2, A3, A4 (priority 6 or more);
  A5 noted; A6 parked. U1 -> observation and interviews (S1-06, S1-04);
  U2 -> desk research (S1-03).
```
Arithmetic check: 4 + 6 + 2 = 12; A1 3 x 3 = 9; A2 3 x 3 = 9; A3 3 x 2 = 6; A4 2 x 3 = 6; A5 2 x 2 = 4; A6 1 x 2 = 2; rows with priority 6 or more: A1, A2, A3, A4 = 4 of 6.

## Common mistakes
- Recording the client's confident statements as facts because of who said them.
- Writing compound or vague rows that cannot be tested ("customers like quality").
- Rating everything as important, so nothing is ranked.
- Counting stated intentions or compliments as solid evidence (S1-23).
- Building the register once at kick-off and never opening it again.
- Deleting refuted assumptions, which hides what the research changed.
- Spending research time on unimportant rows because they are easy to check.
- Treating the priority score as a measurement instead of a sorting aid.

## Quality checklist
- Does every fact have a source and a date for this market?
- Is each assumption one testable sentence with a named holder?
- Do importance scores refer to a stated decision?
- Did people with different roles take part in the rating?
- Does every row with priority 6 or 9 have a method and an owner?
- Is each important unknown turned into an expectation or a research question?
- Was the register updated at the last stage gate, with refuted rows kept?

## Output template
```
FACTS, ASSUMPTIONS, UNKNOWNS REGISTER - [client / project] - version [n], [date]
Decision this serves: ...
ID | Statement (one sentence) | Type F/A/U | Area: customer/company/money/environment
   | Source and date, or who believes it | Importance 1-3 | Evidence 1-3 (3 = none)
   | Priority = Imp x Evid | Method to test | Owner | Status: open/confirmed/refuted | Updated
RESEARCH MUST COVER (priority 6-9): ...
CHECK BY DESK RESEARCH OR NOTE (3-4): ...
PARKED (1-2): ...
UNKNOWNS -> expectation (S2-11) or exploratory question: ...
Changes since last version: confirmed ... | refuted ... | new ...
```

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Practical caution: in owner-led firms the owner's beliefs may be treated as facts by staff. Ask for the source of each statement without challenging the person.
- Polite agreement in a rating session can hide doubt. Let people score rows separately first, then compare.
- A study from another country, or from Tashkent only, counts as weak evidence (score 2) for the regions until checked locally. Published local statistics may be scarce for a category; record that as an unknown.
- Keep rows in the language they were said in, Uzbek or Russian, with a translation column (S3-03).
- Results from Telegram-bot surveys miss older and rural people; note that limit in the evidence column.
