# Strategy choice cascade (S6-08)

The strategy choice cascade is a way of writing a strategy as five linked choices: a winning aspiration (what winning means for this organisation), where to play (which customers, places, products and channels), how to win (why customers there will choose us over rivals), must-have capabilities (what we must be able to do to deliver that), and enabling management systems (the routines, measures and incentives that build and keep those capabilities). It was set out by A. G. Lafley, former chief executive of Procter and Gamble, and Roger Martin, former dean of the Rotman School of Management, in the book Playing to Win (2013). Martin has since explained and corrected common readings of it in a long series of free articles, which this skill draws on.

Evidence grade: B. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. The cascade is a widely used professional framework built from its authors' experience, not a finding from controlled research. No study comparing firms that use it with firms that do not was found among the sources opened for this skill. Its known limits: it organises choices but does not produce the facts behind them, so it needs a diagnosis first (S0-01); and it is often misused as a form to fill in box by box, which Martin himself calls a serious error. Critics have called it a top-down "waterfall"; Martin's reply is that the choices are revised back and forth, which is a design intention and not evidence. Use it to force clear choices and to check their fit; do not present the output as proven to work.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (market data, current capabilities) versus INTERPRETATION (the choices themselves and why they should work). Never lock one box before the others have been drafted. When the cascade is written and tested, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- A diagnosis exists and the team must turn it into a strategy for a company, a business line or a brand.
- The current "strategy" is a list of goals, initiatives or a slogan, and nobody can say what was decided against.
- Two or more directions are on the table and need to be compared as whole packages.

Do not use it when:
- The problem is not yet understood. Go to S0-01 and S0-03 first.
- The task is a campaign or a communication plan. Use S9-07; the cascade sits above it.
- The decision-makers will not take part. Choices written by an agency alone are not choices anyone has made.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. The diagnosis: what the main challenge is and the evidence for it (S0-01, S0-07).
2. Facts on customers, competitors and the company's costs and strengths (S0-13 separates facts from assumptions).
3. Size of the candidate markets (S4-23) and segments (S5-09).
4. The people who can decide, and two to three hours with them.
5. The current stated strategy, if any, to see what it leaves unchosen.

## Procedure
1. Talk about context without fixing anything. Following Martin's advice, discuss what the owners value, the company's history and how it wants to treat customers and staff. Do not hold a session to "agree the aspiration" first: he argues that locking the first box narrows the search so much that a good way to win is unlikely to be found inside it.
2. Draft two or three possibilities, each starting from a where-to-play and how-to-win pair. Martin calls this pair the heart of strategy and insists the two are chosen together: no market is attractive in itself, only with a workable way to win in it. For every where-to-play idea ask at once "how would we win there?" If there is no answer, adjust the arena or drop it. Avoid long lists of markets with no way to win attached.
3. Write the how-to-win as a reason customers choose us, not as a list of actions. Apply Martin's opposite test: if the opposite of the statement is plainly foolish ("we will offer poor quality"), the statement is not a choice. A real how-to-win explains why the firm will be better than rivals in that arena at something customers act on. S6-10 gives the vocabulary (lower cost, differentiation, and so on).
4. Derive the winning aspiration for each possibility. It is the most abstract box and sets direction; adjust it so that the first three boxes fit one another.
5. Run the reality check: capabilities. List the few capabilities the how-to-win cannot work without. Apply the can't/won't test from Martin's article: rivals either cannot match these capabilities or will not, because matching would damage them elsewhere. If rivals have the same capabilities, the result is an average strategy; go back to step 2.
6. Run the reality check: management systems. For each capability name the system that will build and maintain it: a measure, a routine, a hiring or training rule, an incentive. A strategy with only the first three boxes is, in Martin's word, lazy, because it skips this check.
7. Move back and forth until the five choices fit and reinforce each other. Mark every statement as FACT or as an assumption. Sometimes a possibility has to be abandoned because no supporting capabilities or systems can be built.
8. Choose between the possibilities by asking, for each, what would have to be true about customers, competitors and the company for it to succeed (S5-05), and test the least certain conditions with research. Working rule: do not choose on the size of the market alone.
9. Write the chosen cascade on one page, with what was decided against, the open assumptions and the first tests. Return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. Input: a flour mill near Tashkent with capacity 3,000 tonnes a month sells 2,100 tonnes, mostly through wholesalers. Diagnosis: price competition from larger mills; no direct link to end users.

```
POSSIBILITY A                                                    INTERPRETATION
Where to play: small bread bakeries in Tashkent city and region, sold direct
How to win: the same dough behaviour from batch to batch, delivery within
  24 hours in small lots; larger mills sell through wholesalers and will not
  run small direct routes (can't/won't test: "won't")
Aspiration: the first-choice flour of small bakeries in the Tashkent area
Capabilities: batch testing lab; own delivery routes; a baker-technologist
Systems: batch quality log shown to buyers; route plan; bonus on repeat orders
Numbers (invented): 1,200 bakeries x 1.5 t a month; at 25% of them:
  300 x 1.5 = 450 t; sales 2,100 + 450 = 2,550 t = 85% of capacity (now 70%)
POSSIBILITY B
Where to play: 2-kg retail packs for households in supermarkets
How to win: not found. Larger mills already have brands, packing lines and
  shelf space -> fails the can't/won't test
Numbers (invented): 150,000 packs = 300 t; 2,100 + 300 = 2,400 t = 80%
CHOICE: A. Decided against: retail packs, competing on lowest price.
Open assumption to test: bakers will pay the same price for consistency
  (interviews, S1-04; trial with 20 bakeries, S7-02).
```
Arithmetic check: 2,100 / 3,000 = 0.70; 1,200 x 0.25 = 300; 300 x 1.5 = 450; 2,100 + 450 = 2,550; 2,550 / 3,000 = 0.85; 150,000 x 2 kg = 300,000 kg = 300 t; 2,100 + 300 = 2,400; 2,400 / 3,000 = 0.80.

## Common mistakes
- Filling the boxes left to right and fixing each before the next.
- Choosing a market first because it is large, then hunting for a way to win in it.
- A how-to-win that is a list of initiatives or a statement nobody could disagree with.
- Stopping after three boxes, with no capabilities or systems.
- Listing capabilities every competitor also has.
- Producing one possibility only, so there was nothing to choose between.
- Skipping the diagnosis and using the cascade to decorate a decision already made.

## Quality checklist
- Is there a diagnosis behind the cascade, with its evidence?
- Were at least two whole possibilities drafted and compared?
- Were where-to-play and how-to-win chosen as a pair?
- Does the how-to-win pass the opposite test?
- Do the capabilities pass the can't/won't test against named competitors?
- Does each capability have a management system?
- Is what was decided against written down?
- Are the assumptions marked and the first tests planned?

```
STRATEGY CHOICE CASCADE - [organisation / unit] - [date]

POSSIBILITY [A / B / C]

How to win: customers choose us because ... | opposite test: pass / fail
Must-have capabilities: 1 ... 2 ... 3 ... | can't/won't test vs [rivals]: ...
```

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- In owner-led firms the owner often decides alone. Practical caution: get the owner into the session, since choices agreed only with a marketing manager may not hold.
- Polite agreement in a meeting is not a choice. Ask each decision-maker to say what the company will stop doing, and write it down.
- Run the session in the language the decision-makers think in, Uzbek or Russian, and translate the one-page output afterwards (S3-03).
- Where-to-play by geography needs care: Tashkent and the regions may differ in channels and buyers; size each separately and treat any transfer as a hypothesis.
- The examples in the sources are large Western companies. Whether rivals here "will not" follow a move is an assumption to check with competitor evidence, not to assume.
