# Customer journey research (S1-20)
Customer journey research reconstructs, from the customer's side, the sequence of steps and touchpoints a person goes through before, during and after a purchase, and locates where the experience helps or blocks them. The academic reference is Katherine Lemon and Peter Verhoef's 2016 review in the Journal of Marketing. They describe customer experience as the customer's cognitive, emotional, behavioural, sensory and social responses across the whole purchase journey, divide the journey into prepurchase, purchase and postpurchase stages, and sort touchpoints into four kinds by who controls them. The mapping practice itself comes from service design; this skill uses the research-based process described by the Nielsen Norman Group. Evidence grade: A for the framework, which rests on a peer-reviewed synthesis. Two cautions limit the weight to put on a map: Lemon and Verhoef themselves note that empirical work on journeys was still limited, and journeys differ between segments, so a single map is a simplification that hides variation.

Working rules for the assistant:
- Answer in the language the user writes in (Uzbek, Russian or English).
- Label every statement as FACT (reported by customers or shown in data) or INTERPRETATION (your reading).
- When the work is finished, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- The decision concerns the experience across several touchpoints or channels: where buyers drop out, which channel to fix or add, what happens after the sale.
- The team's picture of the path to purchase comes from inside the company and has not been checked with customers.
- A purchase involves search, comparison and contact with several parties (seller, dealer, friends, reviews).
Do not use it when:
- The problem sits in one known touchpoint. Study that touchpoint directly (S1-13 mystery shopping, S1-06 observation, S1-09 test).
- The question is why people buy the category at all, or what the product should be (S1-07, S0-11).
- The team wants to estimate the sales effect of each channel. Lemon and Verhoef describe attribution models for this and note they face causality problems and are mostly applied online.
- No recent buyers or drop-outs can be reached; a map built only from staff opinion is an assumption map and must be labelled so.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
- The decision, and the scenario to map: who, buying what, from first need to which end point.
- The segments expected to travel differently (first-time and repeat buyers, channel, region, B2B roles).
- Internal data: enquiry logs, CRM stages, call and chat records, site and social analytics, complaints, satisfaction scores.
- Access to recent buyers, ideally within the last few months, and to people who started and did not buy.
- The staff who own each touchpoint, for a workshop at the start and the end.

## Procedure
Part A. Decide whether the method fits.
1. Run the fit test with the user: (a) Does the decision concern more than one touchpoint or the links between them? (b) Is the customer's own sequence of steps unknown or disputed? (c) Can we reach recent buyers and non-completers? Three yes answers: proceed. If (a) is no, go to a single-touchpoint method. If (c) is no, produce an assumption map only and plan how to check it.
2. Fix the actor and the scenario. One map has one point of view; the Nielsen Norman Group advises one actor per map. If two segments are expected to differ, plan two maps from the start. Working rule: no more than three segments in one project.

Part B. Plan and run it.
3. Draw the team's hypothesis map first. In a short workshop, staff lay out the steps they believe customers take. Keep it as the written expectation (S2-11); it will be compared with the findings.
4. Review existing data before new fieldwork: counts at each recorded stage, response times, common complaints, most used entry channels. Note which steps leave no data trace; those need customer accounts.
5. Collect customer accounts. The core is qualitative because it shows motives, thoughts and feelings that counts miss. Options:
   - Interviews that reconstruct the most recent purchase step by step: "What happened first? What did you do next? Who did you talk to?" Ask about what happened, not what they usually do (S1-23, S3-04). Laying the steps out on cards during the interview helps recall.
   - Observation in the store, showroom or at home (S1-06), because what people say and do can differ.
   - Diary entries during a long purchase (S1-17), which capture steps close to the time they happen.
   - Walk-throughs of competitors' paths when the product has no customers yet.
   Working rule: 5 to 8 accounts per segment to start, including at least 2 non-completers; decide on more with S2-04.
6. Record every step in the same grid: stage (prepurchase: need recognition, search, consideration; purchase: choice, ordering, payment; postpurchase: use, service requests, engagement), action, touchpoint, who controls it, what the person thought and felt, problem or help, a quote, the evidence source.
7. Classify touchpoints with the four Lemon and Verhoef types: brand-owned (site, own store, advertising), partner-owned (dealers, marketplaces, delivery firms, agencies), customer-owned (the customer's own actions, such as measuring a room or arranging finance) and social or external (relatives, other customers, review sites, social media). A map with only brand-owned touchpoints is incomplete; a later review of journey studies found most looked only at those.
8. Include what came before and after. Past experience shapes expectations for the current purchase, and the postpurchase stage can lead to repurchase or send the customer back to compare alternatives (the loyalty loop). Ask about earlier purchases in the category and about what happened after delivery.
9. Build one map per segment, then compare. Mark steps that all segments share and steps where they diverge (different entry point, different adviser, different barrier). Report divergence as a finding; do not average it away.
10. Add counts. Use a survey (S1-08) or logs to show how many customers pass each touchpoint and where they stop. Quantitative data shows how widespread a step or problem is; it does not replace the accounts. For experience scores, Lemon and Verhoef report that satisfaction and NPS predict about equally well, the Customer Effort Score is weaker, and several metrics together do better than one.
11. Find the critical touchpoints. Lemon and Verhoef call identifying the most influential touchpoints difficult but important. Working rule of this skill: rank each problem by (share of customers who pass the touchpoint) x (share who stop or report a serious problem there), and check the top items against the interview evidence.
12. Compare with the hypothesis map and name what staff had wrong or missed. Hold a closing workshop with touchpoint owners; assign each priority problem an owner.
13. Turn top problems into testable changes (S1-09, S7-02); a link seen in logs is not yet a cause. Report with the template, state limits (who was interviewed, recall period, segments not covered), and return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE for illustration. No real data.
Input: a Tashkent furniture maker gets many Instagram enquiries about sofas but few showroom visits. Fit test passed: several touchpoints, sequence disputed, buyers reachable. Two segments: households furnishing a new flat, and households replacing an old sofa. Twelve interviews (four buyers and two non-completers per segment) and one month of enquiry logs.

Findings from the log (FACT, invented): 400 Instagram enquiries; 120 received a price reply within one hour and 36 of them visited the showroom (30%); 280 received a later reply and 28 visited (10%); in total 64 of 400 visited (16%).

| Stage | New-flat segment | Replacement segment |
|---|---|---|
| Prepurchase | Starts from a designer's or relatives' advice (social); saves posts from several makers; measures the room (customer-owned) | Starts from seeing a neighbour's sofa (social); looks at one or two makers |
| Purchase | Visits two or three showrooms in one day; asks about delivery date and instalments | Asks price in direct messages; visits only if the price is answered |
| Postpurchase | Waits for delivery with no news; phones to ask | Asks about removal of the old sofa (partner-owned, not offered) |

Output: FACT: 5 of 6 in the replacement segment said they stopped contacting a maker that did not state a price in messages. INTERPRETATION: slow or missing price replies are the main block for this segment; the log pattern (30% against 10%) agrees but does not prove cause, since quick replies may have gone to more serious enquiries. Next: test a one-hour reply rule for two weeks (S7-02). The two segments enter differently, so one map would have hidden that the new-flat segment depends on advisers the company does not reach.

## Common mistakes
- Drawing the map in a meeting room and presenting it as research.
- One map for all customers.
- Listing only the company's own channels and leaving out relatives, dealers and the customer's own tasks. Stopping at the payment; no postpurchase stage.
- Interviewing only satisfied buyers and no one who dropped out.
- Asking "how do you usually choose?" in place of reconstructing the last purchase. Treating a drop-off in the logs as the cause without a test.

## Quality checklist
- Is there a named actor and scenario for each map? (yes/no)
- Was the team's hypothesis map recorded before fieldwork and compared afterwards? (yes/no)
- Does every step come from customer accounts or data, with the source noted? (yes/no)
- Are all three stages and all four touchpoint types covered or marked "none found"? (yes/no)
- Were non-completers included? (yes/no)
- Are segment differences shown, not averaged? (yes/no)
- Do priority problems have counts, an owner and a planned test? (yes/no)

## Works with
- S0-06 brand and sales funnel diagnosis: shows at which stage the loss is, before mapping.
- S1-04 interviews, S1-06 observation, S1-13 mystery shopping, S1-16 critical incident technique, S1-17 diary studies: collect the accounts. S1-23: ask about actual past steps.
- S1-18 B2B buying-centre mapping: when several people share the purchase, map each role.
- S1-08 surveys, S4-10 crosstabs: counts per touchpoint and segment. S4-20 Net Promoter Score: one of several experience metrics.
- S4-05 matrix displays: the segment-by-stage table. S5-10 personas: the actor for each map.
- S5-11 opportunity solution tree, S1-09 experiments, S7-02 controlled tests: act on and test priority problems.
- S6-14 marketing mix and S9-07 communication plan: channel and service decisions that follow.

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Conduct interviews in the language the customer chooses, Uzbek or Russian, and keep quotes in the original.
- Ask who else took part in the decision and how, as a neutral step question; do not assume who decides.
- Polite answers can turn problems into "everything was fine". Ask for the sequence of events and timings, not for an evaluation. Messaging apps and phone calls may carry steps that leave no record in company systems; ask customers to show the chat if they are willing.
- A Telegram-bot survey for the counting step misses older and rural buyers; map Tashkent and regional journeys separately where the sample allows.
- The stage model and touchpoint types were developed on Western markets. Use them as a grid, and add stages or touchpoints that customers describe and the grid lacks.
