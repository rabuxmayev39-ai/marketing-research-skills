# Principles of persuasion (S6-15)

The principles of persuasion are seven recurring reasons why people say yes to a request: reciprocity, commitment and consistency, social proof, authority, liking, scarcity and unity. They come from the social psychologist Robert Cialdini, whose book Influence first set out six of them; the new and expanded edition (2021) added unity as the seventh. Each principle is a mental shortcut: people facing many decisions lean on simple cues (what others do, what an expert says, what is running out) and do not weigh every option fully. In marketing the principles are a checklist for designing a message or an offer so that true, relevant cues are easy to notice.

Evidence grade: A. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. The grade applies to the existence of the principles, each of which rests on published experiments. It does not apply to the size of the effect in any one market. Three cautions. Effects depend on context: an independent replication of the best-known social proof field study, run in German hotels, did not find that the "most guests do this" message beat a standard message. Many of the famous numbers come from single studies or from the author's own anecdotes. And the book is a popular synthesis by the theory's author, not a neutral review. Use the principles to generate versions to test, never as a promise of uplift.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (what the client can prove, what a test showed) versus INTERPRETATION (why a version worked). Apply the honesty rule in step 4 without exception. When the message set and test plan are delivered, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- A message, offer, landing page, Telegram post or sales script is being written or improved.
- The team has several true facts about the client (customers served, certificates, guarantees) and must choose which to show first.
- A live test (S9-06, S1-09) is possible, so versions can be compared.

Do not use it when:
- The task is choosing whom to target or how to position the brand; that is S6-01 and S6-02. The principles shape the wording of an offer, not the strategy.
- The client has no true evidence for a cue. Do not build a message on a claim that cannot be proved.
- The audience is in a vulnerable position (debt, illness, urgent need). Pressure cues such as scarcity are not appropriate there. Working rule.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. The action wanted from the audience (call, order, sign up) and the audience description (S6-01, S5-10).
2. The current message or offer, if one exists, and its results.
3. An evidence file from the client: number of customers, real reviews with permission to use them, certificates, guarantees, real stock and real deadlines.
4. The channel and whether a split test is possible, with expected traffic.
5. Legal and platform limits on advertising claims (ask the client's lawyer; do not assume).

## Procedure
1. State the single action wanted and the main reason people currently do not take it (S5-07 can help). A principle is useful only if it answers that reason: doubt about quality points to authority or social proof; delay points to honest scarcity or a small first commitment.
2. Go through the seven principles and note, for each, what true material the client has.
   - Reciprocity: people tend to give back to those who gave first. Cialdini advises gifts that are useful, personal and unexpected, such as a helpful guide or a free measurement visit.
   - Commitment and consistency: people try to act in line with what they have already said or done. Ask for a small, voluntary, active first step (a saved calculation, a written preference) before the large one.
   - Social proof: when unsure, people look at what others like them do. Use real numbers and real reviews from comparable customers.
   - Authority: people follow credible experts. Show real credentials, and let a third party state them where possible; Cialdini's firm reports that introductions by a colleague work better than self-praise.
   - Liking: people agree more readily with those they like. Sources of liking named by Cialdini are real similarity, sincere praise and working together toward a shared goal.
   - Scarcity: people want more of what is rare or ending. Point only to limits that exist, and say what would be lost by waiting.
   - Unity: people are moved by those they count as one of their own group (family, home region, profession). It goes beyond liking: "one of us", not "similar to us".
3. Pick one or two principles per message. Working rule: more than two cues in one short ad compete for attention and make each harder to test.
4. Run the honesty audit. For every cue, write the claim, the evidence and the date checked. Reject any claim without evidence. Cialdini's own line is that pointing to a cue that exists is influence, while manufacturing it is manipulation; he calls invented online reviews a serious problem. Hard rule for this skill: no false scarcity, no fake deadlines or countdowns that reset, no invented or paid-for reviews presented as spontaneous, no made-up customer counts, no borrowed logos or titles.
5. Check for backfire. Cialdini warns that telling people how many others do a bad thing can make it look normal. Do not write "most people ignore X" when the aim is to get people to do X. He also reports that ratings around 4.2 to 4.7 stars persuade more than a perfect 5, which looks too good to be true; show real mixed reviews.
6. Write a control version without the cue and one version per chosen principle. Change only the cue between versions.
7. Test (S9-06, S1-09, S7-02). Decide before the test which measure counts (leads or orders, not only clicks) and the sample needed. Working rule: do not declare a winner on fewer than about 100 conversions per version; with less, report the result as a direction and repeat.
8. Read the result. A version that wins here has won in this channel, audience and month only. Record it in the agency's case file (S8-08).
9. Deliver the message set, the honesty audit table and the test reading. Return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. Input: a Tashkent furniture maker advertises custom kitchens in Telegram. Aim: more requests for a free measurement visit.

```
HONESTY AUDIT                                                            FACT
Claim                                   | Evidence              | Decision
"412 families ordered a kitchen in 2026"| CRM count = 412       | use
"Only 5 slots left this month"          | 40 slots still free   | REJECT (false)
"Master with 15 years of experience"    | work record checked   | use later
TEST: same budget and audience, 600,000 soum per version
Version               | Shown | Clicks | Click rate | Requests | Cost per request
A control (price)     | 4,000 |   96   |   2.4%     |    12    | 50,000 soum
B social proof (412)  | 4,000 |  132   |   3.3%     |    21    | 28,571 soum
Relative lift in click rate: (3.3 - 2.4) / 2.4 = 37.5%
READING                                                        INTERPRETATION
B looks better on both measures, but 12 and 21 requests are far below the
100-per-version working rule. Treat as a direction; repeat for two more
weeks before changing the main advertising.
```
Arithmetic check: 96 / 4,000 = 0.024; 132 / 4,000 = 0.033; 0.9 / 2.4 = 0.375; 600,000 / 12 = 50,000; 600,000 / 21 = 28,571.4, rounded to 28,571.

## Common mistakes
- Inventing the cue: fake "3 left", fake timers, reviews written by staff.
- Stacking five principles in one banner so that none is clear and none can be tested.
- Quoting textbook uplifts (from tipping or hotel studies) to a client as the expected result.
- Using social proof in reverse, by advertising how many people do the unwanted thing.
- Judging by clicks when the aim is orders.
- Treating a principle as a strategy; it cannot repair a weak offer or the wrong audience.

## Quality checklist
- Is the wanted action stated in one line?
- Does each message use no more than two principles, named?
- Does every cue have dated evidence in the audit table?
- Were all scarcity and deadline claims checked against real stock and real dates?
- Is there a control version that differs only in the cue?
- Was the success measure fixed before the test?
- Is the result reported as valid for this channel and audience only?

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- The studies named in the sources opened for this skill were run in North America and Western Europe, and one of them changed result between the United States and Germany. Which cues work best here, and how strongly, is unknown; treat every transfer as a hypothesis and test it.
- Unity (family, home region, profession) and authority may behave differently in Tashkent and in the regions. That is a hypothesis to test by region, not a fact.
- Write and test versions separately in Uzbek and Russian. A cue translated word for word can read as pressure or as boasting; check wording with native speakers (S3-03).
- Telegram audiences miss many older and rural people, so a Telegram test result should not be extended to them.
- Check current advertising and consumer protection rules with a lawyer before using claims about discounts, deadlines or "number one" status.
- Keep customer quotes in the original language, with written permission.
