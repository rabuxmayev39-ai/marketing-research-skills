# Marketing mix (4P, 7P) and 4A (S6-14)

The marketing mix is the set of levers a company controls. The 4P list (product, price, place, promotion) is credited to E. Jerome McCarthy (1960); the term "marketing mix" itself is associated with Neil Borden. The 7P list adds people, process and physical evidence for services and is credited to Booms and Bitner (1981). The 4A framework, by Jagdish Sheth and Rajendra Sisodia (2012), looks from the customer's side: an offer succeeds only if the target customers find it acceptable, affordable, accessible and are aware of it. The Ps are what the company does; the As are the conditions those actions must produce. This skill uses them together as a coverage check on a plan.

Evidence grade: B. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. The Ps are taught everywhere and used by professional bodies such as the Chartered Institute of Marketing, but they are checklists: they name levers and say nothing about which lever matters or how much. A review opened for this skill (Goi, 2009) reports criticism that the 4Ps describe marketing from the producer's side and not the customer's, and that researchers have never agreed which extra Ps are valid. The 4A framework answers the first criticism but has little independent testing of its own. The agency database says 4A was built with emerging markets in mind. The pages opened confirm only that Sheth later used it to organise emerging-market strategies (Sinha and Sheth, 2018), and one secondary source says it was inspired by a framework used at Coca-Cola. Use these lists to find gaps, never as proof that a plan is good.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (what the plan contains, audit and survey results) versus INTERPRETATION (which gap matters most). Do not fill a box just to make the table look complete; an empty box is a finding. When the audit is delivered, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- A marketing plan is being drafted or reviewed and the team wants to be sure every lever was considered.
- Sales are weak and the cause could lie outside advertising (price, distribution, service).
- A plan is being taken to a new region or segment where conditions differ.

Do not use it when:
- The target customer and positioning are not yet chosen. Do S6-01 and S6-02 first; the mix serves a chosen target.
- The question is why customers behave as they do. The lists organise answers; they do not produce them.
- A single lever needs depth (pricing: S6-13; communication: S9-07).

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. The target segment and positioning (S6-01, S6-02).
2. The current or draft plan, including items owned by other departments (sales, logistics, service).
3. Customer-side evidence: survey (S1-08), store or channel audit, mystery shopping (S1-13), interviews.
4. Whether the offer is a physical product, a service or both.
5. Budget and what the client can and cannot change.

## Procedure
1. State the target customer and the outcome wanted (S7-01). Ask the user to confirm. Every later judgment is made for this customer.
2. Choose the list. Working rule: use 7P whenever staff contact, waiting or delivery are part of what the customer buys; otherwise 4P. Always add the 4A check.
3. Audit the company side. For each P write what the plan does today, the evidence, and who owns it. The Chartered Institute of Marketing guide gives these meanings and checks:
   - Product: what is offered, built around what customers want; avoid over-engineering. Price: what customers pay; it positions the offer and is the one element that brings in revenue.
   - Place: getting the product to the right place at the right time and quantity, including online. Promotion: how the offer is communicated; stress benefits, and go where the target customers are.
   - People: staff who meet customers shape how the brand is seen. Process: the whole experience from first contact to after-sales, built for the customer's convenience. Physical evidence: visible cues that reduce uncertainty before buying, such as premises, site and reviews.
4. Audit the customer side with the 4A. One secondary source reports two dimensions for each:
   - Acceptability: functional (it does the job reliably) and psychological (it feels right for people like me). Affordability: ability to pay and willingness to pay (value seen for the money).
   - Accessibility: availability (enough supply) and convenience (easy to get and use). Awareness: brand awareness and product knowledge (they know what it does).
   For each A, write the evidence and a share of the target customers who meet the condition, where data exist.
5. Find the weakest A. The authors' position is that an offer must do well on all four. Working rule: multiply the four shares to see how a single weak condition caps the result, and compare what happens if each A is improved in turn. This arithmetic assumes the conditions are independent, which they are not, so use it only to rank gaps.
6. Link As to Ps. The framework's authors say the As are achieved with marketing and non-marketing resources. Working rule map: acceptability - product, people, process, physical evidence; affordability - price, pack size, payment terms; accessibility - place, process; awareness - promotion, physical evidence. Mark any P spending that serves an A which is already strong.
7. Check consistency. Working rule: the levers must tell one story for the chosen positioning; a premium price with poor packaging or slow service is a conflict to report.
8. Write gaps as actions with an owner, a cost and a measure (S7-01). Name gaps the agency cannot fix (for example distribution) so the client decides.
9. Say what the lists cannot answer and which method will (S4-14, S1-10, S6-13).
10. Deliver the audit table and the ranked gaps. Return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. Input: an invented juice brand, "Bog'bon", plans to spend its regional budget on TV and Instagram in the Fergana valley. A survey of 300 households and an audit of 120 shops were run.

```
4A CHECK, target = households buying 1-litre juice                    FACT
Awareness: know the brand                         60%
Acceptability: of tasters, would buy again        70%
Affordability: price within usual juice spend     50%
Accessibility: 48 of 120 shops stock it           40%
Working-rule product: 0.60 x 0.70 x 0.50 x 0.40 = 0.084, about 8%
If accessibility rises to 70%: 0.60 x 0.70 x 0.50 x 0.70 = 0.147, about 15%
If awareness rises to 80%:     0.80 x 0.70 x 0.50 x 0.40 = 0.112, about 11%
P AUDIT (short)
Promotion: all of the budget | Place: no distributor plan | Price: one
  1-litre pack only | Product: liked
READING                                                     INTERPRETATION
The plan puts money on awareness, the strongest condition. The weakest
are accessibility and affordability. Move part of the budget to
distributor terms and shop coverage; test a 0.5-litre pack (S7-02).
```
Arithmetic check: 48 / 120 = 0.40. 0.60 x 0.70 = 0.42; 0.42 x 0.50 = 0.21; 0.21 x 0.40 = 0.084. 0.21 x 0.70 = 0.147. 0.80 x 0.70 = 0.56; 0.56 x 0.50 = 0.28; 0.28 x 0.40 = 0.112.

## Common mistakes
- Treating the mix as only promotion because that is what the agency sells.
- Running the audit before the target customer is chosen.
- Judging the As from the team's opinion and not from customers.
- Reading the multiplied figure as a forecast of sales or share.
- Ignoring conflicts between levers, such as a premium message with discount pricing.

## Quality checklist
- Is the target customer named before the audit starts?
- Does each P row state what is done now, the evidence and the owner?
- Does each A have customer-side evidence, with assumptions marked?
- Is the weakest A named, with the Ps that could fix it?
- Are conflicts between levers reported?
- Are empty boxes shown as gaps and not filled in?
- Does each action have an owner and a measure?

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Run the audit with the client's sales and logistics people present, in Uzbek or Russian; the agency alone will see only promotion.
- Measure accessibility by visiting shops or channels, not by asking the client; coverage may differ sharply between Tashkent and the regions. Treat this as a hypothesis to check for each project.
- For affordability, ask about pack sizes and payment terms as well as price; polite answers overstate willingness to pay, so prefer purchase data.
- Awareness figures from a Telegram-bot survey miss older and rural people and will not represent all households. Acceptability has a psychological side that depends on local norms; collect it in respondents' own words and keep quotes in the original language.
- Both frameworks come from American authors; apply them here as checklists and treat any claim about which lever matters most as a hypothesis.
