# Mixed methods designs (S1-02)

Mixed methods research collects both qualitative and quantitative data in one study and deliberately connects them. John Creswell and Vicki Plano Clark, in Designing and Conducting Mixed Methods Research, set out the designs most teams now use. Three are basic. In the exploratory sequential design, qualitative work comes first and its findings shape the quantitative stage (for example interviews that supply survey items). In the explanatory sequential design, quantitative work comes first and qualitative work then explains its results. In the convergent design (older texts say concurrent, parallel or triangulation), both are collected in about the same period, usually analysed separately, and then merged and compared. Fetters, Curry and Creswell (2013) add the point that defines the method: the two parts must be integrated, at the design stage, in the methods and in the report. Two studies stapled together are not mixed methods.

Evidence grade: B. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. These designs are an accepted professional standard with detailed guidance, including a best-practice report commissioned by the US National Institutes of Health. They are planning structures, not proof that the combined result is right. Trust the design to organise the work; judge each stage by its own method's standards.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (what each data set shows) versus INTERPRETATION (how you connect or reconcile the two). When the design plan or the integrated findings are delivered, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- The decision needs both "why or how" and "how many or how much". Fetters et al. describe qualitative work as suited to why and how a thing happens and to individual experience, and quantitative work to magnitude, generalising and causality.
- A survey must be written but the answer options and wording are unknown (exploratory sequential).
- Survey or sales numbers exist and nobody can explain a result (explanatory sequential).
- Time is short and two views of the same question are wanted at once for cross-checking (convergent).

Do not use it when:
- One kind of data answers the decision. The NIH report asks for a clear rationale tied to the study's aims; "more data is better" is not one.
- The two parts would answer different, unrelated questions. Then they are two projects; plan each alone.
- The team lacks skill in one of the two approaches, or the deadline cannot hold two stages plus integration. Do one stage well.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. The decision and research questions, each classified by purpose (S1-01).
2. What data already exist: past surveys, CRM or sales figures, earlier interviews.
3. People: who can run and analyse the qualitative part, who the quantitative part, and who owns integration.
4. Calendar and budget for both stages and the gap between them; access to respondents for both, and whether the same people can be reached twice.

## Procedure
1. Confirm the need. Ask the user: "Which part of the decision needs numbers, and which needs reasons?" If only one is named, recommend a single-method study and stop.
2. Write the shared research questions. List each question and mark which strand (QUAL, QUANT or both) answers it. Working rule: every strand must serve at least one question that the other strand also touches. If the lists do not overlap, it is not a mixed design.
3. Choose the design by what is unknown now. Options, language or variables unknown: exploratory sequential (QUAL then QUANT). Numbers exist but a pattern, gap or surprising group needs explaining: explanatory sequential (QUANT then QUAL). Two angles on the same question needed quickly, with both teams available at once: convergent. Fetters et al. state the intent simply: in sequential designs one phase builds on the other; in convergent designs the phases are merged so that results can be compared.
4. Plan the integration point before fieldwork. Fetters et al. name ways strands link at the methods level; plan at least one in writing. Connecting: one strand supplies the sample for the other (for example interview people chosen from survey answers). Building: one strand's findings shape the other's data collection (interview themes become questionnaire items). Merging: the two data sets are brought together for analysis and comparison.
5. Exploratory sequential, how to run it. (a) Run and analyse the qualitative stage fully (S1-04, S1-05, S4-01). (b) Produce a build table: theme, respondents' own wording, the survey item or answer option it becomes. (c) Draft the questionnaire from the table (S3-01), pretest (S3-02). (d) Field the survey on a new, larger sample. Working rule: do not let the fieldwork date of stage two force stage one to end before analysis is done; the build table is the product that justifies the design.
6. Explanatory sequential, how to run it. (a) Run and analyse the quantitative stage. (b) List the results that need explaining: unexpected differences, outlier groups, weak or contradictory answers. (c) Use those results to choose who to interview (connecting) and what to ask (building). (d) Run the qualitative stage and report each explanation next to the number it explains. Working rule: get permission for re-contact during the survey, or the follow-up sample cannot be drawn.
7. Convergent, how to run it. (a) Agree in advance the topics both strands will cover so they can be compared. (b) Collect both in the same period, analyse separately. (c) Merge in a joint display: one row per topic, with the quantitative result and the qualitative finding side by side.
8. Integrate in the report. Fetters et al. list three routes: narrative (weave both kinds of finding together topic by topic), data transformation (for example counting coded themes), and joint displays. Working rule for this agency: always produce a joint display, because clients can read it.
9. Judge the fit of each row. Fetters et al. use three outcomes: the two strands confirm each other, one expands the other, or they are discordant. Do not hide discordance. Working rule: for each discordant row, check sample differences and question wording first, then report it as an open question or a reason for follow-up.
10. State limits: who each strand covered, the time between stages, and anything that changed in the market between them. Return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. Input: a flour mill selling to bakers wants to know why small bakeries switch flour suppliers and how common each reason is. Nothing is known about reasons, so the design is exploratory sequential.

```
Stage 1 QUAL: 12 in-depth interviews with bakery owners (6 Tashkent, 6 regions).
Build table (extract):
Theme from interviews         | Owner's wording                 | Survey answer option
Dough behaves differently     | "xamir har safar boshqacha"     | Flour quality varies between batches
  from batch to batch         |                                 |
Supplier gives no deferral    | "без отсрочки не беру"          | No delayed payment offered
Delivery day not kept         | "aytgan kuni kelmadi"           | Delivery comes late

Stage 2 QUANT: Telegram survey, 150 bakery owners who switched in the last year.
Joint display (extract):
Topic            | QUANT (n=150)        | QUAL (n=12)                        | Fit
Batch variation  | 63 owners = 42%      | most detailed stories; bakers      | confirms
                 |                      | test each new sack                 |
Payment deferral | 45 owners = 30%      | decisive for regional owners only  | expands: check
                 |                      |                                    | by region
Late delivery    | 18 owners = 12%      | raised by 7 of 12, with anger      | discordant

Reading: batch variation is the leading stated reason.                    INTERP
Discordance on delivery: interviews over-sampled owners without own
  transport; survey question asked for "main reason" only. Follow up.     INTERP
Limit: Telegram sample misses owners who do not use Telegram.
```
Arithmetic check: 63/150 = 0.42; 45/150 = 0.30; 18/150 = 0.12.

## Common mistakes
- Running two unrelated studies and calling the report mixed methods.
- Fixing the survey before the interviews are analysed, so stage one changes nothing.
- In explanatory designs, interviewing whoever is easy to reach, not the groups the numbers pointed to.
- Treating twelve interview voices and a survey percentage as the same kind of evidence, or adding them together.
- Dropping the result that disagrees.
- Underestimating time: two stages plus the hand-over and integration each need their own slot in the calendar.

## Quality checklist
- Is there a stated reason why one method alone is not enough?
- Do the strands share research questions, written down?
- Is the design named, with the reason for the order?
- Is the link (connecting, building or merging) planned before fieldwork?
- Is there a build table or a sampling rule that shows how stage one shaped stage two?
- Does the report contain a joint display with a fit judgement per row?
- Is every discordant result reported with a possible cause?

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Published data on many local categories are thin, so exploratory sequential is the most common fit: interviews first, then a survey built from them.
- Keep the wording column of the build table in the original Uzbek or Russian, and write survey options in both languages from that wording, with S3-03 for translation checks.
- If the quantitative strand is a Telegram-bot survey, it is likely to miss older and rural people, while interviews may reach them. State this when comparing strands; a mismatch can come from the samples.
- Polite or agreeing answers can make survey ratings look better than interview stories. Treat that pattern as a discordance to examine, not as confirmation.
- The design guidance comes mainly from US health and education research. The structure transfers; expected timings and response behaviour are hypotheses here.
