# Management decision problem to research problem (S0-02)

This method separates two things that briefs usually mix. The management decision problem is what the decision-maker has to do: it is about action ("should we raise the price?"). The marketing research problem is what has to be learned so that the decision can be made well: it is about information ("how will buyers and sales respond to different price levels?"). The distinction and the steps around it come from Naresh Malhotra's textbook Marketing Research: An Applied Orientation, chapter 2, where problem definition is treated as the first and most important step of a research project. The decision problem tends to be stated in terms of symptoms, such as lost market share; the research problem should point at the possible causes behind them.

Evidence grade: B. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. This is standard textbook practice, now in its seventh edition, and the same advice appears in other research manuals, including the FAO manual opened for this skill. It is a professional rule based on experience; no experiment showing that projects defined this way produce better decisions was found among the sources opened. Its limits: it fails when the client cannot name a decision, and then stakeholder interviews (S0-12) must come first; a translation done only by the researcher can drift from what the client will really act on; and not every business problem is a research problem. Use it as a discipline for every brief, not as proof that the research problem chosen is the right one.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (what the client's records show, what the client said) versus INTERPRETATION (the translation into a research problem, the suspected causes). Never choose a method in this skill. When the research problem is written and checked, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- Any brief arrives, including briefs that already name a method ("we need a survey", "do focus groups").
- The client describes a symptom or a wish for information ("we want to know our customers better") with no decision attached.
- A study is running and the team has lost sight of what it is for.

Do not use it when:
- Nobody on the client side can name a decision, its alternatives or its owner. Run S0-12 first, then come back.
- The decision is already made and research is wanted only to support it. Say so to the client; working rule: do not design research whose result cannot change anything.
- The question can be answered from existing records in an hour. Answer it (S1-03) and skip the project.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. The brief or request in the client's own words.
2. A conversation with the person who will make the decision, not only the person who sent the brief.
3. Basic records around the symptom: sales, leads, conversion, prices, by period.
4. What the client already believes about the causes (S0-13), and the limits: decision deadline, budget, things not open to change.

## Procedure
1. Ask for the decision. "What will you decide when this work is finished? Between which options? By when? Who decides?" Write the answers down. If there is no answer after two attempts, stop and go to S0-12.
2. Run a problem audit with the decision-maker. Malhotra's audit covers seven points: the history of events that led to the need for a decision; the courses of action available; the criteria that will be used to judge them; the actions likely to follow from different findings; the information needed to answer the decision-maker's questions; how each piece of information will be used; and how decisions get made in this organisation. Ask them in this order and record the answers.
3. Separate symptoms from causes. Decision-makers often see only symptoms such as falling sales or lost share. List the symptom with its number and period (FACT), then list the possible causes named by the client, by staff and by the records (INTERPRETATION). If the list of causes is thin, use S0-04 or S0-03.
4. Add outside input before fixing the problem. Malhotra's tasks for this stage are discussions with decision-makers, interviews with industry experts, analysis of secondary (existing) data and, if still unclear, some qualitative research. Working rule: at least a quick look at the client's own records and one outside view before step 5.
5. Write the management decision problem as one action question: "Should we ...?" or "Which of A, B or C should we ...?"
6. Translate it into the marketing research problem in two parts, as Malhotra prescribes: a broad statement that gives the overall direction, and specific components that say what exactly must be found out. Working rule: three to five components.
7. Check for the two classic errors. Too broad: the statement gives no guidance on what to study ("improve the company's image", "understand our customers"). Too narrow: the statement already assumes the answer and shuts out other options ("find out how much to cut the price" when price may not be the cause).
8. For each component write a research question, a hypothesis where one is possible, and the information needed. These are the parts of Malhotra's "approach to the problem". A hypothesis is a statement that data could show to be false.
9. Run the backward check. The FAO manual advises discussing with the client how different findings would change the decision. For each component write: "if the answer is X we will do A; if it is Y we will do B." Working rule: delete any component for which every possible answer leads to the same action; the FAO manual calls such data interesting but not important.
10. Confirm in writing with the decision-maker that the decision problem, the research problem and the "if X then A" lines are right. Hand the result to S2-01 for the full brief.
11. Return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. Input: the owner of a language school in Tashkent writes: "Enrolments are falling. We need a survey about how people see our brand."

```
FACTS from the school's records (invented)
Autumn last year: 2,000 trial lessons -> 1,000 paid enrolments (50%)
This autumn:      2,000 trial lessons ->   800 paid enrolments (40%)
Symptom: enrolments down 200, or 20%; interest (trial lessons) unchanged
AUDIT (short): options: A cut price 10%; B change the trial lesson and the
follow-up call; C more advertising. Decision by 1 December.
MANAGEMENT DECISION PROBLEM
Which of A, B or C should the school do to restore enrolments?
MARKETING RESEARCH PROBLEM (INTERPRETATION)
Broad statement: find out why people who took a trial lesson this autumn
did not enrol, and what they did instead.
Components:
1 What share of the 1,200 non-enrollers went to another school, postponed,
  or chose self-study or a tutor?
2 What role did price, schedule, teacher and follow-up play in their choice?
3 What changed in the trial lesson and follow-up between the two autumns?
4 What do the schools they chose offer and charge?
BACKWARD CHECK
1+2: most went to cheaper schools and name price -> consider A
1+2+3: most postponed or name the trial or no call-back -> do B
Any result: C is weak, because trial lessons did not fall -> drop C now
Rejected wording: "survey about brand image" (too broad, no decision use)
```
Arithmetic check: 1,000 / 2,000 = 0.50; 800 / 2,000 = 0.40; 1,000 - 800 = 200; 200 / 1,000 = 0.20; 2,000 - 800 = 1,200.

## Common mistakes
- Starting from the method the client asked for instead of the decision.
- Writing the research problem as the decision problem again ("research whether to cut the price").
- A broad statement with no components, or components with no link to any action.
- Taking the first symptom as the problem and never listing causes; talking only to the marketing manager when the owner decides.
- Collecting "nice to know" items that no answer could turn into an action, or forcing research onto a problem it cannot solve (the Ontario open textbook gives staff turnover as an example).

## Quality checklist
- Is the decision stated as an action question with named alternatives, owner and date?
- Were the seven audit points asked and recorded?
- Are the symptom (with numbers) and the candidate causes listed separately?
- Does the research problem have a broad statement and three to five specific components, neither too broad nor too narrow?
- Does every component have an "if X then A, if Y then B" line?
- Has the decision-maker confirmed the wording in writing?

## Output template
```
DECISION TO RESEARCH PROBLEM - [client] - [date]
Request (verbatim): ...
Decision-maker: ... | decision date: ... | limits: ...
Symptom (FACT, number, period): ...
Candidate causes (INTERPRETATION): ...
Audit notes: history ... | options ... | criteria ... | use of information ...
MANAGEMENT DECISION PROBLEM: Should / which ... ?
MARKETING RESEARCH PROBLEM
  Broad statement: ...
  Component 1: ... | research question ... | hypothesis ... | information needed ...
  Component 2: ... (repeat)
BACKWARD CHECK: component -> if X then action A | if Y then action B
Too broad / too narrow check: ... | Confirmed by: ... on ...
```

- S0-12 client and stakeholder interviews: when no decision can be named. S0-13 facts, assumptions, unknowns register: sorting what the client believes.
- S0-01 strategy kernel: checks whether the stated problem is a diagnosis or a goal. S0-03 issue tree and S0-04 5 Whys and Ishikawa: generating causes and hypotheses for the components.
- S1-03 desk research first: the secondary data step. S1-01 research design types: the next choice once the research problem is fixed.
- S2-01 research brief and question hierarchy: receives the output. S2-11 write expectations down before fieldwork: locks the hypotheses.
- S7-01 objective and KPI hierarchy: the decision criteria reused as measures.

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- The person who sends the brief is often not the person who decides. Practical caution: ask early who signs off, and get at least one conversation with the owner or director.
- A client may agree politely with the agency's wording without meaning it. Ask them to restate the decision and the options in their own words, in Uzbek or Russian, and keep that wording.
- Clients may ask for a method by name because a competitor used it. Treat the named method as information about expectations, not as the problem.
- Records needed for the symptom may sit in notebooks, 1C exports or Telegram chats. Plan time to assemble them; do not assume they are complete.
- The textbook examples are large Western firms. Whether the same symptom-to-cause patterns apply to a local client is a hypothesis to check.
