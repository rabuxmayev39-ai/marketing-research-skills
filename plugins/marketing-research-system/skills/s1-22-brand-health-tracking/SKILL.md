# Brand health tracking (S1-22)
Brand health tracking is a survey of category buyers repeated in the same form over time. It records what buyers hold in memory about the brands in a category (awareness, links to buying situations, attitude, word of mouth) together with what they buy, so that a team has a baseline before a campaign and can see later whether anything moved. The current professional reference is Jenni Romaniuk's Better Brand Health (2023, Ehrenberg-Bass Institute), which treats tracking as the measurement of category buyers' memory and asks that trackers be designed for the whole category. Evidence grade: B, an established professional standard; the design rules are widely used, and several come from published Ehrenberg-Bass work, but a tracker as a whole is not a tested instrument. Two known limits decide how much weight to put on it. First, scores follow brand size: bigger brands have more users, and users give more answers about a brand. Second, tracking results are usually very stable, so small movements between waves are mostly sampling noise. A tracker describes memory; it does not prove that advertising caused sales.

Working rules for the assistant:
- Answer in the language the user writes in (Uzbek, Russian or English).
- Label every statement as FACT (measured in the survey) or INTERPRETATION (your reading).
- When the work is finished, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- A campaign or launch is planned and the team needs a baseline first, with at least one later wave.
- The client will act on the readings (messages, reach, budget) and can keep the survey identical between waves. The category has enough reachable buyers for a survey of a few hundred.
Do not use it when:
- The question is asked once and not repeated; run a single survey (S1-08).
- The aim is to prove that an advertisement increased sales; use a controlled test (S7-02).
- The affordable sample is so small that the expected change cannot be told from noise (see step 6).
- Sales, search or site data already answer the question. An Ehrenberg-Bass researcher has argued that survey interviews should be a last resort when existing data can supply the measure; that is an opinion piece, and it is a useful prompt to check.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
- The decision and the objective of the planned activity (S7-01), with the size of change the team hopes for.
- A category definition and a rule for who is a category buyer.
- A brand list covering large and small brands and the client.
- A researched CEP list (S1-21) if mental availability will be tracked. A sample source that can be used again in the same way, and the budget per wave. Each brand's market share or penetration from another source, if available.

## Procedure
Part A. Decide whether the method fits.
1. Run the fit test with the user: (a) Will the survey be repeated at least once in the same form? (b) Is there a decision that a reading could change? (c) Can the same kind of sample be drawn each time? (d) Is the hoped-for change larger than the noise at the affordable sample size? If (a) is no, run a one-off survey. If (d) is no, enlarge the sample, lengthen the interval, or do not track.
2. Agree what tracking will not show. It will show levels and changes in memory and reported buying; it will not attribute sales to a campaign.

Part B. Plan it.
3. Design for the category. Romaniuk's test is that any brand in the category could use the same study. Include small brands as well as large ones and represent the whole market, not only the client's direct rivals.
4. Define the sample as category buyers, with the same source, screener and quotas in every wave (S2-02). Make sure non-buyers and light buyers of the client brand are present in numbers: Romaniuk argues that heavy users drown out the buyers who matter for growth.
5. Choose the question blocks. The Ehrenberg-Bass workshop outline lists five measurement areas plus buying behaviour. (a) Category and brand buying: which brands bought in a stated period, how often. (b) Brand awareness: prompted awareness is the main measure. Romaniuk notes that unprompted measures are biased against small brands and non-users. (c) Brand associations: pick-any links between brands and CEPs, with a "none" option (computed in S4-19). Prefer buying situations to image words; she argues general attributes such as trust matter less than what buyers need from the category. (d) Brand attitude, placed after the association questions. (e) Word of mouth, given and received. (f) Distinctive assets are measured in a separate survey before any brand is named (S6-04).
   Working rule: keep the questionnaire short enough for a phone screen, about 10 minutes.
6. Plan sample size against noise. Standard formula for two independent waves of n each, for a percentage near 50%: smallest change that clears 95% sampling error = 1.96 x square root of (2 x 0.25 / n). With n = 400 this is about 7 percentage points; with n = 1,000 about 4.4 points. Sub-groups (non-buyers, a region) have smaller n and wider margins. Tell the user this figure before fieldwork (S2-03).
7. Set the timing. Take the baseline before the campaign starts. The sources opened give no fixed interval; working rule: one wave before, one after the main activity, then at the pace at which the client can act on results, at the same point in the season where the category is seasonal.
8. Freeze the instrument. Same wording, order, brand list, CEP list and answer format each wave. Romaniuk reports that well-meant changes to wording and answer formats have damaged trackers. If a change is unavoidable, run old and new versions side by side for one wave. Pretest once (S3-02) before the baseline.
9. Write expectations down before each wave (S2-11): which measures should move, in which group, by how much.

Part C. Run and read it.
10. Check the sample first. Compare each wave's make-up with the previous one: share of client-brand buyers, region, age, gender, recruitment channel. A changed mix can move scores by itself.
11. Split by buying before anything else. Romaniuk identifies past experience with the brand as the biggest source of difference in answers, larger than age or gender. Report every measure for buyers and non-buyers of the brand separately.
12. Benchmark against size. Put brands in order of share or penetration. Expect large brands to lead on awareness, associations and word of mouth, and small brands to trail (the Double Jeopardy pattern, S4-18). A small brand with scores normal for its size is not in trouble; a large one is not strong just because it leads.
13. Test changes (S4-10). Treat a change as real only if it clears sampling error, appears within buyer and non-buyer groups, and is not shared by all brands. A published practitioner note describes all brands rising or falling together as a wave effect, not a brand effect. Working rule: ask for confirmation in a second wave before acting on a small change.
14. Expect stability and lag. An Admap review by Neil Barnard found tracking results very stable for established brands, tilted towards dominant brands, and tending to follow changes in buying instead of leading them; it also cites early Ehrenberg work in which intention-to-buy scores understated brands about to grow. Attitudes often follow experience. So a flat line after a campaign is common and is not proof of failure, and a rise in attitude may reflect new buyers.
15. Report with the template, including measures that did not move, state limits, and return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE for illustration. No real data.
Input: a juice brand ran a baseline wave before a spring campaign and a second wave after it; 400 category buyers each time through the same Telegram panel and quotas. Prompted awareness rose from 62% to 65%. The client asks whether the campaign worked.

| Group | Wave 1 n | Aware | % | Wave 2 n | Aware | % |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Buyers of the brand | 120 | 114 | 95.0% | 140 | 133 | 95.0% |
| Non-buyers | 280 | 134 | 47.9% | 260 | 127 | 48.8% |
| All | 400 | 248 | 62.0% | 400 | 260 | 65.0% |

Check: 114 + 134 = 248; 133 + 127 = 260. Sampling error of the difference: square root of (0.62 x 0.38 / 400 + 0.65 x 0.35 / 400) = square root of 0.001158 = 0.034; times 1.96 = about 6.7 points.
Output: FACT: total awareness rose 3 points, inside a margin of about 6.7 points. FACT: within buyers it did not change (95.0%), and within non-buyers it rose 0.9 points (47.9% to 48.8%), far inside the margin for a group of that size; the share of brand buyers in the sample rose from 30% to 35%. INTERPRETATION: the rise in the total comes mostly from more brand buyers in wave 2, not from more people knowing the brand; there is no evidence yet that the campaign raised awareness. Whether the larger buyer share is real growth must be checked against sales data. Next: keep the instrument unchanged, repeat after the summer activity, and plan n = 1,000 if a change of about 4 to 5 points needs to be detected.

## Common mistakes
- Building the tracker around the client brand and two rivals. Surveying the client's customers or followers only.
- Changing wording, brand list or sample source between waves. Explaining every one- or two-point movement with a story.
- Comparing total scores when the share of brand buyers in the sample changed. Judging a small brand against the scores of the market leader.
- Reading awareness or attitude changes as proof of advertising effect on sales. Starting to track after the campaign has begun, so no baseline exists.

## Quality checklist
- Was the baseline taken before the activity started? (yes/no)
- Could any brand in the category use this questionnaire unchanged? (yes/no)
- Are small and large brands on the list? (yes/no)
- Are sample source, screener, quotas and wording identical across waves? (yes/no)
- Was the detectable change stated before fieldwork? (yes/no)
- Is every measure shown for buyers and non-buyers separately? (yes/no)
- Are scores read against brand size? (yes/no)
- Are changes tested and unconfirmed ones marked as possible noise? (yes/no)

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Field in Uzbek and Russian with checked equivalent wording (S3-03) and keep the language split constant between waves. Show brand names as they appear locally, in both scripts where a brand uses both.
- Agreeing answer habits can raise prompted awareness for every brand. Working rule: add one invented brand name to the list and report its score as a guide to over-claiming.
- A Telegram-bot panel misses older and rural buyers and its make-up can drift as channels grow; record the recruitment source each wave and report Tashkent and regions separately where numbers allow. Seasonal events can shift category buying; compare the same point in the season where possible.
- The size patterns used as benchmarks were established mostly in Western markets; treat them as hypotheses until local waves confirm them.
