# Insight definition and insight tests (S5-03)

This skill gives a working definition of "insight" for marketing strategy and a set of tests to apply to any candidate. The core definition comes from the strategist Mark Pollard (Strategy Is Your Words, 2020), who describes an insight as "an unspoken human truth" that lets you see the problem in a new way. Other credible definitions are used alongside it: Jeremy Bullmore (WPP) calls an insight a new understanding, usually of behaviour or attitude, that can guide action; Nielsen Norman Group and AnswerLab define research insights as explanations of why a pattern exists. The Account Planning Group (APG) has also published practitioner views that warn against treating insight as one fixed thing. Evidence grade: C, a practitioner tool with little formal testing. The profession does not agree on one definition, and the tests below are a compiled checklist, not a validated instrument. Use them to improve and filter statements, not to certify them.

Always answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label facts and interpretations, and return to the research-orchestrator skill when the tests are done.

## When to use it / When not to use it

Use it when:
- The team has candidate insights (from S5-02 or a workshop) and must choose which to build on.
- A brief or deck contains a line labelled "insight" and someone doubts it.
- A client or creative team asks why a statement deserves the name.

Do not use it when:
- There is no research or evidence yet. The tests judge a statement; they do not produce data.
- The need is a plain, useful finding. A true and useful finding does not have to pass as an insight.

## What you need before starting (inputs)

- The candidate statements, each in one or two sentences.
- The problem they are meant to shed light on (human problem and business problem).
- The evidence behind each: findings, quotes, data, with sources.
- Who the audience is (which people the statement is about).

## Procedure

1. Fix the working definition with the user. Propose: "An insight is a truth about people, usually not said out loud, backed by evidence, that makes us see the problem differently and opens a direction." Ask whether the client uses another definition. If so, record it and test against both.

2. Rewrite each candidate cleanly. One or two sentences, about people, in plain words, no brand name, no "should". If it cannot be rewritten that way, classify it now:
   - A number or pattern: finding.
   - A single quote or seen behaviour: observation.
   - A statement about the product: advantage or product truth.
   - A statement of what to do: implication or strategy.
   - A line that sounds good but claims nothing: slogan.

3. Run the eight tests. Score each pass, partial or fail, with one line of reasoning.

   Test 1, human: Is it about people (motives, beliefs, fears, habits, social rules), not about the market, the category or the brand?

   Test 2, unspoken: Is it something the audience would not normally say unprompted, or would be slightly uncomfortable to admit? If participants said it freely and often, it is a stated reason, so a finding.

   Test 3, true: Is there evidence? Name at least two supports (findings, data, observed behaviour). Also name the best counter-evidence. Fail if support is only the team's feeling.

   Test 4, new light: Does it change how the team understands the problem? Compare "what we thought before" with "what we think now". Pollard's reported pattern "we thought x, but actually y" is a quick way to write this. If before and after are the same, fail.

   Test 5, recognition: Would a member of the audience recognise themselves in it? Bullmore's image applies: a good insight is like a refrigerator, a light comes on when you look in. Check with member checking where possible; otherwise mark "not yet checked".

   Test 6, reaction: Read it to people outside the project. Pollard looks for an emotional response, such as awkwardness or the feeling of hearing a confession. A polite nod and no questions suggests a finding. Record real reactions; do not guess them.

   Test 7, potency of wording: Bullmore separates low-potency insights (accurate, dry, forgotten) from high-potency ones (vivid, memorable, easy to pass on). Is the sentence short, concrete and free of jargon? Can someone repeat it after hearing it once? Improve wording without changing the claim or outrunning the evidence.

   Test 8, useful: Does it open a direction for the problem and the brand? Can the team name at least two different things that could be done because of it? If nothing follows, it may be true but not usable here.

4. Decide.
   - Fails test 1 or test 3: not an insight. Reclassify and send back.
   - Passes 1 and 3 but fails 4: it is a finding. Keep it as a finding.
   - Passes 1, 3 and 4 but is weak on 2, 5, 6 or 7: candidate insight; rework wording or dig further with "why?".
   - Passes 1, 3, 4 and 8 and most others: working insight. State remaining doubts.
   Never present a pass as proof. Write "working insight, support: strong/medium/weak".

5. Rework the near-misses. For each candidate that almost passes:
   - Ask "why?" two or three more times past the stated reason.
   - Look for the tension: what people want versus what they do, or what they say versus what they feel.
   - Try the forms "we thought x, but actually y" and "X is really Y".
   - Go back to the data for behaviour that supports the sharper version. If the data do not support it, keep the weaker, true version.

6. Guard against over-polish. APG speakers have warned about insights that favour novelty over being right and about treating one insight as the whole truth. After rewording, re-run test 3. If the vivid version says more than the evidence, pull it back.

7. Report. Give the user a table of candidates with test results, the decision for each, and what would raise confidence. Then return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## Worked example

INVENTED example for illustration only. A furniture maker in Tashkent sells sofas to households. Problem: buyers visit the showroom, like the sofas, and then buy a cheaper one at a bazaar.

Candidates:
- C1: "70 percent of visitors compare our price with bazaar prices."
- C2: "People want quality furniture at a fair price."
- C3: "A sofa is bought for the guests' eyes, but the buyer answers to the family for the price. So the buyer needs a reason he can say out loud at home, and 'it looked better' is not one."

Results:

| Test | C1 | C2 | C3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Human | fail (market fact) | pass | pass |
| 2 Unspoken | n/a | fail (anyone says it) | pass (not said directly in interviews) |
| 3 True | pass (survey) | pass, trivially | partial: 7 of 14 interviews show the buyer explaining the price to relatives; 2 counter-cases |
| 4 New light | fail | fail | pass: we thought "too expensive", now "hard to justify aloud" |
| 5 Recognition | n/a | pass | not yet checked |
| 6 Reaction | nod | nod | two sales staff laughed and gave examples |
| 7 Potency | n/a | low | medium; shorten |
| 8 Useful | limited | none | pass: sayable reasons, proof to show at home |

Decision: C1 is a finding (keep). C2 is a truism (drop). C3 is a working insight, support: medium. Next: member check with five recent buyers; look at the two counter-cases.

## Common mistakes

- Labelling a statistic or a category fact as an insight.
- Truisms that are true of every category ("people want value").
- Statements about the brand or product presented as human truths.
- Choosing the most surprising line instead of the best-supported one.
- Polishing the wording until it claims more than the data.
- Rejecting useful findings because they are "not insights".

## Quality checklist

- Was a working definition agreed and written down?
- Is each candidate one or two plain sentences about people?
- Does each test have a recorded result and a reason?
- Are at least two pieces of evidence and one counter-case named for each working insight?
- Is there a clear before/after change in how the problem is understood?
- Were reactions real, or marked "not yet checked"?
- Is a support level (strong, medium, weak) attached to every working insight?
- Are rejected candidates reclassified, not just deleted?

## Notes for Uzbekistan

- Test wording in the language the audience uses. A line that is sharp in Russian may be flat or rude in Uzbek, and the reverse. Keep supporting quotes in the original.
- For test 2 and test 6, expect polite agreement in group settings. Prefer one-to-one reactions and watch for what people add after agreeing (examples, stories), not the agreement itself.
- Run test 5 separately for Tashkent and regional audiences when the project covers both, and report results separately.
