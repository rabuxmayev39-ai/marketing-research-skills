# Prefer behaviour over stated reasons (S1-23)
This is a selection rule, not a data-collection technique. It says: when a research question is about what people do or why they do it, first look for evidence of actual behaviour (records, observation, a test, or an account of a specific past action) and only then ask for opinions, intentions or reasons. The rule rests on Richard Nisbett and Timothy Wilson's 1977 review in Psychological Review, which showed that people often cannot report accurately what influenced their own behaviour and answer from plausible theories about causes instead of from access to the process. It is supported by the intention-behaviour gap literature (Sheeran 2002; Sheeran and Webb 2016) and by work on purchase intentions (Morwitz and colleagues). Evidence grade: A, tested in peer-reviewed research over decades, so apply the rule with confidence. Its limit is practical: when no behaviour exists yet, as with a new product, stated data are still needed and must be handled as weaker evidence. The rule also does not say that people cannot tell you anything; they can report what they did, know, think and feel far better than why.

Working rules for the assistant:
- Answer in the language the user writes in (Uzbek, Russian or English).
- Label every statement as FACT (observed or recorded) or INTERPRETATION (including buyers' own explanations).
- When the work is finished, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- Choosing a method for any research question at the S1 stage, or reviewing a draft questionnaire or interview guide.
- A brief contains "find out why customers...", "would they buy...", "how much would they pay...", "what matters most to them...". Findings from stated reasons are about to be used for a costly decision.
Do not use it as a reason to:
- Drop surveys and interviews altogether. Asking is the right tool for what people know, think of and feel now (awareness, brand links, satisfaction), and for accounts of specific events.
- Refuse stated data when no behaviour exists. Use it, calibrate it where possible, and label it.
- Dismiss what buyers say as worthless. Their explanations are data about how they see the purchase and are useful for message language.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
- The list of research questions from the brief (S2-01), and the decision each serves.
- An inventory of behavioural data the client already holds: sales by customer, CRM stages, site and social analytics, enquiry logs, loyalty data, returns.
- What can be observed or tested in the time available (outlets, channels, a landing page, a price offer). Any draft questionnaire or interview guide.
- For intention questions: past cases where the client asked a similar question and later saw real results.

## Procedure
Part A. Decide what kind of evidence each question needs.
1. Classify every research question into one of four types. Did: what people have done (bought, switched, visited, paid). Will: what they will do (buy a new product, accept a price). Why: what caused the behaviour. Holds: what they know, think of, believe or feel now.
2. Apply the evidence ladder (working rule of this skill, built on the sources). For each question, go down only as far as needed:
   - Level 1, records of behaviour: sales, logs, analytics (S1-03).
   - Level 2, observation or a test: watch the purchase (S1-06, S1-13), or change one thing and measure (S1-09).
   - Level 3, accounts of specific recent actions: "the last time you bought..." (S1-04, S1-07, S1-16, S1-17).
   - Level 4, stated intentions and choices under trade-off (S1-10, S1-11, S1-12), calibrated where possible.
   - Level 5, stated reasons, general habits ("usually"), hypotheticals and importance ratings.
   Decision rule: Did questions should be answered at levels 1 to 3. Will questions at level 2 if a test can be built, otherwise level 4. Why questions by inference from levels 1 to 3 across people and groups, with level 5 kept as the buyer's own theory. Holds questions may be asked directly.
3. Check where self-report on causes is weakest. Nisbett and Wilson found accuracy more likely when the influence is salient and a plausible cause, and list conditions that reduce it: time has passed; the cause lies in the mechanics of judgment such as order, position, contrast or anchoring; the cause is context; the cause is something that did not happen; the cause is non-verbal; or the cause seems too small for the effect. If the suspected driver falls in one of these (shelf position, the first price seen, a reply that never came, who else was present), do not rely on asking; observe or test.
4. Decide what to do when no behaviour exists. Options in order: create behaviour with a small test (pre-order page, trial batch in a few outlets, two price lists); find a neighbouring behaviour (what they bought last time for the same need); ask for intentions with safeguards (step 8). State which option was chosen and why.

Part B. Plan and run it.
5. Rewrite the method plan. For each question record the type, the level of evidence chosen, the method ID, and what would count as an answer. Tell the user where a cheaper stated-answer method was replaced and what it costs in time.
6. Rewrite questions aimed at people. Turn general and hypothetical questions into questions about one real, recent event. (a) "How do you usually choose?" becomes "Tell me about the last time you bought. What happened first?" (b) "Why did you choose us?" becomes "What were you using before? What happened on the day you decided to change? What else did you look at?" (c) "Would you buy...?" becomes "Have you bought anything like this in the last year? What, where, for how much?"
   Keep the recall period short and name it; the review notes that reports become more stereotyped as time passes.
7. Handle reasons as theories. Record a stated reason with the tag "buyer's explanation". Look for the cause in what differs between those who acted and those who did not, in the sequence of events, or in a test. Nisbett and Wilson found that observers who only read a description gave much the same explanations as the people who had been through the experience, which is why a familiar-sounding reason is weak evidence.
8. Handle intentions as inflated and uneven. What the sources support:
   - Across 422 studies summarised by Sheeran (2002), intentions and later behaviour correlated at about 0.53, and intenders acted roughly half the time. The review covers many kinds of behaviour, not purchases specifically, so use the figure as a caution, not as a conversion rate.
   - Experiments that raised intentions by a medium-to-large amount produced only a small-to-medium change in behaviour (Webb and Sheeran 2006, reported in Sheeran and Webb 2016).
   - Intentions are followed less when the behaviour is habitual and more when the intention is stable.
   - For forecasting, Armstrong, Morwitz and Kumar found purchase intentions did improve on simple extrapolation of past sales when they were adjusted against past results; raw shares of intenders can overstate or understate purchase.
   So: ask likelihood on a probability scale (Romaniuk's B2B report describes the 11-point Juster scale as a better predictor than standard intention scales), calibrate with the client's own past ratio of stated intention to real purchase when one exists, and present uncalibrated figures as an upper indication only.
9. Write expectations before fieldwork (S2-11), then compare what people said with what they or others did (S4-08). Where the two disagree, behaviour decides the FACT and the gap itself becomes a finding.
10. In the report, mark every finding with its evidence level (1 to 5). Do not let a level 5 finding carry a decision alone; attach a planned check. Return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE for illustration. No real data.
Input: a juice brand drafts a Telegram-bot survey before launching a 1.5 litre family pack. Draft questions: Q1 "Why do you choose our juice?", Q2 "What is most important to you when buying juice?", Q3 "Would you buy a 1.5 litre pack?", Q4 "Which juice brands do you know?"

| Draft | Type | Problem | Replacement and level |
|---|---|---|---|
| Q1 | Why | Asks for a cause; answers will be plausible theories | Interviews on the last purchase with switchers (S1-07), level 3; compare outlets where the brand sells well and badly, level 1 |
| Q2 | Why | Importance ratings, general | "Which juice did you buy last time, where, what size, for what occasion?", level 3 |
| Q3 | Will | Hypothetical; no behaviour yet | Trial in 20 shops for four weeks, level 2; keep an intention question on a 0 to 10 scale, level 4 |
| Q4 | Holds | None; awareness can be asked | Keep, with a prompted brand list |

Calibration of Q3 (invented numbers): at an earlier launch, 45% of surveyed buyers gave a top intention score and 9% of the same panel bought within three months, a ratio of 9 / 45 = 0.2. Now 40% give a top score: 40% x 0.2 = 8%.
Output: FACT: 40% state a top intention. INTERPRETATION: about 8% may buy in three months, if the earlier ratio holds for this pack and this sample; the shop trial is the stronger evidence and decides the launch volume. Q1 answers, if collected, are reported as buyers' explanations and used for message wording only.

## Common mistakes
- Asking "why" and reporting the answers as causes. Asking what people "usually" do or "would" do when a last-time question was possible.
- Running a survey when the client's own sales or CRM data already hold the answer.
- Reading the share of "definitely would buy" as a sales forecast. Throwing out all stated data, including awareness and satisfaction, where asking is appropriate.
- Treating importance ratings as drivers of choice without checking against behaviour (S4-14). Ignoring influences people cannot notice: order, placement, first price seen, things that did not happen.

## Quality checklist
- Is every research question classified as Did, Will, Why or Holds? (yes/no)
- Was existing behavioural data checked before new fieldwork was planned? (yes/no)
- For each Did and Why question, is at least one source at levels 1 to 3? (yes/no)
- Are questions to people about a specific, recent, named event? (yes/no)
- Are stated reasons tagged as buyers' explanations? (yes/no)
- Are intention figures calibrated, or marked as uncalibrated upper indications? (yes/no)
- Does every finding carry its evidence level, with no decision resting on level 5 alone? (yes/no)

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Polite or agreeing answer habits add to the gap between stated and actual behaviour; event questions ("what did you buy last time?") are less exposed to this than opinion questions. Ask event questions in the respondent's own language, Uzbek or Russian, and keep the account in the original wording.
- Many small businesses keep sales records on paper or in messaging apps; ask to see them before designing a survey, and treat them as level 1 evidence with gaps noted.
- A Telegram-bot survey reaches only part of the market and misses older and rural people; behaviour data from shops or dealers in the regions can cover what the bot cannot.
- The studies behind this rule used mostly Western samples. The direction of the rule is safe to apply; treat specific figures, such as how often intentions are followed, as hypotheses here and build local calibration ratios over time.
