# Client and stakeholder interviews about past behaviour (S0-12)

This method is a way of interviewing the client's own people (owner, sales head, finance, production, front-line sellers, distributors) at the start of a project so that the agency leaves with facts: what happened, when, what was tried, what it cost, what data exist. It borrows its rules from Rob Fitzpatrick's short handbook The Mom Test (2013), written for founders talking to customers. His point is that people give kind and optimistic answers when asked for opinions, so the questions must be ones that even a person who wants to please you cannot answer misleadingly: about their life and work, about specific past events, with the interviewer talking little. Applying these rules to a client's staff instead of to customers is this skill's own adaptation (Working rule). The structure of stakeholder interviews (whom to see, which topics to cover) follows a Nielsen Norman Group guide.

Evidence grade: C. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. The Mom Test is a practitioner book based on its author's experience; no formal test of the rules was found among the sources opened for this skill, and the book itself was not opened (it is paid), so its content is reported through two independent summaries. Its listed limit: the result depends on the interviewer's skill. Criticism from one published reviewer: the advice is partly inconsistent (no pitching, yet every meeting should end with a request for commitment), some techniques feel awkward, it assumes easy access to interviewees, and it suggests arranging talks under a pretext, which this skill does not allow (S2-10). Treat what comes out as leads to verify against records, not as evidence.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (a record, a dated event, a number someone can show) versus INTERPRETATION (anyone's explanation, including the owner's). Never write a stakeholder's opinion into the diagnosis as a fact. When the interview summary is written, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- A new project starts and the brief is short, vague or already contains the solution ("we need a new campaign").
- Preparing the first clarification meeting and deciding what to ask and whom.
- Different people in the client company explain the problem differently.

Do not use it when:
- The people to interview are customers. Use S1-04, S1-07 and S3-04.
- The facts can be read from documents the client has already sent. Read them first (S1-03).
- The aim is a formal map of who takes part in a business customer's purchase (S1-18).

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. The client's brief or first message, word for word.
2. Everything the client has sent: sales tables, CRM exports, past research, media plans.
3. A list of people who hold different parts of the picture, and permission to talk to each alone.
4. Two agency people per interview: one leads, one takes notes (the book's advice, as summarised).
5. Thirty to forty-five minutes per person (Working rule).

## Procedure
1. Do the desk work first. Read all materials and remove from your list any question that documents can answer; the book, as summarised, gives this as a preparation rule. Draft the facts, assumptions, unknowns register (S0-13); the interviews exist to move items out of "assumed" and "unknown".
2. Choose whom to interview. The Nielsen Norman Group guide says to include anyone with an interest in the project or whose cooperation is needed, to speak one to one, and to ask each person who else to see. Working rule: at least one person who decides, one who holds the numbers and one who meets customers daily.
3. Set the three most important things to learn. The book advises agreeing the top three questions before each conversation. Write them as gaps in knowledge ("what changed in the month sales fell"), not as questions to read aloud.
4. Write the guide in four blocks (adapted from the Nielsen Norman Group topics): success (what result is wanted, which numbers are tracked today); history (what happened, when it was first noticed, what was tried, what it cost, what the result was); priorities and constraints (budget, deadlines, what cannot change); process (who decides, who must agree, how they want to be kept informed).
5. Turn every opinion question into a past-event question. The summarised rule: ask about specific past events, not habits in general or the future. Working conversions: "Why are sales falling?" becomes "In which month did you first see it, and in which report?"; "Do customers like the product?" becomes "Tell me about the last customer who refused. What did they say and do next?"; "Would a discount help?" becomes "When did you last run a discount, and what did the sales table show?"
6. Open the meeting. Explain the purpose and the time needed, and how answers will be used; the Nielsen Norman Group guide adds telling the person their answers will not be attributed to them. Do not present the agency's ideas; the first rule, as summarised, is to talk about their world and not your idea.
7. During the talk, sort what you hear. The book, as summarised, names three kinds of misleading data: compliments; fluff, meaning general statements ("we usually"), promises ("we will") and hypotheticals ("we might"); and ideas or requests. For fluff, ask for the last real case. For a solution request, ask what problem it would solve and what happened the last time that problem showed. Stay neutral and do not interrupt.
8. Ask for the data behind each claim: "Is that in a report? Can we see it?" Working rule: a number nobody can show is recorded as an assumption with the speaker's name.
9. Close with two questions from the summaries: who else should we talk to, and is there anything we should have asked? Then ask for a concrete next step. The book treats willingness to give time, reputation or money as the test of seriousness; here the equivalents are access to data, an introduction to another stakeholder and a date for the next meeting (Working rule).
10. On the same day, write the summary: facts with sources, claims by person, contradictions between people, data promised. Keep quotes exact and in the original language. With more than five interviews, the Nielsen Norman Group guide suggests coding and grouping the notes into themes (S4-01).
11. Update S0-13, list the questions for the clarification meeting, and return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. A housing developer in Tashkent. Brief: "Sales have dropped, our advertising is weak, we need a new campaign." Five interviews were held: owner, sales head, marketing manager, finance, one sales consultant.

```
CLAIMS (INTERPRETATION, by person)
  Owner: "the advertising stopped working"
  Marketing manager: "sales staff do not handle leads properly"
FACTS OBTAINED (shown in the CRM export and the sales ledger)
                      Q1        Q2      change
  Leads             1,200     1,150     -4.2%
  Contracts            96        58    -39.6%
  Lead-to-contract   8.0%      5.0%
  Finance: first payment raised from 30% to 50% on 1 April
  Sales consultant, last 3 refusals: all three asked about the first
    payment and left after hearing the figure
CONTRADICTION: the owner blames advertising, but lead numbers are almost
  flat; the drop is after the lead.
NEW ASSUMPTION for S0-13: the higher first payment, not advertising,
  explains most of the fall. Evidence: timing + 3 cases (weak).
DATA PROMISED: refusal reasons from CRM by 12 June (sales head)
NEXT: clarification meeting question - can payment terms be changed?
```
Arithmetic check: 1,200 - 1,150 = 50; 50 / 1,200 = 4.17%, rounded 4.2%; 96 - 58 = 38; 38 / 96 = 39.58%, rounded 39.6%; 96 / 1,200 = 8.0%; 58 / 1,150 = 5.04%, rounded 5.0%. Three refusal stories are not a count; they only point to what to check.

## Common mistakes
- Asking the client what they think the cause is and writing the answer down as the diagnosis.
- Asking about the future ("would customers buy if...") instead of what already happened.
- Interviewing only the owner or only the marketing manager.
- Group meetings where junior staff repeat what the senior person said.
- Presenting the agency's solution in the first meeting and collecting compliments.
- Accepting numbers from memory without asking for the report.
- Leaving with no next step: no data promised, no introduction, no date.
- Writing notes days later, with paraphrase in place of the speaker's words.

## Quality checklist
- Were existing documents read first and document-answerable questions removed?
- Were at least a decision-maker, a holder of numbers and a customer-facing person interviewed, each alone?
- Were three learning goals written before each interview?
- Is every important claim tied to a dated event, a record or a named speaker?
- Are facts, claims and contradictions listed separately?
- Did each interview end with a concrete next step?
- Was S0-13 updated on the same day?

## Output template
```
CLIENT / STAKEHOLDER INTERVIEW SUMMARY - [client] - [date]
Interviewed: name, role, date, language | lead / note-taker
Three things we needed to learn: 1 ... 2 ... 3 ...
FACTS (record or dated event; source; who can show it): ...
CLAIMS (INTERPRETATION; who said it; exact quote in original language): ...
WHAT WAS TRIED BEFORE: action | when | cost | result | evidence
CONTRADICTIONS between people or between claim and record: ...
DATA THAT EXIST: what | who holds it | promised by [date]
DECISION PROCESS: who decides | who must agree | constraints
Still unknown -> S0-13: ...
Questions for the clarification meeting: ...
```

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Practical caution: staff may hesitate to contradict the owner in front of others. Interview people separately and say how their words will be used.
- Polite or agreeing answers are likely to opinion questions; past-event questions and requests to see the report reduce this.
- Let each person choose Uzbek or Russian; keep quotes in the original language and translate only in the report (S3-03).
- Hypothesis to check for each client: records may sit in spreadsheets, notebooks or Telegram chats, not in a CRM system. Ask where the information lives.
- Ask before recording; some people speak less freely on a recording. Written notes by a second person are the fallback.
- The Mom Test was written for start-up founders in Western markets. Treat its rules here as working rules.
