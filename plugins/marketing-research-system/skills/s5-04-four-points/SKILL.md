# Four Points framework: problem, insight, advantage, strategy (S5-04)

The Four Points framework is a one-page way to write a brand or communication strategy as four connected sentences: the human problem, an insight that opens the problem up, the brand's advantage, and the strategy that results. It comes from the strategist Mark Pollard, who teaches it through his Sweathead training and in the book Strategy Is Your Words (2020). Its purpose is to force a team to say its thinking briefly and to give a creative team a clear thread to work from. Evidence grade: C, a practitioner tool with little formal testing. It is a writing and thinking discipline, not a proven predictor of results. The four sentences are only as good as the research under them, so treat the output as a hypothesis to check, and keep the supporting evidence attached.

Always answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label what is fact and what is interpretation, and return to the research-orchestrator skill when the page is agreed.

## When to use it / When not to use it

Use it when:
- Research and analysis are done and there are tested insights (S5-03) and known product facts.
- The team must brief a creative team or explain the strategic idea to a client in a few lines.
- A long deck exists and nobody can say the strategy in one breath.

Do not use it when:
- There is no research yet. The framework will then only arrange guesses.
- The task is a full business strategy (where to compete, pricing, channels, capabilities). Use S6-08 or S0-01.
- The decision is a choice between several strategic options. Test the options first with S5-05.

## What you need before starting (inputs)

- The business problem and target (from the client brief).
- The audience definition.
- Findings and at least one working insight with evidence.
- A list of true, checkable facts about the brand or product, and what competitors offer.
- Constraints: budget, timing, what the client will not change.

## Procedure

1. Write the business problem as context. One sentence with a number if there is one ("Reorders from regional bakeries are 20 percent of trials"). This is not one of the four points. It stays above them.

2. Point 1, problem: the human problem behind the business problem.
   - Ask: "What is going on in people's minds or lives that causes the business problem?" Pollard describes this as the obstacle or criticism that stops people buying or wanting the thing.
   - Ask "why?" repeatedly until the answer is about people, not about the company.
   - Write it from the customer's side, in plain words, without the brand's metrics.
   - Avoid table-stakes problems such as "low awareness" unless research shows that is truly the cause; they describe the business symptom.
   - Produce two or three versions. Ask the user which is best supported by the research, not which sounds best.

3. Point 2, insight: an unspoken human truth that sheds new light on the problem.
   - Take working insights from S5-03. If none exist, stop and go back; do not invent one here.
   - Choose the insight that explains the problem in point 1. If the insight is true but about a different problem, either change the problem or set the insight aside.
   - It must be about people, not the brand, and one sentence.
   - Keep its evidence and support level beside it.

4. Point 3, advantage: what the brand truly has that people find motivating.
   - List product, service and company facts. For each ask: is it true today, can we prove it, do competitors have it too, does the audience care?
   - Pollard frames this as a sentence making the case for the brand's key features, from the side of what the customer finds compelling.
   - Choose the fact that becomes meaningful because of the insight. A feature unrelated to the insight does not belong here, however proud the client is of it.
   - Replace abstract nouns ("quality", "innovation", "easy") with the concrete thing.
   - Mark it as fact, with proof.

5. Point 4, strategy: the new way of seeing the brand that solves the problem.
   - Pollard's rule: the strategy solves the problem by bringing the insight and the advantage together. He defines strategy as "an informed opinion about how to win".
   - Write one short sentence. Useful forms from Pollard's teaching: "Show that X is Y" and "It's not X, it's Y".
   - Write at least five versions. Then select.
   - The sentence is a direction for creative work, not a slogan and not a list of activities.

6. Check the links. Read the four points aloud in order and check:
   - Does the insight explain the problem?
   - Does the advantage answer the insight?
   - Does the strategy contain both the insight and the advantage, and does it solve the stated problem?
   - Do all four talk about the same subject? If one point could be removed without the others changing, it is not connected.
   Fix the weakest link, which often means rewriting the problem.

7. Stress tests.
   - Swap test: put a competitor's name on the strategy. If it still works, the advantage is not specific enough.
   - Evidence test: for each point, name the source. Problem and insight need research; advantage needs proof.
   - Use test: give the page to someone who will make the work. If they can start producing ideas, it works. If they ask what it means, rewrite.
   - Honesty test: does the strategy promise something the product cannot deliver?

8. Offer alternatives. Where the research supports more than one reading, produce two or three complete sets of four points, not one. State which is best supported and which is most risky. Let the team choose.

9. Close. Record the chosen set, the evidence, and open doubts. Then return to the research-orchestrator skill.

Decision rules:
- No evidence for the insight: do not finish the page; return to analysis.
- Advantage is not unique: either find a different true advantage or accept a strategy built on saying a shared truth better, and tell the user this openly.
- Strategy longer than about fifteen words: it is probably two strategies. Split and choose.

## Worked example

INVENTED example for illustration only. A housing developer in Tashkent builds mid-priced apartment blocks. All names and facts below are made up.

Business problem (context): site visits are high, but only a small share of visitors sign a contract within three months.

| Point | Sentence | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Problem | Buyers fear paying for years for a flat that may be finished late or not as promised. | Interpretation, from 16 interviews |
| Insight | When people cannot judge a building, they judge the builder's behaviour: every small broken promise during the sale is read as a forecast of the big one. | Interpretation, support: medium |
| Advantage | The developer has handed over its last four blocks on the contract date and publishes weekly site photos. | Fact, documents available |
| Strategy | Show that this developer keeps small promises, so the big one is believable. | Judgement |

Link check: the insight explains the fear; the advantage is proof of kept promises; the strategy joins them. Swap test: a competitor without the on-time record could not claim it. Open doubt: the insight needs member checking with recent buyers.

Rejected version: Strategy "Quality homes for modern families". It fails the swap test and uses neither insight nor advantage.

## Common mistakes

- Writing the business problem (sales, awareness, share) as the human problem.
- An "insight" that is a statistic or a statement about the brand.
- An advantage the brand hopes to have, or one every competitor shares.
- A strategy that is a slogan, or a list of tactics.
- Four good sentences that do not connect to each other.
- Filling the page before the research supports it.
- Offering only one version and defending it.

## Quality checklist

- Is the business problem kept as context, outside the four points?
- Is the problem written from the customer's side?
- Did the insight pass S5-03, and is its support level shown?
- Is the advantage true today and provable?
- Does the strategy join insight and advantage in one short sentence?
- Does the page pass the swap test?
- Could someone outside the project start working from it?
- Are facts and interpretations labelled, with sources?

## Works with

- S5-02 insight ladder and S5-03 insight tests: supply and qualify the insight.
- S5-01 affinity diagram: source of the problem language in customers' words.
- S5-06 means-end value map: helps link the advantage (attribute) to what people value.
- S5-05 what would have to be true: test each alternative set of four points before choosing.
- S6-02 positioning statement: turns the chosen strategy into a formal positioning.
- S6-08 strategy choice cascade and S0-01 strategy kernel: the wider business strategy the four points must fit inside.
- S5-13 pyramid storyline: presents the four points and their evidence to the client.
- S5-14 member checking: check problem and insight wording with real customers.

## Notes for Uzbekistan

- Write the four points in the language the creative team works in, and check the meaning in the other language (Uzbek or Russian). A strategy line that depends on wordplay in one language may not survive translation.
- Keep customer quotes under "evidence" in the original language.
- Clients may agree politely in the meeting and raise doubts later. Ask each decision-maker separately which point they believe least.
- If the audience includes both Tashkent and regional buyers, check whether one problem and one insight hold for both. If not, write two pages.
