# Research brief and question hierarchy (S2-01)

A research brief is the short written document that states why a study is being done, which decision it serves, what must be learned, and the practical frame (who, how, when, budget, output). In the commissioning tradition of the professional bodies (MRS in the UK, ESOMAR internationally, the Research Association New Zealand) the client writes the brief and the agency answers with a written proposal; in a small agency the two are often merged into one document that both sides sign. The question hierarchy inside the brief follows Naresh Malhotra's textbook Marketing Research: An Applied Orientation, where problem definition and the approach to the problem are the first two of six steps of the research process, and the approach has named components: an objective or theoretical framework, an analytical model, research questions, hypotheses, and a specification of the information needed.

Evidence grade: B. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. The brief is standard practice endorsed by professional bodies and textbooks, not a tested instrument. No source opened for this skill measures how much a brief improves research. Treat it as a discipline that prevents avoidable errors, and treat the hierarchy as a checking tool, not as proof that the questions are the right ones.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (what the client said or a document shows) versus INTERPRETATION (the team's reading) inside the brief. Do not fill gaps with invented client facts; write "not known" and add a question for the client. When the brief is signed off, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- A project is starting and money or time will be spent on data collection.
- The client's request names a method ("we need a survey") but not a decision.
- Several people on the client side expect different things from the study, or the scope changed mid-project and the agreed version must be rewritten.

Do not use it when:
- The decision itself is still unclear. Go back to S0-02 and S0-03 first; a brief written on a vague problem only makes the vagueness look tidy. The brief records the conversation with the decision maker; it does not replace it.
- The task is a quick desk check with no fieldwork. A three-line note of decision, question and deadline is enough (S1-03).

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. The management decision and who makes it (from S0-02), plus what the client is able and not able to change.
2. What is already known: past research, sales data, desk findings (S1-03), and the facts, assumptions and unknowns register (S0-13).
3. Deadline of the decision, not only of the report; budget range, even an approximate one.
4. Names of the people who must agree to the brief on the client side, and candidate hypotheses from the issue tree (S0-03).

## Procedure
1. Collect the raw request. Ask the user to paste the client's message or meeting notes. Mark every sentence as FACT, ASSUMPTION or WISH. If the request is only a method ("do 300 interviews"), ask: "What will you do differently depending on the result?"
2. Write the business context in five to eight lines: market, the client's position, what has been tried, and why the question arises now. The New Zealand guide calls the background and role of the research the most important part of a brief and asks for constraints on what the client can act on; include those constraints.
3. State the decision and the intended action. The MRS buyer guidance asks the brief to say what the client expects to do with the findings. Write it as a choice between named options ("enter segment A, segment B, or neither by March"). The same guidance warns to check that the stated problem is the real one and not a symptom; if in doubt, return to S0-04.
4. Separate two objectives. Business objective: the commercial goal. Research objective: what must be learned. The New Zealand guide suggests the verbs: qualitative objectives aim to understand, explore or evaluate; quantitative objectives aim to measure, record or ascertain. If the objective mixes both, plan S1-02.
5. Build the question hierarchy, top down. This skill's working rule, using Malhotra's component names: one decision; one research problem written as a broad statement; two to five specific components; one to three research questions per component; a hypothesis for each question where the team already has a guess; and for each question the information needed to answer it. Number them (RQ1, H1a, I1a) so later documents can refer to them.
6. Test each research question. Working rule: for every RQ write "If the answer is X we will ..., if Y we will ...". A question with the same action under every answer is deleted or moved to a "nice to know" list that is cut first when time is short.
7. Write hypotheses as statements that could turn out false, and record them before fieldwork (S2-11). For qualitative work write expectations instead of formal hypotheses.
8. Specify information needs before choosing questions. For each need note the type (behaviour, attitude, awareness, classification), the likely source (desk, interview, survey, observation) and whether it already exists. This step feeds S3-01 and stops questionnaire items that answer no research question.
9. Choose design and method with a reason. Malhotra's third chapter classifies designs as exploratory, descriptive or causal (S1-01). State the method, why it fits the research questions, and what it cannot show.
10. Define the target group and sample. Who counts as a respondent, which subgroups must be reported separately, how they will be reached, planned numbers and the logic behind them (S2-02, S2-03, S2-04, S2-05).
11. Add the practical frame that the professional guides list: reporting format (topline, full report, presentation, workshop), timing with real constraints, budget, existing research to be shared, and logistics. Add ethics and consent (S2-10).
12. Fix the sign-off. The MRS best-practice guide advises aligning stakeholders before a brief goes out and agreeing in advance a schedule for approving the design, the questionnaire or guide, fieldwork dates and deliverables. Record who signs, the date, and a rule that any later change to the agreed specification is written down and confirmed by both sides (the ESOMAR guideline on mutual responsibilities requires that changes be notified to the client). Deliver the brief on one to two pages, list open questions for the client separately, and return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. Input from the client, a flour mill: "Sales to bakeries are flat. Do a survey of bakers."

```
RESEARCH BRIEF (extract) - Flour mill, B2B bakers
Decision: by 1 March choose ONE of: (a) add delivery in small batches,
  (b) introduce 30-day credit, (c) change nothing and cut price by 3%.
Research problem: what stops small bakeries from making the mill their main supplier?
Components -> questions -> hypotheses -> information needed
C1 Supplier choice
  RQ1 How did bakers choose their current main supplier?
  H1  Delivery reliability outweighs a small price difference. [INTERPRETATION]  I1 Last switch story; who decided.
C2 Terms
  RQ2 Which terms (batch size, credit, price) would change the choice?
  H2  Bakeries buying under 2 tonnes a month prefer small batches to credit.  I2 Monthly volume; storage; payment terms.
C3 Size of the opportunity
  RQ3 What share of small bakeries would accept each option? I3 Counts per option by region.
Action test: RQ1 -> wording of offer; RQ2 -> option a or b; RQ3 -> go/no-go.
Method: stage 1, 12 in-depth interviews (RQ1, RQ2); stage 2, phone survey of 150 bakeries from the mill's lists (RQ3).
Timeline: brief sign-off wk 1 | interviews wk 2-3 | survey wk 4-5 | report wk 6
Budget: interviews 18 mln + survey 30 mln + analysis and report 12 mln = 60 mln UZS
Sign-off: commercial director (client), project lead (agency), date ... | Open question: is option (c) affordable?
```
Arithmetic check: 3 components, 3 research questions, 2 hypotheses; weeks 1 + 2 + 2 + 1 = 6; budget 18 + 30 + 12 = 60 mln UZS. Note what the brief did: "do a survey" became two stages, because RQ1 and RQ2 are why-questions and only RQ3 is a how-many question.

## Common mistakes
- Starting from the method the client named and writing objectives to fit it.
- Objectives such as "study the market" or "understand the consumer" with no decision attached.
- Research questions copied straight into the questionnaire as questions to respondents.
- No hypotheses, so after fieldwork every result looks expected.
- A brief agreed only orally (the MRS guidance discourages verbal-only briefing), or a hidden budget; the New Zealand guide says even a rough figure lets the design fit the means.
- Signed by a contact person who is not the decision maker, or scope added during fieldwork with no written change note.

## Quality checklist
- Is there one decision, written as a choice, with an owner and a date?
- Does every research question pass the action test?
- Does every hypothesis state something that could be false, and is it dated before fieldwork?
- Is every information need linked to a research question, and every planned question to a need?
- Is the method justified by the questions and are its limits stated?
- Are target group, sample logic, timing, budget, output format and existing knowledge included?
- Has the decision maker signed, and is there a rule for changes?

## Output template
```
RESEARCH BRIEF - [client] - [project] - version [n] - [date]
1 Business context [FACT / INTERPRETATION marked]: ...
2 Decision and intended action: options ... | owner ... | decision date ...
3 Business objective: ... | Research objective: ...
4 Question hierarchy
  Component | Research question | Hypothesis or expectation | Information needed | Source
5 Action test: RQ | if X then ... | if Y then ...
6 Design and method: type (exploratory / descriptive / causal) | method | why | limits
7 Target group and sample: who | subgroups | reach | planned n and logic
8 Output: format | language | who receives it
9 Timeline: milestone | date | who approves
10 Budget: ... | Ethics and consent: ...
11 Already known: ... | Out of scope: ... | Nice to know (cut first): ...
Sign-off: client ... | agency ... | date ... | Change rule: written, both sides
Open questions for client: ...
```

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Write the brief in the language the decision maker reads most easily (Uzbek or Russian) and keep the client's own wording of the problem in the original language.
- Clients may agree politely in a meeting and object later. Ask for written confirmation, and send a short summary of the brief in Telegram or email with the question "what is wrong here?" instead of "do you agree?".
- The person who orders research is often not the owner who decides. Ask who will read the result and get that person's view before sign-off.
- State in the sample section whether regions outside Tashkent are covered, and note that Telegram-bot survey samples miss older and rural people. The brief formats come from UK, European and US practice; how formally clients sign documents here is a hypothesis to check with each client.
