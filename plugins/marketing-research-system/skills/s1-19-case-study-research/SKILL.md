# Case study research (S1-19)
Case study research examines a small number of real cases (companies, launches, campaigns, outlets) in depth and in their own setting, using several kinds of evidence for each case, and then compares the cases to explain how or why something happens. Two authors set the standard. Robert K. Yin (Case Study Research, six editions to 2018) defined the design rules: case study protocol, multiple sources of evidence, chain of evidence, replication logic. Kathleen Eisenhardt (1989, Academy of Management Review) described an eight-step route from a set of cases to tested propositions. In marketing work the method is used for competitor analysis and benchmarking. Evidence grade: A, tested and debated in peer-reviewed research for decades, so the procedure can be trusted as a procedure. Its conclusions are a different matter: they transfer to the client by argument (the client's situation resembles the cases on the conditions that matter), never by statistics. Present every conclusion as a supported explanation, not as a market fact.

Working rules for the assistant:
- Answer in the language the user writes in (Uzbek, Russian or English).
- Label every statement as FACT (seen in a named source for a named case) or INTERPRETATION (your explanation).
- When the work is finished, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- The question starts with "how" or "why" and concerns something happening now that the team cannot control or experiment on. Yin gives these three conditions as the method's home ground.
- The decision depends on understanding a mechanism: why some competitors grow, how a benchmark company organises its dealer network, why a launch failed.
- Only a handful of relevant cases exist, and each can be reached through more than one kind of evidence.
Do not use it when:
- The team needs a number for a population (market share, share of buyers who prefer X). Use S1-08 or S4-23.
- Only one source per case is available, for example only the competitor's website. That is desk research (S1-03), not a case study.
- A controlled test is possible and cheaper (S1-09).
- The client wants proof that a practice will work for them. Cases give a reasoned expectation, not proof.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
- The decision and a how or why question from the research brief (S2-01).
- A first idea of what explains the outcome (two to four candidate factors) and at least one rival explanation.
- A list of possible cases with what is known about each, and a way to judge the outcome of interest (for example sales speed, number of outlets, survival).
- Access to at least three kinds of evidence per case (working rule of this skill). Yin lists six: documents, archival records, interviews, direct observation, participant observation, physical artefacts.
- Two people for fieldwork if possible. Eisenhardt recommends several investigators, for example one interviewing and one taking notes.

## Procedure
Part A. Decide whether the method fits.
1. Run the fit test. Ask the user: (a) Is the question how or why? (b) Is it about current, real cases we cannot manipulate? (c) Can we get several kinds of evidence per case? (d) Will the client accept an explanation with stated conditions in place of a percentage? Four yes answers: proceed. If (a) fails, choose a survey or market sizing. If (c) fails, do desk research and say so. If (d) fails, pair the cases with a survey (S1-02).
2. Choose the purpose. Benchmark (learn how a strong player does something) or explanation (why outcomes differ between players). Explanation needs contrasting cases; a benchmark can work with similar ones.

Part B. Plan and run it.
3. Write the question, the candidate factors and the team's expectations before fieldwork (S2-11). Eisenhardt advises defining the question and possibly some constructs in advance while not committing to a theory; Yin asks for propositions where the team has them. Do both: list the factors, mark them as provisional.
4. Define the case and its boundaries. State the unit (a company, one product launch, one branch), the period, and what is outside the case. If sub-units will be analysed (branches inside a company), say so; Yin warns that such embedded designs can drift into studying the sub-units only.
5. Select cases on purpose, not at random. Eisenhardt calls this theoretical sampling and suggests polar types, such as a successful and an unsuccessful player in the same market, where the process is easy to see. Yin separates literal replication (cases expected to show the same result; he suggests 2 or 3) from theoretical replication (cases expected to differ for a reason stated in advance; 4 to 6). Eisenhardt's guide for the total is 4 to 10 cases: fewer gives weak grounding, more is hard to handle. For each case write one line: why it was chosen and what result is expected.
6. Write a case study protocol. Yin's protocol has four parts: overview, field procedures, questions, outline of the report. Use the same question list for every case so the cases can be compared. The questions are addressed to the researcher ("How does this company find new dealers?"), and each lists the sources that could answer it.
7. Plan the evidence per case. Typical sources for a competitor case: documents (website, price lists, catalogues, public reports, advertising), archival records (registers, statistics, the client's own sales history), interviews (customers, dealers, former partners, industry experts), direct observation (outlet visits, S1-13 mystery shopping), physical artefacts (the product and packaging). Combine numbers and words: Eisenhardt notes that quantitative data can show a relationship and qualitative data can explain it.
8. Keep a case database and a chain of evidence, as Yin requires: one folder per case, every item dated with its source, and every statement in the report traceable to items in the folder. Working rule: a key claim about a case needs two independent sources; a claim with one source is marked "single source".
9. Collect and analyse at the same time. After each visit or interview write field notes that answer Eisenhardt's questions: what am I learning, and how does this case differ from the last one? Add a source or a question when a new theme appears; record the change in the protocol.
10. Write up each case separately first (within-case analysis): a two-page account organised by the protocol questions, with a timeline. Do not compare yet.
11. Compare cases. Eisenhardt offers three tactics that push past first impressions: (a) pick a dimension and look for similarities inside groups and differences between groups; (b) take pairs of cases and list how they are alike and different; (c) split the data by source and see whether interviews, observation and documents show the same pattern. Put the result in a cases-by-factors table (S4-05).
12. Shape the explanation with replication logic. Treat each case as a separate test: does the proposed factor appear where the outcome is present and fail to appear where it is absent? A case that does not fit is used to revise the explanation or mark its limits (S4-07). Check each rival explanation in the same table.
13. Compare with what is already known, including sources that disagree; Eisenhardt treats conflicting literature as a way to sharpen the limits of the finding. For a marketing team this means prior project cases (S8-08), published benchmarks and S4-18 norms.
14. Stop when new cases or sources add little (Eisenhardt's theoretical saturation), or when time runs out; state which.
15. State how far the findings transfer. Yin's term is analytic generalisation: the result extends to an explanation, and through it to situations that share the stated conditions. Never write "x of 6 companies, so about y% of the market". Write the conditions under which the explanation should hold for the client, and what would have to be true (S5-05).
16. Have a knowledgeable informant read the draft case descriptions for factual errors where confidentiality allows (a Yin tactic; S5-14), then report with the template and return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE for illustration. No real companies or data.
Input: a mid-size housing developer in Tashkent asks why some comparable developers sell the first building of a project much faster than others. Fit test: how/why question, current cases, several sources available, client accepts an explanation. Six cases are chosen as polar types: three fast sellers (F1 to F3) and three slow sellers (S1 to S3), all in the same price class. Sources per case: sales office visit as a mystery shopper, the project's Telegram channel and advertising for six months, price lists and payment terms, two buyer interviews, one estate agent interview.

| Factor | F1 | F2 | F3 | S1 | S2 | S3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Instalment plan published with exact figures | yes | yes | yes | no | no | yes |
| Weekly construction updates in the channel | yes | yes | yes | no | yes | no |
| Show flat open before sales start | yes | no | yes | no | no | no |
| Price below area average (rival explanation) | no | yes | no | yes | no | no |

Output: FACT: weekly construction updates appear in 3 of 3 fast cases and 1 of 3 slow cases; a published instalment plan in 3 of 3 fast and 1 of 3 slow; a show flat in 2 of 3 fast and 0 of 3 slow; a lower price in 1 of 3 fast and 1 of 3 slow. INTERPRETATION: visible proof of progress together with clear payment terms goes with fast early sales; low price does not separate the groups. S2 and S3 each have one of the two factors and still sold slowly, which suggests the two work together; this is a proposition from six cases, not a tested rule. Transfer: it should apply to the client if its buyers also pay in instalments before completion. Next step: test updates in the client's channel with S7-02.

## Common mistakes
- Picking cases because they are famous or easy to reach, with no stated reason.
- Building each case from one source, usually the company's own marketing.
- Comparing before each case has been written up on its own.
- Counting cases as if they were a sample ("four of six, so most of the market"). Dropping the case that does not fit instead of using it to set limits.
- Studying only successful cases, so that factors shared with failures look like causes. Using a different question list for each case.

## Quality checklist
- Is the question a how or why question tied to a decision? (yes/no)
- Is there a written reason and expected result for every case? (yes/no)
- Does every case rest on at least three kinds of evidence? (yes/no)
- Can each key statement be traced to dated items in the case database? (yes/no)
- Was each case written up separately before the comparison? (yes/no)
- Were rival explanations and non-fitting cases examined in the table? (yes/no)
- Is transfer stated as conditions, with no percentages projected to the market? (yes/no)

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Public documents on companies can be thin or out of date. Plan more observation and interviews, and mark claims that rest on one source.
- Much company communication runs through Telegram channels and Instagram; archive posts with dates, since they can be edited or deleted. Collect documents and interviews in Uzbek and Russian as they come; keep quotes in the original language with a translation beside them.
- Interviewees may speak politely about competitors and partners or avoid criticism. Ask about specific events and check against documents and observation.
- Cases from Tashkent may not carry over to regional cities; treat region as one of the conditions in the transfer statement.
- Explanations taken from Western benchmark companies are hypotheses here until a local case or test supports them.
