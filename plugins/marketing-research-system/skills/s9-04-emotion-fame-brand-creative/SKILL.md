# Emotion and fame in brand creative (S9-04)

This method sets what kind of creative work each layer of a communication plan needs. Its source is Les Binet and Peter Field's The Long and the Short of It (IPA, 2013), an analysis of the IPA Databank, described in the authors' summary deck as 996 campaigns for 700 brands in 83 categories. They compared campaigns that work mainly through feelings ("emotional"), campaigns that work mainly through information and argument ("rational"), and "fame" campaigns, a kind of emotional work usually described as getting the brand talked about (the deck opened gives the label and the results, not a full definition). Their finding: over periods of years, emotional campaigns were linked with more very large profit effects than rational ones, and fame campaigns were the most efficient of all, while rational messages produced a quick, short-lived sales response. The practical use is to give the brand layer an emotional, well-branded, shareable task and the activation layer a clear, rational, direct one.

Evidence grade: A, with reservations that must be told to the client. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. The grade reflects a large data set and a pattern the authors found again in their later B2B analysis; it is not an experiment. Limits: the cases are entries to the IPA Effectiveness Awards, so they are self-reported, mostly UK, mostly large brands, and not a representative sample of ordinary campaigns. Byron Sharp has called this a "very weird data set". Mark Ritson's review accepts the big-brand skew and the self-reporting as real weaknesses while judging the main conclusions sound; an IPA reviewer replies that the aim is to compare more and less effective entries, not to describe the average. The B2B version of the finding rests on fewer than 50 cases, and its authors call it tentative. So: use the direction, do not quote the percentages as forecasts, and test locally (S9-06).

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (test results, what the sources report) versus INTERPRETATION (which emotion fits, whether an idea will be talked about). Never promise an effect size. When the creative tasks are written, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- Creative briefs are being written and the plan has both a brand layer and an activation layer (S6-05).
- A client wants every advertisement to list product benefits and a price.
- Brand work is being judged by clicks or leads in its first weeks.

Do not use it when:
- The whole task is activation: a sale, a launch offer, a tender. Write a direct rational brief (S6-18, S6-15).
- The brand and activation split is not decided. Do S6-05 first; this skill does not set the split.
- The team wants a method for generating ideas. This skill sets the task and the tests, not the idea.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. Communication objectives for each layer (S9-01) and the budget split between layers (S6-05).
2. The buying situations chosen for messages (S9-02).
3. A tested insight about the audience (S5-03): what they care about around this category.
4. The brand's distinctive assets: colours, logo, sounds, characters (S6-04).
5. Facts and offers available for activation: prices, terms, deadlines, proof.

## Procedure
1. Sort every planned piece of communication into brand layer or activation layer, using the S6-05 definitions. One piece, one layer. Working rule: if a piece tries to do both, it usually has to be split into two.
2. Write the brand-layer task as a feeling plus a memory, not as an argument. Fill in: "After seeing this, [category buyers] should feel [emotion] about [buying situation], and remember that it was [brand]." The deck's reasoning is that emotional effects build up over years, while a rational message gives a spike that fades.
3. Choose the emotion from evidence. Use the insight (S5-03) and interview material, not the team's taste. Working rule: emotion means any feeling that fits the brand and the situation, including humour, warmth, pride, relief or surprise; it does not mean sad music.
4. Add the fame test. Fame campaigns in the deck were about four times as efficient as other campaigns on its measure (market share growth relative to extra share of voice, see S4-22). Working rule to judge fame potential before production: would a viewer show or retell this to someone, and would they name the brand when doing so? Ask three people outside the team.
5. Make branding non-negotiable. An emotional film that viewers credit to the category leader builds the leader. An Institute-hosted article reports that emotional content together with a prominent brand name and consistent distinctive assets is linked with higher purchase intention. Require assets early and throughout (S6-04).
6. Write the activation-layer task as a rational, direct request: who is in the market now, what offer, what proof, what single next step. The deck's chart appears to show rational campaigns ahead of fame campaigns on very large direct, short-term effects, which is why this layer is allowed to be plain.
7. Keep the two layers visibly one brand: same assets, same tone of voice, compatible promises. The deck reports that plans combining brand and activation channels were more efficient than brand channels alone.
8. Set the judging rules before launch. Brand layer: read over six months or more (working rule) on memory and brand measures (S4-19, S1-22) and base sales, not on clicks. Activation layer: read weekly on responses and cost per response. The deck's charts suggest that campaigns chosen by pre-testing did better in the short term and worse over two years or more, so do not let a short-term test alone kill brand work (S9-06 gives the test design).
9. Test locally. Because the evidence is from UK award entries, plan at least one comparison in the market, such as two cuts shown to matched audiences with both an immediate and a delayed measure (S7-02).
10. Check honesty and taste: no emotional claim the product cannot support, no use of fear or shame that would mislead (S2-10 principles, S6-15 honesty audit). Then hand the two tasks to S6-18. Return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. A housing developer in Tashkent has a brand layer and an activation layer. Two films were made and shown online to two matched audiences of 100,000 people each; four months later 300 people from each audience were surveyed.

```
CREATIVE TASKS                                                    INTERPRETATION
Brand layer:  families who will buy a flat some day should feel the relief
  of the first evening in their own home, and remember it was [brand]
  (brand colour and jingle from second 1). Fame test: 2 of 3 outsiders
  said they would send film A to a relative.
Activation layer: people looking for a flat now -> price per square metre,
  instalment terms, show-flat address, "book a visit this week".
LOCAL TEST (invented results)                                     FACT
                          Film A (emotional)   Film B (rational)
Reached                       100,000              100,000
Leads within 2 weeks              180                  300
Lead rate                        0.18%                0.30%
Name the brand unprompted,
  4 months later (n=300)      24% (72)             17% (51)
READING: B wins on immediate leads; A leads on memory after four months.
DECISION: film A style for the brand layer, film B content for activation.
```
Arithmetic check: 180 / 100,000 = 0.18%; 300 / 100,000 = 0.30%; 0.24 x 300 = 72; 0.17 x 300 = 51; 24 - 17 = 7 points. Sampling noise for two samples of 300 at these levels is about 6.4 points (1.96 x square root of (0.24 x 0.76 / 300 + 0.17 x 0.83 / 300) = 1.96 x 0.0328), so 7 points is only just above noise: a weak signal, to be repeated before large spending (S2-03).

## Common mistakes
- Quoting the UK percentages to a client as what will happen here.
- Reading "emotional" as sentimental, and producing a tearful film unrelated to the category.
- Emotional work with weak branding, remembered without the brand.
- Loading the brand film with prices, addresses and three product benefits.
- Making activation "emotional" and vague when buyers need terms and a next step.
- Judging brand creative by clicks in the first two weeks.
- Assuming an idea will be talked about because the team likes it.
- Concluding that rational information never matters; in the source it is the tool for short-term response.

## Quality checklist
- Is every piece assigned to one layer?
- Is the brand-layer task written as feeling plus buying situation plus brand memory?
- Is the chosen emotion backed by an insight from research?
- Was the fame question asked of people outside the team?
- Are distinctive assets required from the start of each piece?
- Is the activation task one offer, one proof, one next step?
- Are judging rules and time frames set per layer before launch?
- Is a local test planned and are source limits stated to the client?

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- What moves or amuses people is cultural. Treat every chosen emotion as a hypothesis and check it with local viewers in Uzbek and in Russian before production; do not translate a script word for word (S3-03).
- Humour, family scenes and religious or national themes need particular care. Practical caution: show the script to people of different ages and from outside Tashkent.
- Polite viewers may praise any film in a test. Prefer behaviour measures (watched to the end, shared, named the brand a week later) over "did you like it" (S3-07, S1-23).
- Sharing in messengers cannot be fully counted. Use forwards and view counts as rough signals only.
- Many owners judge advertising by calls this week. Agree the two sets of judging rules in writing before launch.
