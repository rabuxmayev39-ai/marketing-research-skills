# Brand building and sales activation balance (S6-05)

This method comes from Les Binet and Peter Field's The Long and the Short of It (2013), published by the Institute of Practitioners in Advertising (IPA), the UK advertising trade body. They analysed the IPA's databank of campaign case studies and concluded that communication does two different jobs. Brand building creates memories and feelings that influence purchases long after the advertising has run; it works on a broad audience, slowly, and its effects last. Sales activation asks people who are close to buying to act now, through an offer, a piece of information or a direct response; it works quickly on a narrow audience and its effects fade fast. Their headline finding is that results were best, on average, when about 60% of the budget went to brand building and about 40% to activation.

Evidence grade: A. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. The analysis is large (the authors' summary deck reports 996 campaigns, 700 brands and 83 categories) and the two-jobs logic has been repeated in later reports, one of which put the optimum at 62:38. Be honest about what kind of evidence this is. It is a pattern across case studies published by a professional body, not an experiment and not a journal-reviewed test. The cases are entries to the IPA Effectiveness Awards, so they lean towards successful campaigns, larger budgets and UK brands, and the effects are reported by the entrants themselves; critics also point to funding from media owners. Mark Ritson, reviewing these criticisms, accepts the limits and still judges the work broadly right. The 60:40 figure is an average: the authors say the best split varies by category, price level and brand size, and for B2B they estimate roughly 46:54 from fewer than 50 cases. Rely on the direction; treat the exact ratio as a starting point.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (spend by activity, the current split, measured responses) versus INTERPRETATION (the target split, expected effects). Never present 60:40 as a law. When the split and the measurement plan are written, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- An annual or half-year communication budget is being divided between activities.
- A client judges all marketing by leads or sales in the same week and wants to cut whatever does not show them.
- Performance advertising costs are rising or results have flattened, and the question is whether the brand is too weak.

Do not use it when:
- The total budget level is the question. Use S9-05 and S4-22 first; this skill divides a budget, it does not size it.
- The business cannot yet serve more demand, or the product is unavailable in most outlets (S6-03).
- The horizon is under six months and the business will not exist in its present form after that. The long-term job needs time to pay back.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. Last period's spend by activity, with a short description of what each piece of communication asked people to do.
2. The planned budget and period.
3. Business context: category, consumer or B2B, brand size and age, price level, how seasonal sales are.
4. What is measured today: leads, sales, calls, and any brand measures (S1-22).
5. The business objective and time horizon (S7-01, S9-01).

## Procedure
1. Classify every activity by the job it does, not by its channel. Brand building: aimed at all category buyers, usually emotional, no request to act now, meant to be remembered. Activation: aimed at people likely to buy soon, carries an offer, price, information or call to action, judged by immediate response. The same channel can carry either. Working rule: if a piece clearly does both, count it as activation and note it; Binet advises against trying to do both jobs in one piece of advertising because the brand message gets in the way of the sale.
2. Compute the current split: brand spend and activation spend as percentages of the total. Count media and production. Working rule: exclude price discounts given to customers from the total and report them on a separate line, since they are a pricing cost.
3. Set the reference point. Use about 60:40 for consumer brands and about 46:54 for B2B as starting references from the sources, and state that both are averages. Record which way the client's context might move it. The opened sources say the optimum differs by category, price point and brand size but the pages read for this skill do not give the specific figures, so do not quote any.
4. Choose a target range, not a point (Working rule: a band of ten points, for example 50 to 60% brand). Give reasons tied to the context, labelled INTERPRETATION.
5. Plan the move. Working rule: shift no more than about ten points of the total budget per quarter, and where possible run it as a regional test (S7-02), so that short-term sales are not put at risk in one step.
6. Write separate briefs for the two jobs (S6-18). Brand work: broad reach across category buyers (S9-03), emotional, strongly branded with the brand's assets (S6-04), consistent over time. Activation: tight, timed to buying moments, easy to respond to.
7. Set expectations in time. The authors' summary shows activation producing a quick response that decays, and brand effects that are smaller at first but accumulate; the B2B report says brand effects overtake activation effects as the main growth driver after about six months. Tell the client in advance that the short-term numbers may dip while the split moves.
8. Build a two-speed scorecard, as the authors recommend. Short term: responses, leads, sales during activity. Long term: brand measures (S1-22, S4-19), base sales outside promotions, price sensitivity, and business results measured over at least a year. The authors' data show campaigns built on rational messages reporting the most direct effects while emotional and fame campaigns reported more profit, so short-term metrics alone favour the wrong work.
9. Check share of voice. The authors' rule is that brands whose share of category advertising exceeds their market share tend to grow (S9-05). A good split of too small a budget will still do little.
10. Set a review date, record the split actually achieved, and return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. Input: a chain of language-learning centres in Tashkent and Samarkand, a consumer service, with an annual communication budget of 1,200 million soums (m). The owner judges everything by weekly sign-ups.

```
CURRENT SPEND                                                         FACT
Activation: lead ads with a trial-lesson offer 540 m | search ads 180 m |
  flyers with enrolment-week price 240 m                    = 960 m (80%)
Brand: outdoor posters 150 m | online video without offer 90 m = 240 m (20%)
Total 1,200 m. Current split 20:80.
REFERENCE: about 60:40, an average from UK award cases.
TARGET RANGE: 50 to 60% brand within about a year.          INTERPRETATION
  Reason: sign-ups depend on a short enrolment season, so activation stays
  large; brand share is far below any published reference.
PLAN: move 120 m (10 points) per quarter from lead ads to video and outdoor.
  Start of year 240 : 960 (20:80) -> after Q1 360 : 840 (30:70)
  -> after Q2 480 : 720 (40:60) -> after Q3 600 : 600 (50:50)
SCORECARD: weekly sign-ups and cost per sign-up (short); unprompted brand
  recall, sign-ups outside offer weeks, price paid per course (long, 12 months).
EXPECTATION: sign-ups per week may dip in the first two quarters.
```
Arithmetic check: 540 + 180 + 240 = 960; 150 + 90 = 240; 960 + 240 = 1,200; 960 / 1,200 = 80%; 240 / 1,200 = 20%; 120 / 1,200 = 10%; 240 + 120 = 360 and 960 - 120 = 840 (30:70); 480 and 720 (40:60); 600 and 600 (50:50); 240 + 3 x 120 = 600.

## Common mistakes
- Quoting 60:40 as a rule for every brand, category and country.
- Classifying by channel ("TV is brand, online is activation") without looking at what the piece asks people to do.
- Judging brand work by clicks and leads in the same week, then cutting it.
- Cutting activation abruptly, so sales fall before brand effects arrive.
- Putting an emotional story and a hard offer into one piece and getting neither job done.
- Perfecting the split while the total budget is too small to be noticed.

## Quality checklist
- Is every activity classified by job, with the reason noted for doubtful ones?
- Is the current split computed from actual spend, with totals that add up?
- Is the reference figure described as an average from award cases, with its limits?
- Is the target a range, with reasons labelled INTERPRETATION?
- Are there separate briefs and separate measures for the two jobs?
- Has the client been told when each kind of effect should appear?
- Is share of voice checked and a review date set?

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Share-of-voice and category spend data may be hard to obtain. If they are missing, say so and use a rough count of competitors' visible activity as a labelled estimate, not a figure.
- Many small clients will have spent almost entirely on targeted social and messenger advertising with an offer. Classify it by job as in step 1; do not assume all online spend is activation.
- Whether broad-reach media reach buyers outside Tashkent as well as inside is a question to check with current audience data (S8-04), not to assume.
- Brand measures from a Telegram-bot survey miss older and rural people; track the same sample type each time and read changes, not levels.
- The 60:40 and 46:54 references come from mostly UK award cases with large budgets. For a small local brand treat them as hypotheses and prefer a staged move with a test.
