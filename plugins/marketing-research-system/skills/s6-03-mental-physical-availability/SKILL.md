# Growth through penetration: mental and physical availability (S6-03)

This is a growth logic from the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute for Marketing Science, set out by Byron Sharp in How Brands Grow (2010) and extended by Jenni Romaniuk and Sharp in How Brands Grow Part 2 (2016). It says that brands grow mainly by raising penetration, meaning the share of category buyers who buy the brand at least once in a period, and that most of the extra buyers are light buyers who purchase the brand rarely. Two things make that possible. Mental availability is how easily the brand comes to mind in buying situations. Physical availability is how easy the brand is to find and buy. The logic rests on the Double Jeopardy pattern, first described in the 1960s: smaller brands have fewer buyers, and those buyers are also somewhat less loyal.

Evidence grade: A. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. The buying patterns behind the logic have been reported repeatedly across categories and countries, and the Institute cites longitudinal studies as well as one-off snapshots. Use it as the default, with honest limits. Critics, including Mark Ritson and researchers at Kantar, argue that it underplays differentiation (a perceived difference from rivals) and the pricing power that may come with it; Kantar reports from its own brand database that perceived uniqueness goes with willingness to pay more, though the article opened gives no effect sizes. Evidence for luxury and B2B is thinner and comes mostly from the Institute's own surveys; the B2B report opened for this skill does not state sample sizes for its main tables. The Institute itself describes the patterns as regularities that do not hold everywhere, every time.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (penetration, purchase rate, survey counts, shops stocking the brand) versus INTERPRETATION (where the bottleneck is). Do not say that loyalty or differentiation do not matter; the sources do not say that. When the plan is written, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- A client asks how to grow sales and the team must choose between winning more buyers and getting existing buyers to buy more.
- Objectives, target audience breadth and KPIs are being set for a brand in a category with several competitors.
- The client plans to spend mainly on loyalty schemes or on a narrow "ideal customer".

Do not use it when:
- The problem is a short-term sales task with a fixed deadline (a promotion, a launch week). Use S6-05 for the balance.
- The category has only a handful of possible customers who are all known by name, such as a supplier to three state buyers. Use S1-18 and S6-17.
- The product is not yet good enough or is unavailable for supply reasons. Availability in memory cannot fix that.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. A definition of the category and of a category buyer (for example anyone who bought packaged juice in the last three months).
2. Penetration and purchase frequency for the brand and its main rivals, from a survey (S1-08) or sales data (S0-05).
3. Category Entry Points (CEPs): the situations, needs and moments in which people enter the category (S1-21).
4. Distribution facts: which channels and outlets carry the brand, and each channel's share of category sales if known.
5. The brand's distinctive assets, if measured (S6-04), and the current media plan.

## Procedure
1. Diagnose where the brand stands. Put penetration and a loyalty measure (purchase frequency, or retention for subscription-type categories) side by side for all main brands, ordered by size (S4-18). The Double Jeopardy pattern predicts that loyalty rises only slightly with size. The Institute notes that reaching even modest sales targets typically needs a large rise in penetration while purchase rate moves by a fraction of a purchase.
2. Read deviations. A small brand with unusually high loyalty usually has a penetration deficit: according to the Institute's B2B report this often reflects restricted distribution, such as selling in one region only. Check sample sizes before reading small differences.
3. Set the growth objective in penetration terms: how many more category buyers must buy at least once in the period. Record purchase frequency as a secondary measure, expected to rise a little as penetration rises.
4. Define the audience as all category buyers, including light buyers and non-buyers of the brand. The Institute's recommendation is to move some effort from existing customers to buyers who do not yet purchase.
5. Map mental availability. List CEPs using the Institute's prompt words: when, where, while doing what, with what, why, how feeling, with or for whom. Measure which brands buyers link to each CEP with a free-choice survey grid (S1-21, S4-19). Compute three metrics defined by Romaniuk: mental penetration (buyers who link the brand to at least one CEP, divided by the sample), mental market share (the brand's links divided by all brands' links) and network size (the brand's links divided by the buyers who made at least one link).
6. Prioritise CEPs with the Institute's three tests: credibility (can the brand deliver here), competitiveness (how many rivals already hold an advantage) and commonality (how often the situation occurs). Shortlist five to eight. In communication use one CEP per execution and rotate them over time (S9-02).
7. Map physical availability on the three dimensions described by the Institute. Presence: is the brand where buying happens; compare each channel's share of category revenue with its share of the brand's revenue and look for gaps. Prominence: is the brand easy to spot there; paid placement lasts only while it is paid for, and distinctive assets provide prominence the brand owns (S6-04). Portfolio: enough options to cover the main needs without a long tail that spreads resources thin.
8. Find the bottleneck. Working rule: compare the brand's ratio of penetration to mental penetration with the category leader's. A much lower ratio suggests people think of the brand but cannot easily buy it (physical availability first); a similar ratio with low mental penetration suggests memory is the limit (mental availability first). This is a diagnostic heuristic, not a sourced test.
9. Answer the critics inside the plan. Record one relative difference the brand can prove (S6-02) and the price position (S6-13). Working rule: do not cut price or drop product advantages on the grounds that "only availability matters".
10. Set KPIs (S7-01): penetration, the three mental availability metrics, channel coverage against category, and asset strength. Set review points; memory and distribution both build slowly.
11. Write the plan and return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. Input: a juice brand sold in Tashkent and Samarkand. Survey of 400 category buyers (bought packaged juice in the last three months); audit of 500 shops; 6,000 brand-CEP links recorded across all brands.

```
BUYING                                                                FACT
Our brand: 72 of 400 bought -> penetration 18% | 2.1 purchases per buyer
Leader:   180 of 400 bought -> penetration 45% | 2.6 purchases per buyer
MENTAL AVAILABILITY                                                   FACT
Our brand: 160 buyers link it to at least one CEP -> mental penetration 40%
  480 links -> mental market share 480 / 6,000 = 8.0% | network size 3.0
Leader: 280 buyers -> mental penetration 70%
PHYSICAL AVAILABILITY                                                 FACT
Stocked in 140 of 500 audited shops = 28%
Small shops and bazaars: 55% of category sales, 30% of our sales (gap 25 points)
BOTTLENECK                                                  INTERPRETATION
Buyers per person who has the brand in mind: ours 72 / 160 = 45%;
  leader 180 / 280 = 64%. People think of us but often cannot buy us.
Priority 1: presence in small shops and bazaars. Priority 2: widen links
  to two more CEPs. Objective: penetration from 18% to 24% in 12 months
  = 24 more buyers per 400 surveyed.
```
Arithmetic check: 72 / 400 = 0.18; 180 / 400 = 0.45; 160 / 400 = 0.40; 280 / 400 = 0.70; 480 / 6,000 = 0.08; 480 / 160 = 3.0; 140 / 500 = 0.28; 55 - 30 = 25; 72 / 160 = 0.45; 180 / 280 = 0.643; 24% of 400 = 96, and 96 - 72 = 24.

## Common mistakes
- Setting a loyalty target far above what brands of that size normally have.
- Narrowing the audience to heavy buyers or current customers and calling it efficiency.
- Building distribution without memory, or advertising where the product cannot be bought.
- Measuring only awareness of the name and not links to buying situations.
- Reading the logic as "differences and price do not matter" and neglecting the product.
- Applying consumer-goods benchmarks to B2B or luxury without saying that the evidence there is thinner.

## Quality checklist
- Is the category buyer defined, and is the audience all category buyers?
- Are penetration and a loyalty measure shown for the brand and rivals together?
- Is the growth objective stated in penetration terms with a time frame?
- Are mental penetration, mental market share and network size computed from stated counts?
- Are presence, prominence and portfolio each assessed with a fact?
- Is the bottleneck reading labelled INTERPRETATION?
- Does the plan state a provable relative difference and the price position?

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- CEPs must be collected locally, in Uzbek and Russian, in buyers' own words; do not import a list from another country (S1-21).
- A Telegram-bot survey will miss older and rural buyers, so penetration from such a sample is not market penetration. Say so next to every figure, or weight against known statistics (S8-07).
- Physical availability may differ sharply between Tashkent and the regions and between shop types. This is a hypothesis to check with a shop audit, not a known fact.
- Agreeing answer habits can inflate the number of brand-CEP links for every brand; compare brands with each other and do not read absolute levels (S3-07).
- The patterns were established mainly in developed markets. Check Double Jeopardy in the client's own category data before using it to set targets (S8-03).
