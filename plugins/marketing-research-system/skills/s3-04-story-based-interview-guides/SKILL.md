# Story-based interview guides (S3-04)

A story-based interview guide is a discussion guide in which each topic opens with a request for one specific past event ("tell me about the last time you...") and continues with probes into that event, instead of asking for opinions, habits in general or predictions. The approach is common to practitioners of customer and user interviewing. This skill draws on two of them. Steve Portigal, a user researcher, wrote Interviewing Users (2nd ed., Rosenfeld Media, 2023); he treats the guide as a planned list of topics and question types that the interviewer adapts during the conversation. Rob Fitzpatrick, a founder, wrote The Mom Test (2013); his rule is to ask questions about the person's own life and past behaviour that even a kind, biased listener, like one's mother, could not answer with flattery. The purpose is to get accounts of what people did, which are harder to bend toward politeness than answers about what they think or would do.

Evidence grade: C. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. The specific rules here come from practitioners' experience and have not been tested as a package. They are consistent with the research on the limits of self-report that the S1-23 skill summarises (people explain and predict their own behaviour poorly), and they resemble the critical incident technique (S1-16), which has a research record. So use the method as the default way to write a guide, but do not cite it as proof of anything; a recalled story is still a self-report and can be incomplete or tidied.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (what happened in the respondent's story, with quote in the original language) versus INTERPRETATION (what the story suggests about motives). The assistant writes and reviews guides and reviews transcripts; a person conducts the interview. When the guide is delivered, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- Writing the guide for in-depth interviews with customers, non-customers, B2B buyers or the client's staff (S1-04, S1-18, S0-12).
- An existing guide is full of "why", "would you" and "what do you think of" questions.
- Early interviews produced short, agreeable answers.

Do not use it when:
- The respondent has no relevant past event. Use observation (S1-06), or ask about the nearest real situation and what they do now.
- The aim is to measure how many. Use S1-08 with S3-01.
- A specialised format is planned that has its own structure: S1-07 switch interviews, S1-15 laddering, S1-16 critical incidents. Use that skill; the rules here still apply to wording.
- The interview is a concept test. Reactions to a concept are opinions; keep them for the end and read them with S1-23.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. The decision and research questions (S2-01) and the team's written expectations (S2-11).
2. Who will be interviewed and which events they have probably lived through (bought, switched, complained, stopped, recommended).
3. Interview length and setting, and whether a second person will take notes. Portigal recommends two people: one leads, one records and catches missed openings.
4. Words the respondents use for the category, if known.
5. Any concept, product or price the client wants reactions to, so it can be placed last.

## Procedure
1. Separate what you want to know from what you will ask. Portigal distinguishes the questions the team wants answered from the questions put to the participant. Write the research questions on the left of a table; none of them is read out in the interview.
2. For each research question, name the event that would reveal it. Ask the user: "When did this last happen in the person's life or business?" Examples of anchors: the last purchase, the last time it went wrong, the first time, the time they changed supplier, yesterday from start to finish. Fitzpatrick suggests asking for a walkthrough of the last occurrence or of a bad day.
3. Write the opening story question for each topic as an invitation, not a question with a yes or no answer: "Tell me about the last time you ...". Start from the beginning of the event. Portigal's point is that a specific example is easier to answer than a general question and gives material for follow-ups.
4. Add probes under each story question, drawn from Portigal's list of question types. To collect detail: sequence (what happened next), quantity (how many, how often), who else was involved, the complete list ("what else?", asked more than once). To reach what is unsaid: ask what a word or nickname means, ask about a laugh or a hesitation, ask why, ask them to explain it as to an outsider. To show the person's own framework: compare with another occasion, with how others do it, with how it was before.
5. Convert weak questions. Working rule based on Fitzpatrick's categories of bad data (compliments, generalities and hypotheticals, ideas and requests): rewrite every question containing "would you", "do you usually", "do you like", "is it important" into a question about a past instance. In the interview, when the person says "usually", "always" or "I would", the interviewer returns to an instance: "When did that last happen?"
6. Remove presumptions. Portigal warns that asking about "challenges" assumes there are some. Ask what happened and how it went; for sensitive points, mention that people differ ("some do X, others do not") before asking where they stand.
7. Look for evidence of weight. Fitzpatrick's test of whether a problem matters is what the person has already done about it: what they tried, what they paid, whose help they sought. Add the probes "What did you do about it?" and "What did that cost you?".
8. Order and time the guide. Portigal's published sample guide has timed sections: a short introduction (who we are, recording, no wrong answers), an overview of the person, specifics, then future-oriented or concept sections, and a wrap-up asking what was missed. Working rule: give each story topic 10 to 15 minutes and keep at most four story topics per hour.
9. Keep the product out of the first part. Fitzpatrick splits the conversation in two: first the person's life with no mention of the idea, then, with their permission, the idea. Put any concept, brand or price reaction in the last section and label those answers as opinion.
10. Plan the interviewer's conduct: follow the person's order, not the guide's; build each next question on the last answer; say when you change topic; stay silent after an answer, because people often add something deeper to fill the pause; use the person's own words when probing.
11. Plan notes: exact quotes, strong reactions, names and numbers. Portigal's guides are shared with the whole team before fieldwork; do the same with the client. Debrief after each interview (S3-11).
12. Pilot the guide once, then check the transcript: how many topics produced a real story with a time, place and sequence. Rewrite topics that produced only generalities. Return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. Input: a juice brand wants to know why small shops in Tashkent stop reordering. Respondents: shop owners. 60 minutes, Uzbek or Russian.

```
DRAFT QUESTIONS (FACT)                         PROBLEM                 STORY VERSION
"Sharbatimiz sizga yoqadimi?"                  compliment bait         moved to last section
"Odatda qanday buyurtma berasiz?"              generality              T1
"Narx tushsa, ko'proq olarmidingiz?"           hypothetical            T3
"Qanday muammolar bor?"                        presumes problems       T2
GUIDE
Intro 5 min: kim ekanimiz, yozib olish, "to'g'ri yoki noto'g'ri javob yo'q".
T1 15 min: "Oxirgi marta sharbat buyurtma qilganingizni boshidan aytib bering."
   Probes: Keyin nima bo'ldi? Kim qaror qildi? Yana qaysi brendlarni oldingiz? Yana?
T2 15 min: "Расскажите про последний раз, когда вы решили не брать какой-то сок."
   Probes: Что вы сделали с остатком? Во сколько это вам обошлось? (silence)
T3 10 min: "Oxirgi marta yetkazib beruvchi narxni o'zgartirganda nima qildingiz?"
Opinion section 10 min: reactions to the brand and new pack - label as OPINION.
Wrap-up 5 min: "Men nimani so'ramadim?"
PILOT CHECK: T1 story, T2 story, T3 "bunaqa bo'lmagan" -> 2 of 3 topics gave a story;
   T3 anchor changed to the last time the owner changed a shelf price.   INTERPRETATION
```
Arithmetic check: 5 + 15 + 15 + 10 + 10 + 5 = 60 minutes; 4 draft questions = 3 turned into story topics + 1 moved to the opinion section; pilot 2 of 3 story topics produced a story.

## Common mistakes
- Opening with "Tell me about yourself and what you think of our product".
- Accepting "usually we..." as a story and moving on.
- Asking "why?" straight away, before the sequence of events is clear, and getting a tidy reason.
- Asking about the future ("would you buy", "how much would you pay") and reporting it as demand.
- Describing the client's idea early, after which every answer is about the idea.
- Reading the guide in order like a questionnaire and filling every pause.
- Counting compliments and "let us know when it is ready" as findings.
- Treating a vivid story from one person as typical of the segment.

## Quality checklist
- Does every topic open with a request for one specific past event?
- Are research questions kept out of the spoken wording?
- Are hypothetical, generic and opinion questions rewritten or moved to a labelled last section?
- Does each story question have probes for sequence, people, quantity and "what else"?
- Is there a probe for what the person actually did or paid about the problem?
- Is the guide timed, with introduction and wrap-up, and no more than four story topics per hour?
- Is the product or concept absent from the first part?
- After the pilot, did most topics produce a story with time, place and sequence?

## Output template
```
STORY-BASED INTERVIEW GUIDE - [project] - [date] - length [min] - languages: ...
Research question (not asked) | Event that reveals it | Story question (original language) | Probes | Minutes
INTRO: who we are | consent and recording | no right or wrong answers
T1 ... | T2 ... | T3 ... | T4 ...
OPINION SECTION (last, labelled): concept / brand / price reactions
WRAP-UP: "What did I not ask?" | thanks | incentive
INTERVIEWER NOTES: return "usually / would" to "last time" | silence | their words | note quotes, numbers, reactions
PILOT CHECK: topic | story obtained y/n | change made
```

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Write the story questions in both Uzbek and Russian and let the respondent choose; expect switching. Keep quotes in the original language.
- Polite or agreeing answers are likely toward a guest or anyone linked to the client. Story questions reduce this but do not remove it: note praise without an example as "approval, no example", and do not send the client's own staff to interview their customers.
- Asking directly about money, failures or conflicts with suppliers can be uncomfortable. Start with a neutral event, allow time for hospitality before recording, and approach hard points through "the last time".
- Fitzpatrick's advice was written for startup founders and Portigal's for product teams, mostly in Western settings. How readily people here tell a stranger a detailed story is a hypothesis; check in the pilot, in Tashkent and in regions separately.
- Referral recruitment brings similar people with similar stories; record how each respondent was found.
