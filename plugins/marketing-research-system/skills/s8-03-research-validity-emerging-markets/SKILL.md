# Research validity in emerging markets (S8-03)

This method is a checklist for one question: does a theory, a questionnaire or a benchmark that was developed in high-income countries still measure and mean the same thing here? It is based on Steven Burgess and Jan-Benedict Steenkamp's 2006 article in the International Journal of Research in Marketing. They point out that almost all marketing knowledge comes from wealthy industrialised countries, that emerging markets differ in their socioeconomic, cultural and regulative systems (roughly: incomes and demographics and how fast they change; widely shared norms and habits; formal rules and how they are enforced), and that these differences can challenge established theories. They treat emerging markets as natural laboratories for finding where a theory stops working, and describe four research stages: developing theory, acquiring meaningful data, analysing the data, and learning. Their visible text also notes that many consumer concepts do appear to hold widely, so borrowing an existing measure is often reasonable, and that people inside one emerging market vary more in income, education and infrastructure, which can lower the reliability of scales. The vocabulary of equivalence used below comes from the cross-cultural measurement literature (Steenkamp and Baumgartner, 1998; He and van de Vijver, 2012; Meitinger and colleagues, 2020).

Evidence grade: A. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. The grade reflects peer-reviewed sources and a testing tradition (measurement invariance, meaning statistical tests of whether a scale behaves the same in each group) that is well established. Limits, stated honestly: the 2006 article is an academic review and research agenda, not a practitioner manual, and its full text was not opened for this skill, only the publisher's abstract and section excerpts; it does not study Uzbekistan; and the formal tests need large samples and statistical skill that a small agency often lacks. The measurement literature itself warns that exact invariance tests can be too strict, rejecting scales over tiny differences that change no conclusion. So this skill applies the ideas as a practical checklist, as the method database instructs, and treats a passed checklist as reduced risk, not as proof of validity.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (what a pretest or data check showed) versus INTERPRETATION (what a difference between groups means). Never explain a difference between language groups, regions or countries as "cultural" before measurement explanations are ruled out. When the checklist is complete, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- A questionnaire, scale or question wording is taken from a foreign study, a textbook or an international client.
- Results will be compared across groups: Uzbek-language versus Russian-language respondents, Tashkent versus regions, Uzbekistan versus another country, or against a foreign benchmark.
- A Western finding (a typical loyalty level, a price sensitivity rule, an advertising norm) is about to be used as if it applied locally.

Do not use it when:
- The study is small, exploratory and qualitative with no imported measures. Use S2-08.
- The task is general questionnaire wording (S3-01) or sample size (S2-03).
- The team wants a verdict that a theory is true or false here. The checklist only says how far the evidence can be trusted.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. The imported item: theory, scale, questionnaire or benchmark, with its origin (country, year, sample).
2. The planned or collected data: language versions, sampling method, mode (bot, phone, face to face), sample profile.
3. Population figures for comparison from S8-06 and S8-07, with dates.
4. Access to 5 to 10 people from the target groups for a pretest (S3-02).

## Procedure
1. State what is being borrowed and where it was tested. Write the conditions the original assumed: literate respondents used to surveys, private answering, brands present in the category, stable prices, and so on (Working rule: list at least three).
2. Construct equivalence: does the concept exist and mean the same thing here? Ask local informants to describe the concept in their own words before showing any item (S3-05). Check whether the behaviours that signal it locally are the ones the scale asks about. If part of the local meaning is missing from the scale, add local items; if the concept is not recognised, do not measure it with the borrowed scale.
3. Item and translation equivalence: is each question understood as intended in each language? Use team translation with review (S3-03), then cognitive interviews (S3-02) in every language version. Record each misread item and fix or drop it.
4. Sample equivalence: do the groups being compared differ in who was reached? Compare each sample with population figures on age, sex, region, settlement type and language. Different recruitment in each group (a bot in one, street intercepts in another) is a method difference, not a finding about people. Note who is missing and say so in the report (S2-13).
5. Scale and response-style equivalence: do groups use the answer scale in the same way? Compare the share of agreeing, extreme and midpoint answers across groups on items that are unrelated in content (S3-07). Working rules: where agreeing habits are suspected, prefer questions with specific answer options over agree-disagree statements (see S3-01), and treat a gap between groups on a rating item as uninterpreted until response style has been checked.
6. Reliability within heterogeneous samples: compute scale reliability (S2-12) for the whole sample and for main subgroups. Burgess and Steenkamp note that greater variety among respondents may explain lower reliability, so a weak figure in one subgroup is a prompt to look at who that subgroup is and how they read the items, not only a reason to delete items.
7. Decide what comparison the evidence allows. The measurement literature gives three levels: same structure (the concept is measured by the same items in each group), equal loadings (permits comparing relationships such as correlations and regression slopes), and equal intercepts (needed before comparing group averages). If a multi-group factor analysis is feasible (S4-13), run it. If not, apply this Working rule: compare patterns inside each group freely; compare relationships across groups with caution; compare averages across groups only with a warning in the text.
8. Learn in both directions. If the imported theory fails a check, record where and why as a boundary condition, and store it in S8-08. Return the completed checklist to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. A language school wants to use a five-item "brand trust" agreement scale from a foreign study in a Telegram-bot survey in Uzbek and Russian (n = 600), and to compare the two language groups.

```
CHECK                    RESULT (invented)                          DECISION
Construct                informants describe trust mostly as "people
                         I know studied there" - not in the scale   add 1 local item
Item / translation       cognitive interviews, 5 per language:
                         item 4 misread by 3 of 5 Uzbek-version
                         respondents, 0 of 5 Russian-version        reword, retest
Sample                   Tashkent 420 of 600; aged 45+ 48 of 600    report as limit
                         Uzbek version 360, Russian version 240
Scale / response style   average "agree" share on unrelated items:
                         Uzbek version 78%, Russian version 61%     gap of 17 points
Comparison allowed       patterns inside each group: yes
                         group averages: only with a warning; the 17-point
                         gap may be answer style, not trust
```
Arithmetic check: 3 / 5 = 60%; 420 / 600 = 70%; 48 / 600 = 8%; 360 + 240 = 600; 360 / 600 = 60%; 240 / 600 = 40%; 78 - 61 = 17 percentage points.

## Common mistakes
- Translating a foreign scale and assuming the translation makes it valid.
- Reporting a gap between language groups or regions as a real difference without checking sample make-up and answer style.
- Quoting a Western benchmark next to a local number as if both measured the same thing.
- Deleting items to raise reliability without asking why respondents read them differently.
- Rejecting every imported measure on principle; many concepts transfer, and the check is to find out.
- Running formal invariance tests on a small sample and trusting the result.
- Leaving the checks out of the report, so the reader cannot judge the comparison.

## Quality checklist
- Is the origin of every borrowed theory, scale or benchmark stated?
- Did local informants describe the concept in their own words before items were fixed?
- Was every language version pretested with cognitive interviews?
- Is each compared group's sample profile set against population figures?
- Were agreeing, extreme and midpoint answer shares compared across groups?
- Does the report say which comparisons are allowed and which carry a warning?
- Are failed checks recorded as boundary conditions for later projects?

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- No fact about Uzbek respondents is asserted here; the sources did not study the country. Each check is to be run on the project's own data.
- The team works in Uzbek and Russian. Treat the two language versions as two instruments until the pretest shows they are read alike; keep verbatim answers in the original language.
- Polite or agreeing answer habits are a possibility to measure (step 5), not a fact to assume.
- Telegram-bot samples miss older and rural people; say so wherever a result is generalised, and compare Tashkent and the regions separately.
- Most theory in the method index was tested on Western samples; write each transfer as a hypothesis.
