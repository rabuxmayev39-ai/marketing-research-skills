# Distinctive brand assets (S6-04)

Distinctive brand assets are the elements other than the brand name that make people think of the brand: a colour or colour combination, a logo shape, a typeface, a character, a spokesperson, a tagline, a sound or jingle, a pack shape. The method was developed at the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute for Marketing Science and set out by Jenni Romaniuk in Building Distinctive Brand Assets (2018). It measures each asset on two numbers, fame and uniqueness, places it on a grid, and uses the grid to decide which assets to use everywhere, which to invest in, and which to leave alone. The purpose is recognition: when people can tell at a glance whom a message or a pack comes from, each exposure is filed in memory under the right brand.

Evidence grade: A. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. The measurement approach comes from an academic research institute and rests on published research into memory and branding. Honest limits apply. Romaniuk herself says branding is necessary but not sufficient, and that there is no simple formula linking a gain in distinctiveness to a gain in sales. Strong assets take years of consistent use, so the method pays back slowly and punishes frequent redesign. And there is published disagreement: an Oxford researcher, Felipe Thomaz, is reported as saying that in an analysis of thousands of video ads the distinctiveness of assets was not among the top ten drivers of performance. The pages opened for this skill were Institute and trade-press sources, not the book or journal papers, and they give no numeric cut-offs for the grid.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (counts, fame and uniqueness scores, where assets are used) versus INTERPRETATION (which quadrant, what to do). Never recommend changing an asset before it has been measured. When the asset plan is written, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- A client wants to "refresh" a logo, pack, colours or slogan.
- Advertising is noticed but people do not know whose it is.
- A new brand must choose which identity elements to build, or an agency needs fixed branding rules for a creative brief.

Do not use it when:
- The task is to decide what the brand should mean or promise. That is positioning (S6-02); assets signal who is speaking, not what is said.
- The brand has almost no buyers and no communication yet. There is nothing to measure; choose candidate assets by the selection rules in step 8 and measure after a period of use.
- A survey of category buyers is not possible. Without measurement the grid is only opinion; say so.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. An inventory of every identity element in use across packs, signs, vehicles, advertising, social media, documents and staff clothing, with dates of introduction and changes.
2. The same for the main competitors, at least their colours, logos and characters.
3. Clean test material: each asset shown or played with the brand name removed.
4. Access to category buyers for a survey (S1-08, S2-02).
5. Retired assets from the brand's past; the Institute notes these can sometimes be revived.

## Procedure
1. Build the inventory. List every candidate asset by type: colour, shape or logo, word-based (tagline, typeface), face or character, sound, pack form. Record where and how consistently each is used. Romaniuk separates consistency of form (the asset always looks or sounds the same) from consistency of use (where it appears); form matters most while an asset is being built.
2. Prepare the stimuli. Strip the brand name from each asset. Include competitors' assets in the test so that the grid shows relative strength.
3. Survey category buyers, not only the brand's customers. For each asset ask an open question such as "which brand, if any, does this make you think of?". Do not offer a list of brands. Romaniuk warns that prompting can inflate fame scores by up to about 20 percentage points.
4. Calculate fame: the percentage of category buyers who name the brand, unprompted, when shown the asset without the name.
5. Calculate uniqueness: how exclusively the asset is linked to this brand and not to competitors. Working rule for the calculation: responses naming our brand divided by all responses naming any brand for that asset. The opened sources define uniqueness in words only, so this formula is this skill's operational version. The Institute's article ranks uniqueness as the more important of the two.
6. Place each asset on the grid, using the Institute's four quadrants:
   - Use or lose (high fame, high uniqueness): a working asset. Use it everywhere and protect it from change.
   - Invest (high uniqueness, low fame): owned but not yet known. Build it through consistent use with wide reach.
   - Avoid (high fame, low uniqueness): linked to the brand but also to rivals, so it can trigger competitors.
   - Test or ignore (low on both): where all new assets start. If real effort has not moved it, rework it sharply or switch to another element.
   Working rule: draw the lines at 50% fame and 70% uniqueness for a first reading, state that these are this skill's lines and not the Institute's, and show the raw numbers.
7. Compare with what managers expected (S2-11). Romaniuk notes that marketers tend to overrate their own assets because they know their own advertising far better than buyers do.
8. Choose a small set to build. Working rule: two to four assets of different types, for example one colour, one shape and one sound or character, so that the brand can be signalled in visual, audio and small-space settings. For new assets follow Romaniuk's advice to prefer elements with no strong existing meaning, so the brand does not have to fight an old association, and avoid design fashions that rivals will also follow.
9. Write the usage rules: the exact form of each asset, where it must appear, and that it is not open to change in creative work. Tell teams that the brand name still does the main branding job; an asset can stand in for the name only when its fame is close to 100%.
10. Set the review. Working rule: re-measure with the same unprompted method every 12 to 24 months, and before any redesign. Changing the measurement method breaks comparison.
11. Hand the rules to S6-18 and S9-07. Return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. Input: a Tashkent housing developer tests four assets, name removed, in a survey of 300 people planning to buy a flat. Each person gives one answer per asset.

```
RESULTS                                                               FACT
Asset                Our brand  Other brands  No brand   Fame   Uniqueness
A Logo symbol           180         30           90      60%      85.7%
B Green colour           90        135           75      30%      40.0%
C Tagline                45          5          250      15%      90.0%
D Building silhouette   156         96           48      52%      61.9%
GRID (working lines: fame 50%, uniqueness 70%)             INTERPRETATION
A Use or lose: put on every board, banner, document and video end frame.
C Invest: unique but little known; use in every ad for two years, unchanged.
D Avoid: known, but 96 of 252 brand answers named a rival developer.
B Test or ignore: green is shared across the category; do not rely on it.
MANAGERS' EXPECTATION before fieldwork: "green is our strongest asset".
DECISION: no logo redesign. Build A + C + a short sound for video and radio.
```
Arithmetic check: rows sum to 300 (180 + 30 + 90; 90 + 135 + 75; 45 + 5 + 250; 156 + 96 + 48). Fame: 180 / 300 = 60%; 90 / 300 = 30%; 45 / 300 = 15%; 156 / 300 = 52%. Uniqueness: 180 / 210 = 85.7%; 90 / 225 = 40.0%; 45 / 50 = 90.0%; 156 / 252 = 61.9%. D: 156 + 96 = 252.

## Common mistakes
- Showing a list of brands, or leaving the name visible, when measuring assets.
- Surveying only the brand's own customers or its social media followers.
- Treating identity as a design matter and changing it for freshness; the Institute cites the Tropicana pack change, reversed within about a month, as a costly case.
- Altering an asset while it is still being built, which creates several weak assets.
- Investing in an asset that buyers also link to competitors.
- Expecting assets alone to raise sales, or letting assets replace the brand name too early.

## Quality checklist
- Were assets tested with the brand name removed and with an open, unprompted question?
- Was the sample drawn from category buyers, with n stated?
- Are fame and uniqueness shown with the counts behind them?
- Are the grid lines labelled as working rules?
- Were competitors' assets measured too?
- Is each chosen asset fixed in an exact form with usage rules?
- Is a re-measurement date set, using the same method?

## Output template
```
DISTINCTIVE ASSET AUDIT - [brand] - [date]
SAMPLE: category buyers defined as ... | n = ... | method: unprompted, name removed
INVENTORY: asset | type | in use since | where used | consistent form? y/n
RESULTS (FACT): asset | named us | named others | no brand | fame % | uniqueness %
GRID (INTERPRETATION; lines: fame ...%, uniqueness ...%): asset | quadrant | action
COMPETITOR ASSETS TO STAY AWAY FROM: ...
EXPECTED VS FOUND: ...
ASSETS TO BUILD (2-4): asset | exact form | where it must appear
RULES: no change without re-measurement | brand name always present until fame near 100%
```

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Word-based assets (taglines, names of characters) need a decision on language and script: Uzbek in Latin or Cyrillic, or Russian. Test each version as a separate asset; do not assume they share fame.
- Record answers exactly as given, in the original language, including local nicknames for brands, and code them afterwards.
- A Telegram-bot sample will miss older and rural buyers, and sound assets cannot be tested fairly if respondents answer with the sound off. Check for this in the questionnaire.
- Polite respondents may guess a brand to be helpful. Offer an explicit "no brand comes to mind" option and treat guesses spread across many brands as low uniqueness.
- Which colours or symbols are common in a local category is an empirical question. Audit competitors' signs and packs before choosing; do not rely on general beliefs about colour meanings.
