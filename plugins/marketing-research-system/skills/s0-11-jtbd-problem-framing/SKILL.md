# Jobs-to-be-Done problem framing (S0-11)

Jobs-to-be-Done (JTBD) is a way of looking at demand: people do not buy a product for its own sake, they "hire" it to make progress in a particular situation, and they "fire" it when it does the job badly. The idea was developed over many years by Clayton Christensen of Harvard Business School with colleagues, and set out for managers in a 2016 Harvard Business Review article by Christensen, Hall, Dillon and Duncan. A job has functional, social and emotional sides, and the situation matters more than who the buyer is. This skill uses only one part of JTBD: at the start of a project it rewrites the client's problem ("our product is not selling") as a question about the customer's job ("what are people in this situation trying to get done, and what do they hire for it?"). The interview method is covered in S1-07 and the analysis of interview data in S4-09.

Evidence grade: B. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. JTBD is widely taught and used, but it is a lens, not a measured theory: its support is mostly cases and stories (the milkshake, the condominium developer), told by its own authors. No controlled test of whether job-based framing leads to better decisions was found among the sources opened for this skill. Published criticism, from a Nielsen Norman Group article whose author openly favours personas: a job statement averages the emotional and social context across all users, does not separate user groups with different needs, and can push teams towards costly reinvention. Treat every job written at intake as a hypothesis to be checked with real buyers, never as a finding.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (records, counts, things customers did) versus INTERPRETATION (every job statement written before fieldwork). Do not invent customer motives to fill a gap; write "unknown" and send it to S0-13. When the reframed problem is written, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- The brief describes the problem through the product: features, quality, "people do not understand our advantages".
- The client names only same-category brands as competitors.
- Sales data show customers leaving, but nobody knows what they chose instead.

Do not use it when:
- The job has already been researched; go to S1-07 for interviews or S4-09 for analysis.
- The question is how many people do something or how big the market is (S1-08, S4-23), or the problem is operational and already located, such as stock-outs (S0-04).

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. The client's own statement of the problem, in their words.
2. What the client sells, to whom, at what price, through which channels.
3. Any behaviour records: enquiries, sales, reasons for loss noted by sales staff, repeat purchases, reviews.
4. One or two people who talk to customers every day (S0-12 gives the interview rules).
5. Thirty to sixty minutes with the client to react to the reframed problem.

## Procedure
1. Write down the client's problem statement unchanged. Underline every phrase that is about the product or the company. Working rule: if no customer and no situation appears in the statement, reframing is needed.
2. Find the situations. The authors treat the circumstance as the unit of analysis, more important than age, income or other buyer traits. Ask the client and front-line staff: when exactly do people come to you? What happened in their life or business just before? Ask for the last three real cases, not for a typical customer (S0-12).
3. Draft one to three candidate jobs. Working rule (a common practitioner format, not taken from the 2016 article): "When [situation], I want to [progress], so that [result]." Keep the product out of the sentence. A good job statement would still make sense if the client's product did not exist.
4. Add the three sides the authors say every job has. Functional: the practical task. Social: how the person wants to be seen by others. Emotional: how they want to feel, and what they fear. Mark each as an assumption unless a record supports it.
5. List the job candidates: everything a person in that situation could hire. Christensen and colleagues call competitors job candidates and show that they often come from outside the category (in the milkshake story: a bagel, a banana, boredom). Include doing nothing and home-made solutions. Use records to see where lost customers went; if there are none, mark the list as assumed.
6. Look for where the job is done badly. The sources point to workarounds, to customers using a product for something it was not designed for, and to people who use nothing because existing options cost too much or are too complicated. Ask staff for examples; do not guess.
7. Rewrite the problem. Put the client's version and the job version side by side: "Product view: ... Job view: people in [situation] trying to [progress] currently hire [candidates]; we lose them because [hypothesis]." State what the client would do differently if the job view were true. If nothing would change, the reframing adds nothing; say so.
8. Check the frame against its known weaknesses. Working rule: if two customer groups in the same situation want clearly different results, write two jobs and do not average them; decide later which to serve (S5-09).
9. Turn the frame into research needs. Each job, each candidate and each "because" is a hypothesis. Enter them in S0-13 and S2-11. Propose S1-07 switch interviews with recent buyers and recent non-buyers as the main test; the Sloan article describes building about 25 situation cases and grouping them to find the dominant jobs.
10. Show the reframed problem to the client, agree which version drives the research brief (S0-02, S2-01), and return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. A language school in Tashkent. Client's statement: "Sales of our general English course are falling. We need advertising that explains our 120-hour programme and experienced teachers."

```
RECORDS (FACT in this invented case)
  Enquiries last quarter: 400 | Enrolled: 80 | Not enrolled: 320
  Enrolment rate: 80 / 400 = 20%
  Manager's notes exist for 100 of the 320 who did not enrol:
    chose a private IELTS tutor            45
    postponed, "no time now"               25
    chose a phone app or free videos       20
    chose another language school          10   (total 100)
CANDIDATE JOBS (INTERPRETATION, to test)
  J1 When I have a date for a university or visa application, I want a
     required test score by that date, so that the application is not lost.
     Functional: score by a deadline | Social: family expects the result
     Emotional: fear of wasting a year
  J2 When my employer starts work with foreign partners, I want to speak
     at meetings without embarrassment (not in the records; unknown).
JOB CANDIDATES for J1: private tutor, exam-preparation centre, self-study
  with an app, postponing the application, the school's general course
REFRAMED PROBLEM
  Product view: people do not know how good our course is.
  Job view: only 10 of 100 recorded losses went to another school; 45
  went to tutors who promise a score by a date. Hypothesis: for J1 a
  general course is a weak candidate, whatever its quality.
  If true: build a deadline-based exam product; advertising the 120
  hours would not help.
TO RESEARCH: S1-07 with 10-12 recent enrollers and non-enrollers.
```
Arithmetic check: 400 - 80 = 320; 80 / 400 = 0.20; 45 + 25 + 20 + 10 = 100; 10 / 100 = 10%; 45 / 100 = 45%. The 100 noted cases are not a random sample of the 320, so the shares are indications only.

## Common mistakes
- Writing the job with the product inside it ("wants a 120-hour course").
- Presenting an intake job statement as an insight before any customer has been interviewed.
- Describing a buyer type (age, income) and calling it a job.
- Keeping only the functional side and dropping the social and emotional ones.
- Listing only same-category brands as competitors and leaving out "do nothing".
- Merging groups with different desired results into one job, or writing a job so broad ("be successful") that it points to no decision.

## Quality checklist
- Is the client's original statement recorded word for word?
- Does each job name a situation and a desired progress, with no product in it?
- Are functional, social and emotional sides written and marked as fact or assumption?
- Are job candidates from outside the category and "do nothing" on the list?
- Is it stated what the client would do differently under the job view?
- Is every job marked as a hypothesis with a planned test?
- Were different groups kept as separate jobs where their desired results differ?

## Output template
```
JTBD PROBLEM FRAMING - [client] - [date]
Client's statement (verbatim, original language): ...
Records available (FACT): ...
Situations reported (last real cases): 1 ... 2 ... 3 ...
Job [n] (INTERPRETATION): When ..., I want to ..., so that ...
  Functional: ... | Social: ... | Emotional: ... | evidence: fact / assumption
Job candidates (incl. do nothing, workarounds): ... | source: record / assumed | done badly where: ...

Job view of the problem: ...
What changes for the client if the job view is true: ...
Hypotheses to test -> S0-13, S2-11: ... | Method: ...
```

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Write the job in the customer's own language and words, Uzbek or Russian; translate only for the report (S3-03).
- The social side may involve family or community expectations. This is a hypothesis to ask about, not a fact about Uzbek consumers.
- Polite or agreeing answers are likely if staff or customers are asked "do people want X?". Ask what the last customer did.
- Client records (CRM notes, Telegram chats) may be thin, especially outside Tashkent; say what they cover. The published JTBD cases opened here are largely American, so treat any transferred job as a hypothesis.
