# Trustworthiness criteria (S2-08)

Trustworthiness is the set of four tests that Yvonna Lincoln and Egon Guba proposed in Naturalistic Inquiry (1985) for judging qualitative research, as counterparts to the validity and reliability tests used for numbers. Credibility asks whether the findings are a correct reading of what participants said and did. Transferability asks whether a reader can judge if the findings apply to another setting. Dependability asks whether the research process was consistent and can be followed. Confirmability asks whether the findings come from the data and not from the researcher's preferences. Each test has techniques: prolonged engagement, persistent observation, triangulation, peer debriefing, negative case analysis and member checks for credibility; thick description for transferability; an audit trail and audit for dependability and confirmability; and a reflexive journal across all four. This skill uses them as the agency's quality checklist for interviews, groups and observation.

Evidence grade: B. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. The criteria are the common standard in qualitative research and are still widely used. They are also debated: Morse (2015) argues the strategies have not been tested for their effect on finished research, and Morse and colleagues (2002) argue that checks applied only at the end cannot correct a study. So treat the criteria as a disciplined checklist, not as proof; a study can tick every technique and still be wrong.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (what was done, with the document that shows it) versus INTERPRETATION (your judgement of whether a criterion is met). When the audit is delivered, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- Designing a qualitative study: decide which techniques will be built in, while they are still cheap.
- Fieldwork is running: check at a midpoint so problems can still be fixed.
- A qualitative report is about to go to a client, or the team is judging someone else's qualitative study.

Do not use it when:
- The study is a survey or experiment. Use S2-13 total survey error, S3-09 and S4-10.
- The team wants a stamp to add after the work is finished. Techniques that were not done cannot be claimed afterwards.
- The need is to judge the quality of the interviews themselves from transcripts. Use S2-09 alongside this skill.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. The research brief, questions and written expectations (S2-01, S2-11).
2. The sampling plan and achieved sample with recruitment routes (S2-06).
3. Guides, recordings or transcripts in the original language, field notes and interview snapshots (S3-11).
4. Analysis files: codes, matrices, memos, and the record of changes (S4-01, S4-05).
5. The draft findings, and a colleague outside the project who can act as debriefer or auditor.

## Procedure
1. Set the stage. Ask the user: "Are we planning, mid-fieldwork, or reviewing a finished report?" Planning: choose techniques and assign owners. Mid-fieldwork or finished: audit what exists. Following Morse and colleagues (2002), prefer checks during the study over checks at the end.
2. Credibility. Go through the techniques; for each, record done, partly done or not done, with the evidence.
   - Prolonged engagement: enough time with participants and setting to build trust, see the range of variation and test for misinformation. Working rule for an agency: record total contact hours and whether interviews ran long enough for follow-up questions.
   - Persistent observation: focus in depth on the elements most relevant to the question, instead of staying broad throughout.
   - Triangulation: more than one data source, method or analyst (S2-07).
   - Peer debriefing: sessions with a colleague who has no stake in the project and who questions methods, conclusions and biases. An opened textbook notes credibility is stronger when the report shows what changed as a result.
   - Negative case analysis: search for cases that contradict the explanation and revise it until they are accounted for (S4-07).
   - Member checks: feed data, interpretations and conclusions back to the people they came from (S5-14). Morse and colleagues caution that participants often cannot recognise themselves in findings abstracted across many people, so treat disagreement as data, not as a veto.
3. Transferability. Check for thick description: behaviour and experience reported together with context, so an outsider can make sense of them. The report should state setting, sample strategy and characteristics, inclusion criteria, interview procedure and topics, and changes made along the way. The reader, not the researcher, makes the transfer judgement; the researcher's duty is to supply enough description for it. Decision rule (working): any sentence that applies a finding to a group not interviewed must be labelled a hypothesis.
4. Dependability. Check the audit trail: a record kept from the start of the project of each research step and decision, including who was selected and why, guide versions, coding changes and meeting notes. An opened textbook states that without an audit trail dependability cannot be assessed.
5. Confirmability. Pick the findings the recommendation depends on and trace each back: finding, theme, coded extracts, transcript and respondent ID. Working rule: trace at least five findings or all strategy-driving ones, whichever is more; any finding that cannot be traced is removed or relabelled as the analyst's hypothesis. Where possible a person outside the team does this audit, as in the published example where an external auditor checked that codes followed from the data.
6. Reflexivity. Check that the researchers kept a reflexive journal (Korstjens and Moser call it a diary): notes on their own assumptions, their relationship with participants and the client, and how these may have shaped questions and readings. Ask: "What did you expect to find, and what in the data went against it?" If nothing went against expectations, flag it.
7. Summarise. For each criterion give a judgement (met, partly met, not met) as INTERPRETATION, with the facts behind it. List fixes that are still possible (more interviews, a second coder, a debrief session, added context in the report) and limits that must be stated in the report because they cannot be fixed.
8. Decide. Working rule: a report goes to the client only if confirmability tracing passed for all strategy-driving findings and every unmet criterion is written into the limitations section. Return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. Input: a Tashkent housing developer commissioned 14 interviews with recent apartment buyers. The draft report has 6 key findings. Audit before delivery.

```
TECHNIQUE               STATUS    EVIDENCE [FACT]
Prolonged engagement    partly    14 interviews, average 40 min = 560 min = 9 h 20 min;
                                  4 interviews under 25 min
Persistent observation  done      guide narrowed after interview 5 to the choice stage
Triangulation           partly    second coder on 4 of 14 transcripts; no second method
Peer debriefing         not done  no session held
Negative case analysis  done      2 buyers who chose on price alone discussed in report
Member checks           not done  -
Thick description       partly    sample table present; recruitment route missing
Audit trail             done      guide v1-v3, coding log, decisions file
Reflexive journal       not done  -
COUNT: done 3, partly 3, not done 3 = 9 techniques
TRACE TEST: 6 key findings traced to transcripts: 5 traced, 1 not traced
  ("buyers distrust unfinished blocks" rests on the analyst's summary only)
JUDGEMENT [INTERPRETATION]: credibility partly met; transferability partly met;
  dependability met; confirmability partly met (5 of 6).
ACTIONS: hold one debrief session; add recruitment route (all via the developer's
  sales office - state as a limit); remove or relabel finding 6; send summary to
  3 participants for comment.
```
Arithmetic check: 14 x 40 = 560 minutes; 560 / 60 = 9 hours with 20 minutes left. Status count 3 + 3 + 3 = 9 rows. Trace test 5 + 1 = 6.

## Common mistakes
- Writing "trustworthiness was ensured" with no record of what was done. Treating the techniques as boxes to tick at the end; several only work during fieldwork and analysis.
- Counting a chat with a teammate who shares the same assumptions as peer debriefing.
- Using member checks as a vote, or dropping a finding because one participant disliked it.
- Claiming transferability ("this applies to all buyers") instead of giving the reader the description to judge it.
- An audit trail rebuilt from memory after the project. A reflexive note that only lists the researcher's job title, with nothing on expectations or the client relationship.

## Quality checklist
- Is each of the four criteria judged separately, with evidence?
- Were at least two credibility techniques used during the study, not added afterwards?
- Does the report describe setting, sample, recruitment and procedure in enough detail for a reader to judge transfer?
- Does an audit trail exist that was kept as the project ran?
- Can every strategy-driving finding be traced to transcripts and respondent IDs?
- Did the researchers record their expectations and what contradicted them?
- Are unmet criteria stated as limitations in the report?

## Works with
- S2-07 triangulation, S4-07 negative case analysis, S5-14 member checking: three of the credibility techniques have their own skills. S2-09 qualitative literacy indicators: reviews the quality of the fieldwork itself; use both for a full review.
- S2-04, S2-05, S2-06: sample size and selection logic, part of dependability and thick description.
- S2-11 write expectations down before fieldwork and S3-11 interview snapshot: raw material for the reflexive journal and audit trail. S4-01 thematic analysis, S4-04 coding cycles, S4-05 matrix displays: where the audit trail of analysis is produced.
- S2-10 ethics and consent: member checks and audits must respect confidentiality. S7-04 project retrospective: carry lessons forward.
- S2-13 total survey error: the parallel quality framework for surveys.

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Keep transcripts, coded extracts and quotes in Uzbek or Russian through analysis and audit; a trace test on translated text can hide what was said.
- Member checks may draw polite agreement instead of correction. Ask participants what is missing or wrong, and send a short summary in their own language; a Telegram message can work, but note who did not reply.
- Peer debriefers and auditors should be outside the project and not dependent on its lead; agreeing with a senior colleague is easy. Thick description should state Tashkent or region, language of interview, who else was present, and how the person was recruited, so a reader can judge transfer between city and regions.
- Short visits rarely amount to prolonged engagement in settings where hospitality comes before frank talk; record time spent before and after the recorded part.
- The criteria were developed in Western academic research. They are used here as a working checklist; no claim is made that they were validated locally.
