# Negative / deviant case analysis (S4-07)

Negative case analysis is the deliberate search for respondents, episodes or observations that do not fit the pattern the team believes it has found, followed by a decision on what each exception means for the finding. It has roots in analytic induction, a strategy introduced by Znaniecki in 1934 that takes the case that does not fit as the starting point for testing a hypothesis. Lincoln and Guba (Naturalistic Inquiry, 1985) are the usual reference for it as a technique supporting the credibility of qualitative findings, and Seale (The Quality of Qualitative Research, 1999) is a common reference for deviant cases as a quality check. Morse and colleagues (2002) call seeking negative cases essential and place it during the study, not after it.

Evidence grade: B. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. This is a standard recommended practice in the methods literature, but, as a 2015 review in Qualitative Health Research notes about rigour strategies in general, there is little evidence on how well such strategies work. Treat it as a discipline that reduces confirmation bias and sharpens findings, not as proof that a finding is correct.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (what the deviant case said or did) versus INTERPRETATION (what the team concludes about the pattern). When the negative case log is complete, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- Draft themes, segments or insights exist and the team is starting to agree with itself.
- A finding is stated as "all", "most" or "always".
- A decision with real cost will rest on the finding.
- The team says "we are not hearing anything new" and wants to stop fieldwork.

Do not use it, or limit it, when:
- No pattern has been stated yet; there is nothing for a case to contradict.
- The data are a single interview or a few short answers.
- The team would use one exception to throw out a well-supported finding, or would use the exercise to list exceptions without deciding anything.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. The findings to test, each written as one falsifiable sentence.
2. The full dataset with respondent IDs and profile data, not only the coded extracts.
3. The list of respondents whose quotes support each finding, and therefore also the list of those who were not quoted.
4. The sampling record: who was recruited, how, and who is missing.
5. Whether more fieldwork is still possible.

## Procedure
1. Rewrite each finding so that it can fail. Ask the user: "What would a respondent have to say or do for this to be wrong?" Record the answer as the disconfirming profile. If nobody can describe one, the finding is too vague; sharpen it first.
2. Search the existing data for exceptions. Go through every case, not a sample. Look in four places: respondents who say the opposite; respondents who say the same but describe doing something else (pass these to S4-08); respondents who were never quoted for this finding; and passages earlier coded as "other" or left uncoded. McPherson and Thorne (2006) warn that in qualitative work the analyst notices outliers early and can write them out of the account through interpretive choices, so check the uncoded material deliberately.
3. Verify each exception. First check whether it comes from a transcription, translation or coding error, or from a misunderstood question. Keep it as a negative case only after that check. Record it with ID, quote in the original language and context.
4. Classify the exception. Following McPherson and Thorne, ask what it represents: (a) a real contradiction of the finding; (b) variation within the same phenomenon (a different kind of customer or situation); (c) a different phenomenon that was wrongly grouped with this one; (d) an error (already removed in step 3).
5. Look for what explains it. Compare the negative case with the fitting cases on profile and circumstance: segment, city or region, purchase channel, experience, who pays, who decides. State the candidate explanation as INTERPRETATION.
6. Imagine the cases you do not have. McPherson and Thorne suggest thinking through plausible variations that the sample did not capture, by age, gender, culture or experience. List the groups absent from the sample for whom the finding might fail. If fieldwork is open, recruit two or three people who match the disconfirming profile from step 1. Morse et al. treat this as part of sampling adequacy.
7. Decide. For each finding choose one outcome and write the reason: (a) Revise: the finding is reworded so that it also covers the exception; (b) Bound: the finding holds under stated conditions; the exception marks the boundary; (c) Split: there are two patterns for two groups or situations; (d) Keep and report: the exception is unexplained; the finding stays and the dissenting case is reported next to it; (e) Drop: exceptions are frequent enough or strong enough that the pattern does not stand. Working rules of this skill, not sourced thresholds: one unexplained exception does not overturn a finding supported across segments; a finding whose exceptions all come from one identifiable group must be bounded or split; a finding with no exception found after a real search is reported as "no negative case found in this sample", not as universal.
8. Check the saturation claim. If the team says the data are saturated, ask whether disconfirming cases were actively sought. McPherson and Thorne point out that a saturation claim can simply mean the search stopped, and that small homogeneous samples make this easy.
9. Report. For every key finding show the wording before and after, the negative cases, the decision and what remains unexplained. Do not turn the counts into percentages of a market. Return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. A Tashkent housing developer interviewed 16 recent apartment buyers.
Input finding: "Buyers choose the developer mainly on trust built through relatives' recommendations."
Disconfirming profile: a buyer who had no recommendation, or who bought against one.
Output:
```
Cases supporting: 11 of 16                                              FACT
Negative cases after error check: 3 (B4, B9, B13); never quoted: 2      FACT
B4: "Hech kim maslahat bermagan, o'zim qurilishga uch marta bordim"     FACT
B9: "Родственники отговаривали, но рассрочка была только здесь"         FACT
B13: bought from abroad, relied on Telegram channel videos              FACT
Classification: B4, B13 = variation (no local family network);
  B9 = real contradiction (terms outweighed advice)                     INTERP
Explanation: all three bought without family present in the city        INTERP
Decision: BOUND + SPLIT
  Before: buyers choose on relatives' recommendations
  After: buyers with family in the city rely on relatives; buyers
  without one build trust through site visits or video evidence;
  instalment terms can override advice (1 case, reported)               INTERP
Absent from sample: buyers who considered and rejected the developer
Next step: recruit 3 rejecters before the insight is fixed
```

## Common mistakes
- Looking for exceptions only among the quotes already selected. Explaining every exception away as "an unusual person".
- Treating one exception as a refutation of a broad, well-supported pattern. Listing exceptions without deciding revise, bound, split, keep or drop.
- Skipping the error check, so a translation mistake becomes a "negative case". Claiming saturation without having sought disconfirming cases.
- Reporting "no exceptions" when the sample was homogeneous. Doing the search only after the report is written, when nothing can change.

## Quality checklist
- Was each finding stated so that it could fail, with a disconfirming profile?
- Was the whole dataset searched, including unquoted respondents and uncoded passages?
- Was each exception checked for recording, translation and coding error?
- Was each exception classified and compared with fitting cases?
- Does every finding have a recorded decision and reason?
- Are unexplained exceptions reported and not hidden?
- Are groups absent from the sample named as limits?
- Is the before and after wording of revised findings shown?

## Works with
- S4-01 thematic analysis, S4-04 coding cycles, S4-06 Gioia method: supply the themes to test.
- S4-03 abductive analysis: an unexplained exception is a surprise that calls for a new explanation.
- S4-05 matrix displays: a case-by-theme matrix makes empty and contradicting cells visible.
- S4-08 say-do analysis: finds cases that agree in words and deviate in behaviour.
- S2-07 triangulation and S2-08 trustworthiness criteria: negative case analysis is one credibility technique among several.
- S5-03 insight tests and S5-05 what would have to be true: run after findings have survived this step.
- S5-14 member checking: themes disputed by respondents come here as negative cases.

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Polite or agreeing answer habits can hide disagreement, so spoken exceptions may be rare. Look for exceptions in described behaviour, hesitations and short answers, not only in open disagreement.
- Search in the original Uzbek or Russian text. A translated summary can smooth an exception away. Keep negative-case quotes in the original language.
- Check whether the exceptions cluster by Tashkent versus regions before calling them individual oddities, and name the regions the sample does not cover.
- If recruitment ran through a Telegram bot, older and rural people are probably absent. List them under absent cases; do not read their absence as agreement.
- Explanations borrowed from Western studies for why a case deviates are hypotheses to check locally.
