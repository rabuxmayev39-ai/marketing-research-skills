# Affinity diagram / KJ method (S5-01)

An affinity diagram sorts many separate notes (observations, quotes, facts) into clusters by likeness, names each cluster, and stacks clusters into a hierarchy. The Japanese anthropologist Jiro Kawakita devised it in the 1960s (hence "KJ method") to make sense of raw fieldwork notes and reach new hypotheses. Hugh Beyer and Karen Holtzblatt later adapted it for interview and observation data inside their Contextual Design process, and it is now a standard synthesis step in UX and market research. Evidence grade: B, an established professional standard. It is widely taught and used, and there are published accounts of practice, but it is a way of organising data, not a validated measurement. Treat the clusters as a well-ordered description of what was heard, not as proof of how common or how important something is.

Always answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). In every output, mark what is a fact from the data (a note, a quote, a count) and what is an interpretation (a cluster name, a theme, a ranking).

## When to use it / When not to use it

Use it when:
- Interviews, observations, open survey answers or shop visits are finished and the team has roughly 40 or more separate notes.
- The team needs a shared picture of the data before writing findings.
- Several people collected data and each holds a different part of the story.

Do not use it when:
- There are fewer than about 20 notes; just read and discuss them.
- The team wants to test a fixed list of hypotheses. Sorting into pre-set boxes is coding, not affinity work.
- The question is "how many" or "what share". Counts of notes are not statistics about the market.
- The notes are summaries of summaries. Go back to transcripts first.

## What you need before starting (inputs)

- One focus question for the session (for example: "What gets in the way of a baker switching flour supplier?").
- Raw material: transcripts, field notes, open answers, photos with captions.
- A participant list with codes (P01, P02 ...) and basic descriptors (role, city or region, segment).
- A place to work: a wall with sticky notes, an online board, or a spreadsheet with one row per note.
- Two or more people who know the data, if possible. The method can be done alone, but team discussion is a large part of its value.

## Procedure

1. Agree the focus question. Ask the user: "What single question should this sorting answer?" If they give several, pick the one tied to the project decision and park the rest. One question per round.

2. Create notes. Break the raw material into notes with these rules:
   - One point per note. Split any note that contains "and" joining two ideas.
   - Each note is a fact: what a person said or did, close to their own words. No interpretation yet.
   - Keep verbatim quotes in the original language. Add a short translation in brackets only if the team asks.
   - Give each note an ID: participant code plus sequence number (P07-12). This lets anyone trace a note back to its context.
   - Tag the note type: quote, observed behaviour, or researcher remark.
   Produce a numbered note list and tell the user how many notes came from each participant. If one participant supplies more than about a quarter of all notes, say so.

3. Reduce if needed. If there are several hundred notes, ask the user whether to prioritise notes relevant to the focus question or to split the work into rounds. Do not silently drop notes.

4. Cluster bottom-up. Read notes one at a time and place each next to notes that say something similar.
   - Do not start from category names, interview guide questions, or the brief's sections. Let groups form from content.
   - Do not group by keyword. "Price" notes may belong to three different ideas (cannot afford, does not trust cheap, uses price to judge quality).
   - Do not force a note into a cluster. A single note that fits nowhere stays as its own group.
   - If a note truly belongs in two clusters, duplicate it and mark the copy.
   - Aim for small clusters first, roughly 3 to 8 notes each. A cluster of 20 usually hides two or three ideas; split it.
   If working with a human team, suggest they do this step quietly, each person moving notes freely, and talk only after the first pass. Silent sorting reduces the pull of the loudest voice.

5. Name each cluster. Write the label after the cluster exists, never before.
   - The label must cover every note in the cluster. If one note does not fit the label, move the note or rewrite the label.
   - Working rule for this skill: write the label as a short statement of what the notes say ("Bakers judge flour by how the dough behaves on day one"), not a topic word ("Quality"). Topic words hide the content.
   - Mark each label as an interpretation.

6. Build the hierarchy. Group cluster labels into higher-level themes, and name those too. Two levels above the notes are usually enough (cluster, then theme). Stop when further grouping only produces vague headings.

7. Walk the wall. Review the whole diagram with the team:
   - Read every label and its notes aloud or in sequence.
   - Merge clusters that say the same thing; split clusters that say two things.
   - Check that leftover notes have a place or are kept visibly as outliers.
   - Ask: "What did we expect to see that is missing?" Record absences as a separate list.

8. Count, carefully. For each cluster record the number of notes and the number of different participants behind it. Check whether one or two people produced most of a cluster. Report counts as a description of this sample only.

9. Prioritise (optional). If the team must choose where to focus, use a simple silent vote: each person picks their top three clusters for the focus question and gives 3, 2 and 1 points. Rank by total. Present the ranking as the team's judgement, not as a research result.

10. Document. Save the full hierarchy, note IDs per cluster, one or two representative quotes per cluster in the original language, the outlier list, the absence list and open questions. Keep a photo or export of the board.

11. Hand over. Tell the user which clusters look ready to be written up as findings and which need more data. Then return to the research-orchestrator skill.

Decision rules:
- If the first-pass clusters match the interview guide sections one to one, the sort was top-down. Redo step 4.
- If more than about a third of notes sit in "other", the notes are too vague or the focus question is wrong. Fix that before continuing.
- If the user asks the AI to do the clustering alone, do it, but label the result "first-pass proposal" and ask at least one person who did the fieldwork to review it.

## Worked example

INVENTED example for illustration only. A juice brand's agency interviewed 12 small-shop owners in Tashkent and two regional towns. Focus question: "What decides which juice brands a small shop keeps on the shelf?"

Input (5 of 96 notes):
- P03-07 (quote): "Беру то, что спрашивают" [I stock what people ask for]
- P05-02 (behaviour): keeps one brand in the fridge supplied by its distributor, others on a warm shelf
- P08-11 (quote): "Agent har hafta keladi, qarzga ham beradi" [The agent comes weekly and gives credit]
- P11-04 (quote): says he dropped a brand after two late deliveries
- P02-09 (behaviour): cannot name the price difference between two brands he sells

Output (extract):

| Theme (interpretation) | Cluster label (interpretation) | Notes | Participants |
|---|---|---|---|
| The supplier relationship decides the shelf | A reliable weekly visit matters more than the brand | 11 | 7 of 12 |
| The supplier relationship decides the shelf | Credit terms keep a brand in the shop | 8 | 5 of 12 |
| The shop owner follows demand, not preference | Owners stock what customers name at the counter | 9 | 8 of 12 |
| Fridge space is controlled by others | Whoever supplied the fridge owns the cold shelf | 6 | 4 of 12 |

Outliers: P09-03, an owner who chooses by his own children's taste. Absence: nobody mentioned advertising unprompted.

## Common mistakes

- Sorting into categories decided in advance, or into the interview guide's questions.
- Labels that are topics ("Price", "Delivery") instead of statements.
- Mixing interpretation into the notes, so later readers cannot tell what was actually said.
- Removing outliers to make the diagram tidy.
- Reading note counts as market size or importance.
- Treating the diagram as the result. The diagram is an input to findings and insights.
- Letting one person, or the AI, cluster alone with no review.

## Quality checklist

- Is there one written focus question?
- Does each note hold one point and carry a participant ID?
- Were clusters formed before labels were written?
- Does every label cover all notes under it and read as a statement?
- Are outliers and absences listed, not deleted?
- Are counts shown as notes and as distinct participants, with a warning about sample limits?
- Did at least one person who did the fieldwork review the result?
- Are facts and interpretations visibly separated?

## Output template

```
AFFINITY DIAGRAM
Focus question:
Data: [n] interviews / [n] notes / dates / places
Prepared by: [people]; AI first pass: yes/no; reviewed by:

THEME 1 (interpretation): [statement]
  Cluster 1.1 (interpretation): [statement]
    Notes: [IDs]   Count: [n] notes, [n] of [N] participants
    Quotes (fact, original language): "..." (P__)
  Cluster 1.2 ...

THEME 2 ...

OUTLIERS (fact): [note IDs and text]
ABSENCES (what we expected but did not hear):
TEAM PRIORITY VOTE (judgement, optional): 1) ... 2) ... 3) ...
OPEN QUESTIONS / DATA GAPS:
NEXT STEP: [method ID]
```

## Works with

- S5-02 insight ladder: clusters become findings, then insights and implications.
- S4-03 abductive analysis and S4-07 negative case analysis: use the outlier and absence lists as starting points for surprises and counter-examples.
- S5-06 means-end value map and S5-07 COM-B: re-read clusters through these lenses after the bottom-up sort, not before.
- S5-09 needs-based segments: check whether clusters split by participant type.
- S5-14 member checking: take cluster labels back to a few participants.

## Notes for Uzbekistan

- Teams often work in Uzbek and Russian at once. Keep each quote in the language it was spoken in; write labels in the team's working language.
- Do not merge two notes just because their translations look alike; check the original wording.
- Polite or agreeing answers can produce many similar "everything is fine" notes. Tag notes as prompted or unprompted, and give more weight to described behaviour than to stated approval.
- Keep a location tag on every note (Tashkent or region, city or village) so the team can check whether a cluster comes from one place only.
