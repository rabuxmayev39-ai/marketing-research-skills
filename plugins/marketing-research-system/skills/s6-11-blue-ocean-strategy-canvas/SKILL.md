# Blue ocean strategy canvas (S6-11)

The strategy canvas is a one-page chart created by W. Chan Kim and Renée Mauborgne and popularised in their book Blue Ocean Strategy (2005). The horizontal axis lists the factors an industry competes on and invests in; the vertical axis shows how much of each factor buyers receive. Joining one company's points gives its value curve (also called its strategic profile). The authors pair the canvas with the four actions framework: four questions (eliminate, reduce, raise, create) used to redraw the curve so that an offer is both different and cheaper to deliver. In their vocabulary a "red ocean" is an existing market where firms fight over existing demand, and a "blue ocean" is new market space where demand is created.

Evidence grade: C. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. The authors' own site says the approach rests on a study of more than 150 strategic moves in more than 30 industries over more than 100 years. Those are cases chosen and read after the outcome was known, so they show what successful moves looked like, not that drawing a canvas causes success; companies that tried the same thing and failed are not counted. An academic working paper opened for this skill (Burke, van Stel and Thurik, 2009) makes the related point that evidence built mainly on case studies lacks generality, and finds that blue ocean and competitive strategies overlap in practice. Treat the canvas as a structured way to see sameness and generate options. It does not predict demand.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (what each offer contains, prices, research results) versus INTERPRETATION (scores given by the team, the new curve, expected demand). Never present a new curve as proof that a market exists. When the canvas and test plan are delivered, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- Offers in a category look alike and compete mainly on price or on "a bit more of everything".
- The client is willing to remove or cut things, not only to add.
- There is, or can be, evidence of what buyers and non-buyers value.

Do not use it when:
- The task is a communication fix for an unchanged offer (use S6-02, S9-02).
- The client cannot change product, service or cost structure.
- A demand forecast is required. The canvas gives options; sizing needs S4-23 and testing needs S1-09 or S1-10.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. A clear definition of the category and the buyer group (S6-01).
2. Three to five real competing offers, plus the main alternative from outside the category (what people do if they buy none of them).
3. Evidence about each offer: price lists, mystery shopping (S1-13), sites, interviews (S1-04), survey ratings (S1-08).
4. Evidence from people who do not buy from the category at all.
5. The client's cost of providing each factor, at least roughly.

## Procedure
1. Fix the frame. Write one sentence naming the category, buyer and geography. Ask the user to confirm.
2. List the competing factors. These are the things companies in the category invest in and advertise: price, range, speed, service, location and so on. Take them from competitor materials and from buyers' own words. Working rule: keep 6 to 10 factors; merge overlaps; treat price as a factor where a high score means a high price.
3. Choose the players. Working rule: plot the client, two or three competitors (or one line for a typical competitor) and, if useful, the outside alternative.
4. Score the offering level for each player on each factor. The authors' pages define the axis as the level buyers receive but give no numeric scale. Working rule: use 0 to 5 where 0 = not offered and 5 = highest in the market; write the evidence next to each score; mark scores without evidence as assumptions (S0-13).
5. Draw the "as-is" canvas and read it. The authors describe this diagnostic use: it shows where the industry invests and how similar the profiles look to buyers. Working rule: if the client's line lies within 1 point of the typical competitor on almost every factor, state plainly that buyers have little reason other than price to choose.
6. Look outside the current buyers. The authors offer two aids. Three tiers of noncustomers: people who buy only from necessity and are close to leaving, people who considered the category and refused, and people who never considered it. Six paths: look across alternative industries, other strategic groups within the industry, other buyer groups, complementary products and services, the functional versus emotional balance, and trends over time. Ask the user which of these the existing research covers; plan fieldwork for gaps (S1-04, S1-07).
7. Ask the four questions for every factor, and record the answers in an ERRC grid (a four-box table). Eliminate: which factors taken for granted add little for buyers and can be removed? Reduce: which are overbuilt and can go well below the industry standard? Raise: which should go well above the standard, removing compromises buyers now accept? Create: which factors never offered should be added? The authors stress asking all four together; they warn that a company which only raises and creates adds cost and over-engineers the offer.
8. Draw the "to-be" curve and check it. Working rule checks: (a) at least one factor eliminated or reduced; (b) the new line visibly departs from the competitor line on several factors; (c) the idea can be said in one sentence a buyer would understand; (d) the cost removed is compared with the cost added.
9. Turn the curve into testable assumptions. For each raised or created factor write what would have to be true (S5-05): enough buyers want it, will pay, and the client can deliver it. For each eliminated factor: buyers will not walk away.
10. Recommend tests before launch: concept interviews, a choice study (S1-10), MaxDiff on factors (S1-11) or a small live pilot (S7-02).
11. Deliver both canvases, the ERRC grid, the assumption list and the test plan. Return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. Input: an invented Tashkent sofa maker, "Divan Uy", sells mid-price sofas through a large showroom. Mystery shopping at three competitors and 12 buyer interviews supply the scores (0 to 5).

```
Factor                 Typical competitor   Client now   Client to-be   Action
Price level                    3                3             3         keep
Number of models               4                4             2         REDUCE
Fabric choice                  4                4             2         REDUCE
Showroom size                  4                3             0         ELIMINATE
Delivery speed                 2                2             5         RAISE
Instalment terms               3                3             3         keep
Warranty length                2                2             4         RAISE
Fixed delivery date            0                0             5         CREATE
Washable covers                0                0             4         CREATE
Gap to competitor (sum of absolute differences over 9 factors)        FACT of scoring
  now:   1 point   -> 1 / 9  = 0.11 per factor
  to-be: 22 points -> 22 / 9 = 2.44 per factor
Monthly cost, million soum: showroom rent removed -40; delivery crew +18;
  cover production +6; net change -16
READING                                                           INTERPRETATION
Today the client is almost a copy of its rivals. The to-be offer is "a sofa
in 7 days on a fixed date, with covers you can wash", sold from a small
sample room and online. Untested: whether buyers will order without a
big showroom. Test with a 2-month pilot in one district (S7-02).
```
Arithmetic check: now gaps 0+0+0+1+0+0+0+0+0 = 1; 1 / 9 = 0.11. To-be gaps 0+2+2+4+3+0+2+5+4 = 22; 22 / 9 = 2.44. Cost: -40 + 18 + 6 = -16. Actions: 1 eliminate + 2 reduce + 2 raise + 2 create + 2 keep = 9 factors.

## Common mistakes
- Only raising and creating, so the offer becomes dearer to make and no simpler.
- Scoring from the team's opinion and then treating the scores as data.
- Calling a new advertising message a blue ocean when the offer has not changed.
- Skipping non-buyers, so the canvas only mirrors existing customers.
- Treating the to-be curve as a forecast and launching without a test.

## Quality checklist
- Is the category, buyer and geography stated in one sentence?
- Does every score have a named piece of evidence or an "assumption" mark?
- Were all four questions answered, with at least one eliminate or reduce?
- Is price on the canvas and is the cost effect of the changes estimated?
- Were non-buyers or outside alternatives considered?
- Is each raised, created or eliminated factor written as a testable assumption?
- Does the report say that the method's evidence is case-based (grade C)?

## Output template
```
STRATEGY CANVAS - [client] - [category, buyer, geography] - [date]
AS-IS (FACT where evidenced)
Factor | competitor A | competitor B | outside alternative | client | evidence
Reading: where profiles converge ...
NONCUSTOMERS / PATHS LOOKED AT: ...
ERRC GRID (INTERPRETATION)
Eliminate: ... | Reduce: ... | Raise: ... | Create: ...
TO-BE CURVE: factor | new level | cost effect
One-sentence offer: ...
ASSUMPTIONS TO TEST: assumption | test | pass rule (working rule)
LIMITS: scores are judgments; method is grade C; demand untested
```

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Run workshops in the team's language and keep buyer phrases for factors in the original Uzbek or Russian; translated factor names often lose the buyer's meaning.
- Polite or agreeing answers can make every factor look "important". Prefer forced choices (S1-11) and observed behaviour over rating scales.
- Competitor facts may not be published. Plan mystery shopping and store visits; do not score from websites alone. Offers and alternatives may differ between Tashkent and the regions, so treat one canvas per geography as a working rule until evidence shows they match.
- Telegram-bot samples miss older and rural people, who may be the very non-buyers the method asks about.
- The published cases are mostly large firms in other economies. Treat any transfer as a hypothesis to test.
