# PESTEL (S0-09)

PESTEL is a checklist for scanning the world outside a company and its market: political, economic, social, technological, environmental and legal factors. Its root is Francis Aguilar's Scanning the Business Environment (1967), a study of how managers gather outside information, which grouped the environment into four sectors: economic, technical, political and social. Aguilar defined scanning as acquiring information about events, trends and relationships outside the organisation to help management plan its course. Later writers reordered the letters into PEST or STEP and added ecological and legal headings; who added which, and when, is not reliably recorded. The method is used to prepare market entry, to check regulated categories (food, health, finance, children's products, advertising rules), and to feed the "context" part of a situation analysis.

Evidence grade: C. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. Choo's review of the scanning literature reports several studies linking environmental scanning in general with better company performance, and adds that scanning helps only when it is tied to strategy and planning. That evidence concerns the habit of scanning, not the six-letter checklist; no test of the PESTEL form itself was found among the sources opened. The tool is descriptive: it lists conditions and does not say what to do. It is easy to fill with true but irrelevant facts. The CIPD, the UK professional body for people management, lists further drawbacks: too little data oversimplifies, too much causes paralysis, the data may rest on wrong assumptions, fast change makes the scan age quickly, and it works only if repeated. Treat a PESTEL as a sourced list of outside conditions for one decision, and nothing more.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (with source and date) versus INTERPRETATION (what the factor may mean for the decision). Never state a law, tax rate, tariff, licence rule or economic figure from memory: look up a current official source for each factor and cite it, or mark it UNKNOWN. Send every legal question to a qualified lawyer; the assistant's output is not legal advice. When the scan is written, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- A client plans to enter a new country, region or category.
- The category is regulated, or rules on advertising, labelling, licensing or data apply.
- A situation analysis (S0-07) needs its "context" heading filled with sourced facts.

Do not use it when:
- The question is about customers or competitors. Use S0-07, S0-10 or primary research.
- The decision is small and short-term, such as next month's posts, and no outside rule or trend bears on it.
- Nobody will verify sources. An unsourced PESTEL is worse than none because it looks like knowledge.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. The decision, the market (country, region, category) and the time horizon, for example 12 or 36 months (S0-02).
2. Access to current official sources: the legislation database, the statistics agency, the central bank, the relevant ministries and regulators, plus international bodies and industry associations. See S8-04 to S8-07 for the sources this agency uses.
3. Two or three people with different views (client, agency, an industry expert); the CIPD advises several contributors.
4. Access to a lawyer or compliance specialist for the legal and political headings.

## Procedure
1. Set the scope in one sentence: decision, market, horizon. Following the CIPD steps, cover both present conditions and plausible changes within the horizon.
2. Build a long list under the six headings. Prompts: Political - policy direction, stability, trade restrictions, state support. Economic - growth, inflation, exchange and interest rates, wages, credit. Social - population and age structure, norms, attitudes to health, lifestyles. Technological - new technologies and how fast they spread, payment and delivery infrastructure. Environmental - climate, resources, sourcing, emergencies. Legal - laws on products, labelling, advertising, employment, imports, tax, data. All six are explored even if some end up empty.
3. Source each factor before keeping it. Record the fact, the official source, its date and the date checked. Working rule: one primary source per factor (the law, the regulator, the statistics release), not a news article about it. No source: tag UNKNOWN and move it to the questions list.
4. Apply the relevance test, the step that keeps the scan from filling with irrelevant facts. For each factor complete the sentence "Because of this, for our decision ... changes by ...". Working rule: if the sentence cannot be completed with a concrete effect on demand, cost, price, access or permitted communication, drop the factor to the appendix.
5. Rate what remains, as the CIPD steps advise rating items by the risk they pose. Working rule: impact on the decision 1 to 5 times likelihood within the horizon 1 to 5; where a factor is already in force, likelihood is 5. Put at most ten factors on the main page.
6. Mark direction for each: opportunity, threat or both. Mark FACT for the condition and INTERPRETATION for the effect.
7. Route legal and regulatory factors. Write each as a question for a lawyer ("May a children's drink carry claim X on the label?") with the source found so far. Do not answer it yourself.
8. For the top factors write options (what the company could do) and an indicator to monitor with a named source and frequency. The CIPD stresses agreeing which trends to watch and repeating the scan. Working rule: review the watch list every quarter and redo the scan yearly or before any major decision.
9. Pass the top factors to S0-07 (context), S0-13 (assumptions) and, as a recap only, S0-08. Return to research-orchestrator.

## Common mistakes
- Filling every box with general facts about the country that change nothing in the decision.
- Writing laws, rates or figures from memory or from an old report.
- Citing news articles in place of the law or the statistical release.
- Treating legal findings as advice without a lawyer.
- Listing factors with no rating, so the long list is the output.
- Doing the scan once and never updating it.
- Arguing about which heading a factor belongs under; the heading does not matter, the effect does.
- Using PESTEL to describe customers or competitors.

## Quality checklist
- Are decision, market and horizon stated at the top?
- Does every kept factor have a primary source, its date and the date checked?
- Can each kept factor complete the "for our decision ... changes by ..." sentence?
- Are there ten factors or fewer on the main page, rated and ordered?
- Are fact and interpretation separated for each factor?
- Is every legal item written as a question to a lawyer?
- Is there a watch list with source and frequency?
- Is the date of the next review set?

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- This skill deliberately contains no facts about Uzbekistan's laws, economy or politics. Look each one up at the time of the project in the official source and cite it with its date.
- Practical caution: rules can change between the scan and the launch. Record the date checked for every legal and economic factor and re-check before the decision.
- Official texts may exist in Uzbek and Russian; cite the version you read and keep key terms in the original language.
- National averages may hide differences between Tashkent and the regions; where a factor is regional, say which region the source covers.
- Online survey and social media data miss older and rural people; do not use them alone for the social heading.
- Advertising, labelling, licensing and personal-data questions always go to a lawyer; the assistant only prepares the questions and sources.
