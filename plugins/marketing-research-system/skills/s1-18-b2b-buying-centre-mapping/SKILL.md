# B2B buying-centre mapping and key-informant interviews (S1-18)

A buying centre is the set of people in an organisation who take part in a particular purchase. The term comes from Robinson, Faris and Wind (1967). Frederick Webster and Yoram Wind (1972) built the best-known general model of organisational buying: buying is shaped by environmental, organisational, interpersonal and individual factors, and members of the buying centre play the roles of user, influencer, buyer, decider and gatekeeper. Later literature added the initiator, and the American Marketing Association's dictionary lists all six. Johnston and Bonoma (1981) showed how to map a buying centre in practice by following the communication about one specific purchase from person to person. This skill combines the role model with that mapping approach and with key-informant interviewing: find out who was involved in a real purchase, then interview each role.

Evidence grade: B. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. The role model is a long-established textbook standard; it is a classification, not a tested prediction. The research around it does give tested warnings: Silk and Kalwani (1980) found that informants in the same firm agreed fairly well on who took part but poorly on how much influence each had, and Phillips (1981) concluded from 506 firms that single-informant reports are not reliable enough to stand alone. Trust a map built from several people about one concrete purchase. Treat a map drawn from one informant, or about purchasing "in general", as a hypothesis.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (who did what in a named purchase, as reported, and by how many informants) versus INTERPRETATION (role labels, influence weights, generalisation to other firms). When the map and the interview findings are delivered, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- The client sells to organisations and the decision is whom to reach, with what message, in what order.
- Deals stall or are lost and nobody knows who inside the customer blocked them.
- The purchase is new, important or complex for the customer; Johnston and Bonoma found that such purchases involve more people, departments and levels.
- The team can reach at least two people in each customer organisation.

Do not use it when:
- The buyer is one owner who decides alone and no one else is involved; check this with two questions before planning a map, then use S1-04 or S1-07.
- The purchase is a routine repeat order placed by one clerk and the decision at stake is not about changing supplier.
- Only one informant per firm is available and the client wants firm conclusions about influence.
- The client wants market size or shares. Use S4-23 or S1-08.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. The decision and research question (S0-02, S2-01).
2. The product or service and the type of customer organisation.
3. A list of recent, specific purchases: won, lost and stalled deals, and customers of competitors.
4. A route in: one contact person per organisation who took part in the purchase.
5. The client's own sales team's view of who decides, written down before fieldwork (S2-11), for later comparison. Confidentiality terms the team can offer respondents (S2-10).

## Procedure
1. Check fit. Ask the user: "Is more than one person usually involved on the customer side? Can we name recent purchases and reach two or more people in each?" If not, return to the research-orchestrator skill with S1-04 or S1-07.
2. Fix the unit of analysis: one specific, recent purchase decision per organisation, not purchasing in general. Johnston and Bonoma studied purchases mostly completed within the previous six months; some of their informants still needed help to reconstruct their part. Working rule: prefer purchases decided in the last 6 months, accept up to 12.
3. Classify each purchase by buying situation (Robinson, Faris and Wind): new task, modified rebuy, straight rebuy. Record how important and how complex the purchase was for the customer. Expect a wider buying centre for new, important and complex purchases, and do not merge maps across situations.
4. Sample organisations on purpose (S2-06): vary size, sector, won and lost, and location. Working rule for a small project: 4 to 8 organisations, at least 2 informants in each, more for a new-task purchase.
5. Enter through one contact and snowball. Following Johnston and Bonoma: ask the first person who they communicated with about this purchase and what was discussed; interview each person named about their own part; continue until no new participant is named. In their study the entry contact was usually in purchasing; note that the entry point shapes who gets named, so ask every informant the same "who else" questions.
6. Use an interview guide built on the timeline of the purchase, not on the role labels (S3-04): How did the need first come up? Who raised it? What happened next, step by step? Who was asked, who prepared requirements? Which suppliers were considered and how did they come to be on the list? Who dropped any? Who compared the offers? On what criteria? What was argued about? Who had to approve? Who signed? Who placed the order and negotiated terms? Who uses the product now? What do they say about it? Who could have stopped it? Did anyone try? What information did you personally look for, and where? Ask about what the informant did and saw. Phillips advises simple questions about observable things and warns that broad judgments across many objects add error.
7. Assign roles after the interviews, from the reported actions. Working definitions, following the standard list: Initiator: first recognises the need or raises the idea. User: will work with the product; often shapes requirements. Influencer: affects the choice through specifications, advice or opinion, often a technical expert. Decider: has the authority to choose or approve the supplier. Buyer: negotiates terms and places the order. Gatekeeper: controls the flow of information and access to the others. One person can hold several roles and one role can be shared. Record people outside the firm who took part (consultant, designer, relative, auditor).
8. Describe the structure. Count, as Johnston and Bonoma did: levels of the hierarchy involved (vertical involvement), departments or functions involved (lateral involvement), total people involved (extensivity), and who talked to whom. Note any person said to have shaped the decision without taking part in the talks; their network approach admits it can miss such indirect influence.
9. Compare informants within each firm. Build a table of who named whom in which role. Treat participation named by two or more informants as confirmed. Treat influence claims with more care: Silk and Kalwani found low agreement on influence, and several earlier studies they review found people rating their own role higher than others rated it. Do not average away disagreement; report it.
10. For each role, summarise what that person needed, what information they used and what could make them say no. Keep these as separate role profiles; do not blend them into one "customer".
11. Compare across firms by buying situation and firm type (S4-05 with one row per firm and one column per role). Compare with the sales team's prior view from the inputs.
12. Report the maps, role profiles, disagreements and limits. Say plainly how many firms and informants each claim rests on. Then return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. A Tashkent furniture maker selling to hotels and restaurants wants to know whom to approach first on fit-out projects. Input: 4 recent fit-out purchases (2 won, 2 lost), all new-task; entry through the person the sales team knew; snowball interviews: 3 + 3 + 3 + 2 = 11 interviews. ## Common mistakes
- Interviewing one person per firm, usually the purchasing contact, and presenting their view as the firm's. Asking "who decides in your company?" in general instead of reconstructing one purchase.
- Asking informants to name their own role from the list; people tend to claim the decider role. Treating role labels as job titles. Roles change from purchase to purchase.
- Missing gatekeepers and outside advisers because nobody asked how suppliers got onto the list.
- Merging new-task purchases and routine reorders into one map. Averaging conflicting accounts instead of reporting the disagreement.
- Studying only won deals reached through the client's friends.

## Quality checklist
- Is each map about one named, recent purchase?
- Were at least two informants interviewed in each organisation?
- Was the snowball question asked of every informant until no new names came up?
- Were roles assigned from reported actions, not from titles or self-description?
- Is each participant marked with how many informants named them?
- Are disagreements between informants reported?
- Are purchases grouped by buying situation, and are lost deals included?
- Does the report state how many firms and informants each claim rests on?

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Interview each person in their preferred language, Uzbek or Russian, and keep quotes in the original. Role terms translate poorly; ask about actions and assign roles afterwards.
- Access usually depends on an introduction. Use it, but record who introduced whom, and add firms reached by another route, because a referral chain shows one network. Access is the main practical limit of this method; plan time for it.
- Junior staff may be unwilling to describe a superior's part, or may attribute every decision to the head of the firm out of respect. Interview people separately, ask about steps and documents instead of "who had the power", and treat "the director decides everything" as a claim to check with a second informant.
- Polite or agreeing answers: an informant may confirm the interviewer's guess about who decided. Do not offer guesses. Family members, founders who no longer hold a post, and outside advisers may take part. Ask the open question "who else was asked or informed?" and treat any pattern as a hypothesis, not a fact about local firms.
- State-linked buyers and tenders follow formal rules; collect the procedure documents as a second source and map the formal and the actual process separately. Compare Tashkent and regional organisations before merging. The models cited were built on North American firms decades ago; treat role patterns from that literature as hypotheses here.
