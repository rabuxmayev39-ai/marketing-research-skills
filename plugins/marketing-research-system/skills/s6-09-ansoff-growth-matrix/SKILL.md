# Ansoff growth matrix (S6-09)

The Ansoff matrix is a two-by-two table for sorting growth options. One side asks whether the product is existing or new for the company; the other asks whether the market (the customers, uses or places served) is existing or new. That gives four routes: market penetration (existing product, existing market), market development (existing product, new market), product development (new product, existing market) and diversification (new product, new market). It comes from Igor Ansoff's article "Strategies for Diversification" in Harvard Business Review in 1957. The usual reading is that risk rises as the company moves away from what it already knows: penetration lowest, the two middle routes moderate, diversification highest.

Evidence grade: C. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. The matrix is a classification, not a tested theory: it names options and says nothing about which will succeed, how large they are or what competitors will do. The risk ordering is reasoning, not a measured result; no source opened for this skill gives failure rates by quadrant. There is published criticism of its logic: John Dawes (2018) points out that "new" is ambiguous. A product truly new to the firm often takes it into an unfamiliar market at the same time, which makes the diversification box overlap with product development; and if a new product does not imply a new market, then new product plus new market is not always a leap into an unknown business. A professional-body teaching article adds that four fixed boxes simplify what are really continuous degrees of newness, and that the matrix should not be used alone. Put little weight on the label itself; the value is in making the team state what is new and what must be learned.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (current products, customers, sales) versus INTERPRETATION (the quadrant, the risk rating, any probability). Never present a quadrant as a recommendation. When the classified list is delivered, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- The client has several growth ideas mixed together and needs them sorted and named.
- The team wants to see whether all its ideas sit in one quadrant, or whether an obvious route has been ignored.
- A risky idea is being discussed as if it were routine and needs to be shown for what it is.

Do not use it when:
- The question is why sales are falling. That is diagnosis (S0-01, S0-05).
- A choice between options is needed. The matrix does not choose; go on to S4-23 and S5-05.
- There is only one option. A table with one entry adds nothing.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. The current products and the current markets, described from sales records: who buys, where, for what use, through which channel.
2. The list of growth ideas, from the client and from research (S5-11, S5-02).
3. For each idea, what the company would need that it does not have: knowledge of customers, channels, technology, licences, staff.
4. Any evidence on size and competitors for each idea (S4-23, S0-10).
5. The client's own rule for what counts as "new", or agreement to set one in step 2.

## Procedure
1. Describe the starting point as FACT. List current product lines and current markets with their share of sales. This is the top-left box and everything else is measured against it.
2. Fix what "new" means before sorting. Working rules, to deal with the ambiguity Dawes describes: a product is new if making or delivering it needs skills, equipment or suppliers the company does not use today. The teaching article opened for this skill makes the same point from the other side: minor changes to a product do not count as product development. A market is new if the company has no customers, no channel and no sales experience in that customer group, use or geography.
3. Write each idea as one sentence in the form "sell [product] to [customer group] in [place] through [channel]". Ideas that cannot be written this way are goals or slogans, not options.
4. Sort each idea into a quadrant using the step 2 rules. Penetration covers winning rivals' customers, getting current customers to buy more or more often, and converting non-users inside the current market. Market development covers new customer groups, new uses and new geographies. Product development is a significant new product for current customers. Diversification can be related (close to current skills, or a move up or down the supply chain) or unrelated.
5. Grade the degree of newness instead of stopping at the box. Working rule: rate product newness and market newness each from 0 (same as today) to 3 (nothing in common with today) and note what exactly must be learned. This follows the suggestion, reported in the teaching article, to treat the axes as continuous.
6. State the risk reading and its basis. The standard ordering is penetration lowest, market or product development moderate, diversification highest. Say that this is the framework's reasoning, and add the specific risks per idea: for a new market, lack of knowledge of local conditions; for a new product, development cost and failure; for both, the sum.
7. Check the spread. If every idea is diversification, ask why penetration was ignored (S6-03 argues most growth comes from more buyers in the existing market). If every idea is penetration in a shrinking market, note that the teaching article calls that route harder when the market declines.
8. List the evidence each idea needs next. Typical needs: penetration, share and buyer data (S0-05, S4-18); market development, sizing and channel research in the new market (S4-23, S1-03); product development, need and concept research with current customers (S1-07, S1-10); diversification, both.
9. Hand the labelled list on for sizing and choice (S4-23, S5-05, S6-08). Return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. Input: a Tashkent furniture maker selling sofas and chairs to restaurants and hotels has four growth ideas. Revenue figures are extra revenue per year in billion UZS; chances of success are the team's own guesses, not outputs of the matrix.

```
START (FACT, invented): upholstered seating -> restaurants and hotels in Tashkent
IDEA                               QUADRANT             NEW?    REVENUE  CHANCE  INVEST
                                                     (prod/mkt)
1 More restaurants and hotels      Penetration          0 / 0     6.0     60%     0.8
  in Tashkent
2 Same seating to restaurants in   Market development   0 / 2     5.0     40%     1.5
  Samarkand and Bukhara
3 Outdoor terrace furniture for    Product development  2 / 0     4.0     35%     1.2
  current clients
4 Desks and benches for schools    Diversification      2 / 3     9.0     15%     4.0
WEIGHTED REVENUE MINUS INVESTMENT                               INTERPRETATION
1: 6.0 x 0.60 = 3.60; 3.60 - 0.8 = 2.80
2: 5.0 x 0.40 = 2.00; 2.00 - 1.5 = 0.50
3: 4.0 x 0.35 = 1.40; 1.40 - 1.2 = 0.20
4: 9.0 x 0.15 = 1.35; 1.35 - 4.0 = -2.65
READING: idea 4 has the largest headline revenue and the weakest case. The
matrix only showed that it is new on both sides; the numbers are guesses
that S4-23 and S5-05 must now check. Evidence needed first: for idea 2,
channel and buyer research in the two cities.
```
Arithmetic check: 6.0 x 0.60 = 3.60; 5.0 x 0.40 = 2.00; 4.0 x 0.35 = 1.40; 9.0 x 0.15 = 1.35; 3.60 - 0.8 = 2.80; 2.00 - 1.5 = 0.50; 1.40 - 1.2 = 0.20; 1.35 - 4.0 = -2.65.

## Common mistakes
- Treating the quadrant as the answer and stopping there.
- Sorting without agreeing what "new" means, so the same idea lands in different boxes for different people.
- Calling a small change in colour or size product development.
- Presenting the risk ordering as measured fact.
- Leaving out competitors, market size and the company's own capacity to change.
- Assuming diversification is exciting and penetration is dull, and never examining the existing market.

## Quality checklist
- Is the current product-market position described from sales records?
- Are the rules for "new product" and "new market" written down?
- Is each idea a full sentence with product, customer, place and channel?
- Does each idea have a quadrant and a degree of newness on both sides?
- Is the risk reading marked as INTERPRETATION, with idea-specific risks named?
- Are any probabilities labelled as the team's guesses?
- Is the next evidence step listed for each idea?

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- "New market" often means the regions for a Tashkent firm, or the reverse. Practical caution: treat another region as a genuinely new market until channel and buyer research says otherwise; do not assume habits transfer.
- Neighbouring export markets are market development with extra unknowns (rules, logistics, payment). Write these as open questions; do not state them as facts without checking current sources (S8-07, S1-03).
- Owners may favour a new line of business for reasons of standing or opportunity. Keep the matrix neutral: show the newness and the evidence gap in both Uzbek and Russian versions of the table.
- Polite agreement in a workshop can hide doubt about a risky idea; collect the chance-of-success guesses in writing and separately before discussion.
