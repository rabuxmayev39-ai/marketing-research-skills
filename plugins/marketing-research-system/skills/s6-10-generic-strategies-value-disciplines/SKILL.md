# Generic strategies and value disciplines (S6-10)

These are two related frameworks for naming the basic way a business competes. Michael Porter's generic strategies (Competitive Strategy, 1980) combine two choices: the type of advantage (lower cost than rivals, or differentiation, meaning something customers value enough to pay more for) and the scope (the whole market or a narrow segment, called focus). That gives cost leadership, differentiation, and focus in a cost or a differentiation form. Porter warned that a firm mixing them without a clear choice risks being "stuck in the middle". Michael Treacy and Fred Wiersema (Harvard Business Review, 1993; The Discipline of Market Leaders, 1995) offered three value disciplines: operational excellence (reliable standard products at the lowest total cost), product leadership (the best or newest product) and customer intimacy (offers tailored to particular customers, with long relationships). Their rule: lead in one discipline, stay at an acceptable level in the other two, and build the whole operating model around the chosen one.

Evidence grade: B. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. Both frameworks are standard in strategy teaching, and Porter's has been tested many times, with mixed results. One study opened for this skill (124 UK manufacturers) found firms with a clear cost or differentiation orientation outperformed those without one, yet also found the strategies explained only part of the performance differences. An open-access review lists studies from Spain, Korea, India, Ghana, China and Greece in which firms combining low cost and differentiation (hybrids) did as well or better, mostly in turbulent conditions. So hybrids exist and "choose one" is not a law. The value disciplines rest on the authors' study of selected successful companies, which is weaker evidence; a reference page also records a 1995 controversy over bulk purchases of the book, which concerns its bestseller status and not its content. Use both as a consistency check on an offer, not as a rule that predicts profit.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (prices, costs, ratings, what the adverts say) versus INTERPRETATION (the strategy label and the verdict on consistency). Do not call a firm "stuck in the middle" on the label alone. When the check is delivered, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- The team needs to check whether price, product, service, operations and advertising of one offer point the same way.
- A how-to-win statement is being written (S6-08) and needs plain vocabulary.
- A client claims "best quality, lowest price and personal service" at once and the claim must be examined.

Do not use it when:
- The task is to find the problem. This framework labels a position; it does not diagnose (S0-01).
- The unit is a whole group with unrelated businesses. Apply it to one business line in one market at a time.
- There is no information on competitors' prices or costs. The labels are relative to rivals and cannot be assigned without them.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. The business line and market under review, with three to five named competitors.
2. Prices against competitors, and whatever is known of unit costs (S1-13 can collect prices).
3. What customers say they choose on and how they rate the firm (S4-15, S4-14).
4. The firm's operating facts: range, delivery, service staffing, sales incentives.
5. The firm's own claims: adverts, sales scripts, website (S4-21 can count them).

## Procedure
1. Fix the unit: one business line, one market, named rivals. Write the firm's own stated strategy in one sentence before looking at data (S2-11).
2. Collect the facts in a table: price index against the market average, cost position if known, range width, service level, and customer ratings on the main choice criteria. Mark each cell FACT with its source, or "unknown".
3. Classify on Porter's two questions. Advantage: is there evidence of lower cost than rivals, or of a price premium customers accept? Scope: whole market or a defined segment? Working rule: a low price without a cost advantage is a discount, not cost leadership; a "premium" claim without a price premium or a preference in blind comparison is not yet differentiation.
4. Classify on the value disciplines. Which one discipline does the firm lead in, on customer evidence? Are the other two at an acceptable level? Working rule for "acceptable": at or above the level customers call the minimum for considering a supplier (ask it directly, or use the competitor average); state which was used.
5. Build the consistency table. For each element of the offer and of the operating model (price, range, product quality, delivery, service, sales incentives, hiring, advertising claims) write whether it supports or contradicts the classified strategy. This is the core output. The sources give the logic: a product-leadership firm slowed by bureaucracy, or an operational-excellence firm with weak controls, is working against its own choice.
6. Handle hybrids honestly. If the evidence shows both low cost and a valued difference, do not force one label. Working rule: accept a hybrid only when the team can name what makes it possible (for example a process or a supply source rivals lack) and the facts show both a cost advantage and a preference. Otherwise record "no dominant orientation shown" and list what is missing.
7. Check sustainability in one line each: can rivals copy the cost advantage or the difference, and what stops them (S6-08's can't/won't test).
8. Write recommendations as removals of contradictions: change the element that conflicts, or change the stated strategy to match what the firm really does. Roger Martin notes one frequent trap: a differentiated firm adding a cheap "fighting brand" with no real way to win in the low-price segment.
9. Report the labels, the consistency table, the open unknowns and the recommended changes. Return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. Input: a Tashkent housing developer describes itself as "premium comfort". Facts collected from price lists, a buyer survey (n = 150) and an audit of 12 adverts.

```
FACTS (invented)
Price: 9.5 m UZS per m2 vs district average 10.0 -> 5.0% below
Build cost: 6.8 m per m2 vs peer estimate 6.5 -> 4.6% above
Margin per m2: 9.5 - 6.8 = 2.7 vs peers 10.0 - 6.5 = 3.5 (77% of peers)
Adverts: 7 of 12 premium claims (58.3%), 5 of 12 discount or instalment (41.7%)
Buyer ratings (1-5): finish quality 3.1; on-time handover 2.9; sales service 4.2
  Minimum acceptable named by buyers: 3.5
CLASSIFICATION                                                   INTERPRETATION
Porter: no cost advantage (costs above peers) and no price premium ->
  no dominant orientation shown
Value disciplines: leads in none; handover is below the acceptable level
CONSISTENCY (stated strategy: differentiation, whole market)
Supports: sales service 4.2; premium advert claims; lobby design; location
Contradicts: price below average; discount adverts; finish 3.1; handover 2.9;
  sales bonus paid on volume only
9 elements checked: 4 support, 5 contradict
RECOMMENDATION: either fund the premium (finish, handover) and stop discount
  messages, or restate the strategy as value pricing and cut cost.
```
Arithmetic check: (9.5 - 10.0) / 10.0 = -0.050, or 5.0% below; (6.8 - 6.5) / 6.5 = 0.046, or 4.6% above; 9.5 - 6.8 = 2.7; 10.0 - 6.5 = 3.5; 2.7 / 3.5 = 0.771, or 77%; 7 / 12 = 0.583; 5 / 12 = 0.417; 7 + 5 = 12; 4 + 5 = 9.

## Common mistakes
- Assigning a label from the firm's slogan and not from prices, costs and customer evidence.
- Calling a low price "cost leadership" when costs are not lower than rivals'.
- Declaring every mixed position "stuck in the middle" despite evidence that hybrids can work.
- Reading "lead in one discipline" as permission to be poor in the other two.
- Applying the labels to a whole company with several different businesses.
- Treating the label as a forecast of profit.

## Quality checklist
- Is the unit one business line in one market with named rivals?
- Are price and cost positions shown against competitors, with sources or "unknown"?
- Is the Porter label backed by evidence of a cost advantage or an accepted premium?
- Is the leading value discipline backed by customer evidence, and are the other two checked against a stated minimum?
- Does the consistency table cover operations and incentives, not only advertising?
- If a hybrid is claimed, is the enabling factor named?
- Are recommendations written as contradictions to remove?

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Cost data on competitors are rarely public. Mark cost position "unknown" instead of guessing, and rely on price evidence collected in the field (S1-13).
- Posted prices and paid prices may differ where bargaining and instalment terms are common; this is a practical caution to check per category, not a verified fact. Record the price actually paid.
- Polite or agreeing answer habits can lift all service ratings; compare the firm's ratings with competitors' from the same respondents, not with the scale midpoint (S3-07).
- A Telegram-bot buyer survey misses older and rural customers; say so when the "acceptable level" comes from such a sample, and check Tashkent and the regions separately.
- The supporting studies come mainly from Western and a few other economies. Whether a clear single orientation pays off here is a hypothesis.
