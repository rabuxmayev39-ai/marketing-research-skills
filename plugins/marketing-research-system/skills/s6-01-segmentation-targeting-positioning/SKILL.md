# Segmentation, targeting, positioning (S6-01)

Segmentation, targeting and positioning (STP) is a three-part strategic decision. Segmentation divides a market into groups of buyers who are similar inside a group and different between groups. Targeting chooses which groups to focus on. Positioning chooses the image the organisation wants to hold in those buyers' minds. The idea of segmentation as a strategy goes back to Wendell Smith (1956), who described treating a market with varied demand as several smaller, more uniform markets; the three-part sequence became the backbone of strategy teaching through textbooks such as Kotler and Keller's Marketing Management. The procedure below follows the open-access textbook by Sara Dolnicar, Bettina Grün and Friedrich Leisch (2018), which sets out segmentation analysis in ten steps.

Evidence grade: B. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. STP is the standard way strategy is taught and practised, and the statistical side of segmentation is well studied. What is not established is that a narrowly targeted brand grows faster. The Ehrenberg-Bass Institute reports, as an empirical regularity, that the customer profiles of competing brands seldom differ: rival brands sell to much the same kinds of people and differ mainly in how many buyers they have. So use STP to make choices explicit, and treat any claim that "our buyers are a special type" as something to test, not assume.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (segment sizes, scores, data used) versus INTERPRETATION (which segment is attractive, why). Do not present segments as natural groups found in the market; they are a useful way to cut it. When the choice is documented, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- A client must decide which buyers to design the offer, price and sales effort for, and resources do not allow serving everyone equally well.
- Buyers in the category clearly differ in needs, purchase volume or buying situation (for example small bakeries versus industrial bakeries).
- A positioning (S6-02) is about to be written and nobody has agreed whom it is for.

Do not use it when:
- The team has not decided whether segmenting is worth its cost. Dolnicar and colleagues list the costs: analysis time, a separate marketing mix per segment, and ongoing monitoring. Serving the whole market with one offer is a legitimate choice.
- The aim is only to plan advertising reach. For reach, the evidence above points to covering all category buyers (S6-03, S9-03), not a narrow segment.
- There is no data on buyers beyond the client's opinion. Do the research first (S1-08, S1-04).

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. A definition of the market: the category, the geography, and who counts as a category buyer.
2. Data on buyers: a survey (S1-08), customer records, or for a first pass a reasoned list of buyer types with estimated sizes (S4-23).
3. The organisation's strengths and limits: capacity, distribution, price level, sales team.
4. The current customer profile, to compare with the category as a whole.
5. People from sales, production and management who will have to live with the choice.

## Procedure
1. Decide whether to segment at all. Ask the user what would change if a segment were chosen: product, price, channel, message. If nothing would change, stop and record that an undifferentiated strategy is being used. The textbook names three options: undifferentiated (one mix for all), concentrated (one segment; suited to firms with few resources but high risk) and differentiated (several segments).
2. Set the selection criteria before looking at any segments. First the knock-out criteria, which a segment must pass: it is similar inside, distinct from other segments, large enough to be worth a tailored mix, matched to what the organisation can deliver, identifiable in the market, and reachable. The team must put a number on "large enough". Then choose attractiveness criteria. The textbook, citing McDonald and Dunbar, advises no more than about six, weighted by having team members spread 100 points across them and negotiating to agreement.
3. Choose the basis for dividing the market. Commonsense segmentation uses one variable chosen in advance (for example type of business or region). Data-driven segmentation groups buyers on several variables by analysis (S4-11, S4-12). The two are often combined. Working rule: prefer variables that describe needs, buying situations or volume over demographics alone, because they connect more directly to what the client can change (S5-09).
4. Collect or compile the data and extract segments. The textbook warns that analysis cannot rescue poor data and that clear-cut natural segments are rare (it reports finding them in 2 of 32 tourism data sets). If segments come from cluster analysis, check that a repeat run gives similar groups.
5. Profile and describe each segment: size in buyers, size in volume or value, needs, how they buy, where they can be reached, who serves them now. One row per segment.
6. Apply the knock-out criteria. Remove segments that fail any of them and record why.
7. Score the remaining segments. Rate each on every attractiveness criterion (working rule: 1 to 10), multiply by the weight, sum, and divide by 100. The textbook's segment evaluation plot puts segment attractiveness on one axis and the organisation's relative competitiveness on the other; use it if the team wants a picture.
8. Run the breadth check (this skill's addition, based on the Ehrenberg-Bass finding). State what share of category buyers and what share of category volume the chosen target covers. Compare the client's current customer profile with the profile of all category buyers. Working rule: if the target covers under half of category buyers, write down explicitly what growth is being given up and why. Working rule: separate the "design target" (whose needs shape the offer) from the "reach target" (whom communication should reach); keep the reach target broad.
9. Select one or a few segments. The textbook calls this the point of no return, since strategy, product and channels will follow. The decision belongs to the client's managers, not to the analyst.
10. Set the positioning for the chosen target (hand over to S6-02) and only then plan the marketing mix (S6-14). The textbook's order is segmentation and targeting first, positioning second, tactics last.
11. Record how the choice will be reviewed: segment sizes and needs change. Return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. Input: a flour mill in Fergana region surveys 300 businesses that buy flour and asks monthly use. Knock-out rule set in advance: a segment must hold at least 4% of volume. Weights: volume 30, growth 15, fit with the mill's strengths 25, reachability 15, low competition 15.

```
SEGMENT PROFILE                                                      FACT
Segment                 Outlets  t/month each  Volume t  Share of volume
A Tandir bread bakeries   150        4.0         600        30.8%
B Pastry shops             60        1.5          90         4.6%
C Industrial bakeries      30       40.0       1,200        61.5%
D Canteens, restaurants    60        1.0          60         3.1%
Total                     300                  1,950       100.0%
KNOCK-OUT: D fails (3.1% is below 4%).
SCORES (rating 1-10 x weight)                    ratings are INTERPRETATION
A: 7x30 + 5x15 + 8x25 + 8x15 + 5x15 = 680 -> 6.80
C: 10x30 + 4x15 + 4x25 + 6x15 + 3x15 = 595 -> 5.95
B: 3x30 + 7x15 + 7x25 + 6x15 + 6x15 = 550 -> 5.50
DECISION: design target A; serve B with the same product range.
BREADTH CHECK: A + B = 210 of 300 outlets (70%) but 690 of 1,950 t (35.4%).
  Given up for now: C, 61.5% of volume, which needs tender skills and
  bulk delivery the mill lacks. Review in 12 months.         INTERPRETATION
```
Arithmetic check: 150 x 4 = 600; 60 x 1.5 = 90; 30 x 40 = 1,200; 60 x 1 = 60; sum 1,950. 600 / 1,950 = 30.8%; 90 / 1,950 = 4.6%; 1,200 / 1,950 = 61.5%; 60 / 1,950 = 3.1%; shares sum to 100.0%. A: 210 + 75 + 200 + 120 + 75 = 680. C: 300 + 60 + 100 + 90 + 45 = 595. B: 90 + 105 + 175 + 90 + 90 = 550. 210 / 300 = 70%; 690 / 1,950 = 35.4%.

## Common mistakes
- Choosing criteria after seeing the segments, so that the favourite segment wins.
- Treating segments from a cluster analysis as real, fixed groups without checking that they repeat.
- Picking a target so narrow that most category buyers are excluded, then using the same narrow target for media.
- Describing the current customers and calling that a segment, without comparing them with all category buyers.
- Counting segments by number of buyers only and ignoring volume or value (or the reverse).
- Writing the positioning before the target is agreed.

## Quality checklist
- Was the decision to segment (or not) made explicitly, with what would change named?
- Were knock-out and weighted attractiveness criteria written down before segments were scored?
- Is each segment sized in both buyers and volume or value?
- Do the weights sum to 100 and are the scores reproducible?
- Does the report state the share of category buyers and volume the target covers?
- Are the design target and the reach target stated separately?
- Is a review date set?

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Run the survey in Uzbek and Russian and check that segment descriptions mean the same in both; keep respondents' own words in the original language.
- Segment sizes from a Telegram-bot sample will understate older and rural buyers. Do not read bot-sample shares as market shares; size segments from records or statistics where possible (S8-07).
- Tashkent and the regions may differ in channels and buying habits. Treat region as a candidate segmentation variable to test, not as a known divide.
- Polite or agreeing answers can make every need look important and flatten differences between segments (S3-07). Ask about past behaviour and volumes as well as attitudes.
- The finding that rival brands share similar buyers comes mostly from Western and developed-market data. Treat it here as a hypothesis worth checking with the client's own customer data.
