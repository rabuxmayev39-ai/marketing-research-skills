# Pyramid principle storyline for research reports (S5-13)

The pyramid principle is a way to organise a document so the reader gets the main answer first and the supporting points below it in logical groups. Barbara Minto developed it at McKinsey & Company, where she worked from 1963 to 1973, and published it as The Pyramid Principle. The introduction follows Situation, Complication, Question, Answer (SCQA; Minto's own site calls the framing part the SCQ framework). In a research agency it is used to turn a pile of findings into a storyline the client can follow and act on.

Evidence grade: B. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. The method is a long-standing norm in consulting and business writing and is taught in universities, but it is a convention for clear communication, not a research-tested technique. It improves how findings are understood; it does not make weak findings stronger. Minto describes the pyramid as a tool for finding out what you think, so gaps it exposes are a signal to go back to the analysis.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). In the storyline, mark each supporting point as FACT (data) or INTERPRETATION (the team's reading or recommendation). When the storyline is approved, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- Analysis is finished and the team must write a report, deck, or summary for a decision-maker.
- A draft exists but reads as "what we did" in order of fieldwork.
- The client asks "so what should we do?" and the report does not say.

Do not use it when:
- Analysis is not done. A pyramid needs an answer at the top. Go back to S5-01 to S5-03.
- The deliverable is a methods appendix, a transcript file, or a data table.
- The purpose is open exploration with the client (a workshop), where giving the answer first would close the discussion.
- The reader is likely to reject the conclusion outright. Still build the pyramid for your own thinking, but consider presenting the reasoning before the conclusion (see step 6).

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. The decision the client has to make and the original research question (from the brief or S0-03).
2. Checked findings and insights with their evidence (S5-01, S5-02, S5-03).
3. Who the main reader is, what they already know and believe, and how much time they will give.
4. Format and length limits (slides, pages, language).

## Procedure
1. Identify the reader and the decision. Ask: "Who decides on the basis of this report, and what will they do differently after reading it?" If there are several readers, choose the one who decides.

2. Draft the introduction as SCQA.
   - Situation: facts the reader already knows and agrees with. No news here. Two or three sentences.
   - Complication: what changed or went wrong in that stable situation and creates the reason to write. It need not be a problem; it can be a change or an opportunity.
   - Question: the question the complication raises in the reader's mind. One question. It should match the decision from step 1.
   - Answer: your main point, in one sentence that could stand alone.
   Test: if the reader would not ask this question after hearing the situation and complication, rewrite them.

3. Write the governing thought (the Answer) as a full sentence with a verb and a position. "Findings on bakers" is a topic, not an answer. "The mill should compete on delivery certainty, not price" is an answer. If the research does not support any answer, state that plainly as the answer and say what is missing.

4. Build the key line: the two to four points directly under the answer. Each one answers the question the reader would naturally ask on hearing the answer, usually "why?" or "how?". All key-line points must answer the same question.

5. Work down each branch with the vertical rule. Every statement raises a question in the reader's mind (why? how? what do you mean?); the level below must answer it. Do not raise a question you do not answer, and do not answer one the reader has not been led to ask. Stop when the next level would be raw evidence: quotes, counts, observations.

6. Choose the horizontal logic for each group.
   - Inductive grouping (default at the key line): several points of the same kind that together support the point above. You should be able to name the group with a plural noun (reasons, steps, segments, barriers).
   - Deductive chain: each point follows from the previous one and leads to a "therefore". Use it lower down, for short arguments, or when the reader must be walked through reasoning before accepting the conclusion. It is heavier for the reader, so avoid it at the key line when possible.

7. Apply the three pyramid rules to every group.
   - The point above summarises the points below. A summary states what they add up to; "there are three problems" only announces them.
   - Points in a group are the same kind of thing.
   - Points in a group are in a logical order: time (steps, sequence), structure (parts of a whole), or rank (most important first). Pick one and state it.

8. Check each group for overlaps and gaps (MECE: mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive). Overlap: could one piece of evidence sit under two points? Merge or redraw. Gap: is there an obvious part of the whole that is missing? Add it or say why it is out.

9. Attach evidence at the base. Under each lowest-level point list the supporting data: number of interviews, quotes in the original language, figures, sources. Label FACT. Label recommendations and readings INTERPRETATION. If a point has no evidence, either remove it or mark it as an assumption. Include known disconfirming cases (S4-07) under the point they qualify.

10. Build bottom-up as a cross-check. Take any findings not yet placed. Group them, write a one-sentence summary for each group, and see whether the summaries fit under the existing key line. If a strong group does not fit, the answer may be wrong or incomplete; revise the answer rather than hiding the group.

11. Turn the pyramid into a storyline. Write each key-line and second-level point as a full-sentence headline. Read the headlines alone, top to bottom: they should tell the whole argument. Replace empty headings such as "Findings" or "Conclusions" with the actual point. Because readers jump around, each section should make sense by itself.

12. Review with the user. Show SCQA, the answer, and the headline list before any slides or prose are written. Ask: "Does the answer respond to the client's question? Is anything important missing or overstated?" Revise, then hand over and return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. A housing developer in Tashkent asked why site visitors do not sign contracts. Twelve interviews with recent visitors.

```
S  The company gets steady visits to its sales office from online ads.
C  Over the last two quarters fewer visitors signed, although prices
   and ad spend did not change.
Q  What stops visitors from signing, and what should be changed first?
A  Visitors leave because they cannot verify the handover date; the
   company should first make construction progress checkable.
                                                      [INTERPRETATION]
KEY LINE (answers "why do you say that?" - inductive, ranked)
1. Doubt about the handover date is the most frequent reason to wait.
   - 8 of 12 raised it unprompted (FACT)
   - "Qachon topshirishadi, hech kim aniq aytmadi" (R5) (FACT)
   - Negative case: 2 buyers with relatives in the firm had no doubt (FACT)
2. Price and layout, which the sales team blames, rarely decide.
   - 3 of 12 named price as main reason (FACT)
   - 10 of 12 were satisfied with layouts (FACT)
3. Visitors already try to check progress themselves and fail.
   - 5 of 12 drove to the site; "Охрана не пустила" (R9) (FACT)
NEXT (answers "how?" - time order)  [INTERPRETATION]
 a. Monthly dated photo report sent to every visitor
 b. Guided site visits on Saturdays
 c. Handover date and penalty shown in the contract summary
```

Headline test: reading only A and points 1-3 gives the full argument.

## Common mistakes
- Writing the report in the order the work was done (method, sample, findings, then conclusion at the end).
- A topic at the top instead of an answer.
- A situation that contains news the reader does not yet accept.
- Key-line points that answer different questions (two "why" points and one "how").
- Group headings that label instead of summarise ("three barriers").
- Mixed kinds in one group (a finding, a recommendation and a quote side by side).
- Overlapping points, or an obvious missing part.
- Using the pyramid to make thin evidence look solid, or leaving out data that does not fit.
- Long deductive chains at the top that make the reader wait for the point.

## Quality checklist
- Does the introduction contain all four parts, with a situation the reader already accepts?
- Is the answer one full sentence that responds to the client's question?
- Does each key-line point answer the same single question raised by the answer?
- Does every higher point summarise, not just label, the points below it?
- Are points in each group the same kind, in a stated order, without overlap or obvious gaps?
- Does every lowest-level point carry evidence, labelled FACT or INTERPRETATION?
- Do the headlines alone tell the full argument?
- Are disconfirming cases shown where they apply?

## Output template
```
PYRAMID STORYLINE - [client / project] - [date]
Reader and decision: ...

INTRODUCTION
Situation: ...
Complication: ...
Question: ...
Answer (governing thought, one sentence): ...          [INTERP]

KEY LINE (question answered: why? / how?; logic: inductive/deductive;
          order: time / structure / rank)
1. [full-sentence point]
   1.1 [sub-point]
       - evidence: [n of N, quote in original language, figure]  [FACT]
       - disconfirming case: ...                                 [FACT]
2. ...
3. ...

RECOMMENDED ACTIONS (order: ...)                        [INTERP]
a. ...  b. ...  c. ...

UNPLACED FINDINGS (and why they are left out): ...
OPEN QUESTIONS / LIMITS OF THE EVIDENCE: ...
HEADLINE LIST (for slides or sections): ...
```

## Works with
- S0-03 issue tree: the issue tree structures the question at the start; the pyramid structures the answer at the end, and its key line often mirrors the tree's branches.
- S5-01 affinity diagram, S5-02 insight ladder, S5-03 insight tests: supply the grouped, checked findings for the base.
- S4-07 negative case analysis and S2-08 trustworthiness criteria: supply the disconfirming cases and limits to show.
- S5-04 Four Points: a short alternative for a single finding; the pyramid organises the full report.
- S5-14 member checking: do it before the storyline is fixed, so changes flow in.
- S5-11, S5-12, S6-02, S6-08, S6-18: their outputs can be reported with this storyline, or the storyline's answer feeds them.

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Draft the pyramid in the language of the final report (Uzbek or Russian). Keep respondent quotes in the language spoken; do not translate them into the report language without showing the original.
- Reports often go to an owner-founder with little time. Give the answer and key line on the first page or slide. If the answer contradicts the owner's known view, prepare a short deductive path to it and put the strongest evidence right under the answer.
- Opening with the conclusion can feel abrupt in a formal meeting. A brief courteous opening is fine, but keep SCQA and the answer within the first minute.
- If the client expects a traditional structure (goals, method, sample), move those to an appendix or one slide after the answer rather than dropping them.
