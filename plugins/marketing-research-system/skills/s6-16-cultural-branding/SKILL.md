# Cultural branding (S6-16)

Cultural branding is an approach to brand strategy developed by Douglas Holt in How Brands Become Icons (2004) and extended with Douglas Cameron in Cultural Strategy (2010). Its claim is that the strongest brands in identity-heavy categories (products people use to say who they are, such as clothing, drinks, cars, homes) did not win through better features or a list of image adjectives. They won by offering a story that helped people deal with a tension between what society tells them to be and how their lives really are. Holt calls that tension a cultural contradiction and the story an identity myth. The myth is made believable by borrowing from a populist world: a group seen as living by its own beliefs and not for money or power. The 2010 book restates this as an ideology (a point of view) expressed through myth and cultural codes (the look, language and symbols that carry it), offered at a moment when social change has opened an ideological opportunity.

Evidence grade: B. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. The approach is an established school of brand strategy, but its evidence is case-based. Holt's method, which he calls brand genealogy, traces successful brands backward through decades of their advertising and social history. That explains past winners; it does not test whether following the steps produces a winner, and brands that tried and failed are not studied in the same way. Published commentary opened for this skill adds that the first book leaned on American cases, that the 2010 book gives two slightly different versions of its stage model, that the authors say little about downsides such as alienating part of the public, and that the account is advertising-centred. Treat the method as a disciplined way to generate and check a brand idea, not as a formula.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (what ads show, what people said) versus INTERPRETATION (the tension, the myth). Never assert what a society believes; write every tension as a hypothesis with its evidence and what would disprove it. When the cultural brief is delivered, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- The category is identity-heavy and competitors all say similar things.
- The client wants a long-term brand idea and has budget for brand communication over years.
- Time and access exist for qualitative research and content analysis.

Do not use it when:
- The category is bought on price, availability or specification (most B2B supplies, commodities). Use S6-03, S6-13 or S6-17.
- The client needs sales this quarter; this is slow brand building (S6-05).
- The client is not willing to take a point of view that some people will dislike.
- The tension touches religion, ethnicity, politics or family matters in a way that could harm people or the client. Working rule: stop and get senior and legal advice first.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. Category and brand history: the client's past communication and the main competitors' ads from recent years.
2. Target group definition (S6-01) and any existing research on it.
3. Access to people for in-depth interviews (S1-04) and to social media discussions (S1-14).
4. Desk sources on social and economic change (S1-03, S0-09, S8-05, S8-06, S8-07).
5. The client's limits: topics it will not touch, and its real behaviour, since a myth the company does not live by will not be believed.

## Procedure
1. Check fit using the lists above and record the decision.
2. Map the category orthodoxy: the taken-for-granted way all brands in the category present themselves. Collect a sample of competitor ads and code what each promises, shows and implies about the buyer (S4-21). Working rule: at least 40 to 60 ads across the main brands and channels. Output: a table of shared conventions with counts. This is FACT about ads, not about consumers.
3. Look for social disruption: changes that make the orthodoxy fit people's lives less well. Holt and Cameron describe these as historical shifts that loosen people's ties to existing brands. Use desk research and published data only; cite each source.
4. Form tension hypotheses. Write two to four statements in the form: "People in [group] are told [ideal] but experience [reality], and feel [strain]." Mark each HYPOTHESIS. Holt notes that resonant myths grow from everyday worries about work, family and change, so look there and not only at big public debates.
5. Research the hypotheses. Use interviews about real episodes (S3-04), indirect and projective questions where the topic is sensitive (S3-06, S3-10), and netnography (S1-14). Look for negative cases (S4-07). Working rule: keep a hypothesis only if it appears unprompted in the stories of a clear majority of interviews in the target group and you can describe who does not feel it.
6. Find source material. Identify groups, places, crafts or traditions that the target group sees as living sincerely by a different answer to the tension; Holt's term is populist world, and examples in the summaries opened include sports communities, frontier life and immigrant neighbourhoods. Check that the brand has a real, honest link to that world. Borrowing without a link is the main way these brands get rejected. Working rule.
7. Write the cultural brief. One summary of Holt's method opened for this skill gives three parts: the myth treatment (the story that addresses the tension), populist authenticity (which world it draws on and why the brand has a right to), and a charismatic aesthetic (a distinctive style). Add the cultural codes to use and to avoid.
8. Prototype and test. Holt's firm describes testing concepts through qualitative research. Show two or three rough expressions to people in the target group (S9-06). Ask what the brand seems to stand for and who it is for, not whether they like it.
9. Run a risk review: who could be offended or excluded, what the worst reading is, whether company conduct matches the story. Agree stop rules with the client.
10. Plan for continuity. Holt's 2016 article, as summarised in a secondary source, advises keeping the idea alive by responding to current debates relevant to it. Set measures (S7-01, S1-22). Return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. The tension below is made up to show the format; it is not a claim about any real society. Input: a Tashkent furniture maker wants a brand idea for its home range.

```
ORTHODOXY (60 competitor ads coded)                                      FACT
"European-style luxury, showroom-perfect rooms": 45 ads = 75%
Price and instalments: 9 ads = 15% | Other: 6 ads = 10%
TENSION HYPOTHESES                                             INTERPRETATION
H1: Young families are shown a perfect showroom home as the ideal, but their
    home must work for children and frequent guests; they feel judged.
H2: Buyers want new styles but fear losing a sense of their own home.
INTERVIEW CHECK (16 interviews, stories about furnishing a home)         FACT
H1 raised unprompted in 11 of 16 = 69% | H2 in 5 of 16 = 31%
Negative cases for H1: 5 people, mostly without children at home.
DECISION: keep H1 as working hypothesis; drop H2 for now.
CULTURAL BRIEF (draft)
Myth treatment: a home is judged by how well people live in it together.
Authentic source: the maker's own workshop craftsmen; link is real (FACT).
Aesthetic: real homes in use, not staged rooms.
Risk: may read as criticism of buyers who like the luxury look. Test first.
```
Arithmetic check: 45 + 9 + 6 = 60; 45 / 60 = 0.75; 9 / 60 = 0.15; 6 / 60 = 0.10; 11 / 16 = 0.6875, rounded to 69%; 5 / 16 = 0.3125, rounded to 31%; 16 - 11 = 5.

## Common mistakes
- Stating a tension as a fact about society because it sounds convincing in the meeting room.
- Copying a tension from American or European cases and assuming it exists locally.
- Confusing a trend (something popular now) with a contradiction (a strain people feel).
- Attaching the brand to a cause or subculture it has no honest link with.
- Writing a myth the company's own conduct contradicts.
- Skipping the risk review.
- Expecting short-term sales from a long-term identity idea.

## Quality checklist
- Is the category orthodoxy shown with counts from a real sample of ads?
- Is every tension written as a hypothesis with evidence for and against?
- Were negative cases looked for and reported?
- Is each claim about social change tied to a cited source?
- Does the brand have a real link to the source world it borrows from?
- Was a risk review done and were stop rules agreed?
- Are FACT and INTERPRETATION separated throughout?

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- No tension is assumed here. Any statement about what people in Uzbekistan feel must come from the team's own research or a cited local source and stay labelled as a hypothesis until checked.
- Holt's cases are mainly American, and his terms (national ideology, populist worlds) were built on that history. Whether the same mechanism works here is itself a hypothesis.
- Research Tashkent and the regions separately, and Uzbek-speaking and Russian-speaking groups separately; do not merge them before comparing.
- People may give polite or socially approved answers on identity topics. Prefer stories about real events and indirect questions, and keep quotes in the original language.
- Telegram and social media listening miss many older and rural people; say so in the brief.
- Topics linked to religion, nationality, gender roles or politics carry real risk for a client. Take local legal and senior advice before any public use.
