# Critical incident technique (S1-16)

The critical incident technique (CIT) is a set of procedures for collecting concrete accounts of events that clearly helped or harmed an outcome, and for classifying them. John Flanagan developed it in US aviation psychology in the 1940s and described it in 1954 in five steps: set the general aim, plan and specify, collect the data, analyse, interpret and report. Bitner, Booms and Tetreault (1990) brought it into service research, and Dwayne Gremler (2004) reviewed 141 service studies that used it and proposed guidelines for applying and reporting it. In marketing, respondents describe in their own words a specific encounter that left them very satisfied or very dissatisfied; the incidents are then sorted into categories that show what the firm does that wins or loses customers.

Evidence grade: A. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. The method has a long record in peer-reviewed research. Give good weight to the content of the incidents and to categories that two judges can apply consistently. Give little weight to frequencies as estimates for all customers: the method depends on memory and deliberately collects extremes, so it says little about ordinary, unremarkable service.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (what the respondent reported happened, in their words) versus INTERPRETATION (the category assigned, the cause inferred, the recommendation). When the classified incidents are delivered, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- The decision is about service failures, complaints, recovery, delight or why customers switched or stayed.
- The team cannot list in advance all the things that matter; CIT needs no prior hypotheses and lets respondents decide what is important.
- Concrete stories are needed for training, service standards or later survey items.
- Respondents have had direct, recent contact with the firm or its competitors.

Do not use it when:
- The question is about routine, everyday experience or average satisfaction. CIT covers extremes. Use S1-08 or S1-17.
- The events are long past. Flanagan showed that delay loses incidents and biases recall toward the dramatic.
- The decision needs the share of customers affected by each problem. Use a survey built from the CIT categories.
- Respondents only have general impressions and cannot describe a specific event.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. The decision and research question (S0-02, S2-01).
2. A clear statement of the activity being studied (for example "buying a flat from enquiry to handover") and what counts as a good outcome.
3. Access to customers, lost customers or staff who personally experienced incidents recently.
4. A collection mode: individual interviews (Flanagan's preference), group interviews, or written forms.
5. Two judges for classification, plus a third person for disputes if possible. A spreadsheet with one row per incident.

## Procedure
1. Check fit. Ask the user: "Are we trying to learn which specific events make customers very pleased or very upset, or how satisfied customers are in general?" and "Can we reach people who had such an experience in the last months?" If the answer is general satisfaction or old events, return to the research-orchestrator skill with S1-08 or S1-04.
2. Step 1, general aim. Write one short sentence on the aim of the activity that the client and the team accept, for example "the buyer gets the promised flat on time without unexpected cost or effort". Flanagan's test is whether those who will use the results accept the aim.
3. Step 2, plans and specifications. Write down: which situations count (channel, period, own firm or competitors); who may report (only people who experienced or directly saw the event); and the criteria for a usable incident. Flanagan's criteria: actual behaviour is reported; the reporter observed it; the relevant situation is described; the reporter judged it critical; the reporter says why. A service study in the Bitner tradition (Marselos 2010) required interaction between customer and employee, a very satisfying or dissatisfying result, a discrete episode, enough detail to picture it, and occurrence within the last 12 months. Working rule: set the recall window as short as recruitment allows, and never beyond 12 months.
4. Write and pretest the question. Wording changes what is reported. Flanagan advises asking what the person did, not how they behaved, and limiting the choice (for example "the most recent time") so that dramatic or stereotyped stories do not dominate. One opened study (Moreira and Troccoli) split a single question into parts after a failed pilot: the reason for the contact, what was said and done, and what caused the feeling. A workable set built on that (the first and last questions are this skill's additions): "Think of a recent time when dealing with [firm] left you very satisfied (or very dissatisfied). When was it? What was the reason for the contact? What exactly was said and done? What made you feel that way? What did you do afterwards?" Pretest with a few typical respondents and ask them to restate the task in their own words.
5. Decide whether to collect positive incidents, negative ones or both. Collect both unless the brief is only about failures; ask for each separately.
6. Step 3, collect. Let the respondent talk; do not lead. Wait for recall instead of prompting with examples. Record situation, behaviour and consequence for each incident, in the original language. Treat vague accounts as a sign of poor recall. Written forms reach more people but give shorter, more ambiguous stories; interviews allow probing for detail.
7. Screen incidents against the criteria from step 3. Count and report how many were collected and how many were dropped, and why.
8. Decide when there are enough. Flanagan: 50 to 100 incidents can be enough for a simple activity; the test is that adding 100 more incidents yields only two or three new behaviours. Working rule for small projects: keep collecting until a fresh batch of about 20 incidents adds no new category, and state the count. Count incidents, not respondents.
9. Step 4, analyse. (a) Choose the frame of reference from the intended use: by stage of the customer journey, by source of the problem (firm, employee, other customers), or by type of employee response. (b) Sort a first sample into tentative piles, name and define each pile, then classify more incidents and revise. Split large categories into subcategories. Flanagan describes this step as inductive and partly subjective. (c) Choose the level of detail by what the client can act on: a few broad categories or many specific ones. Give categories neutral, parallel names. (d) A second judge classifies the incidents independently using the definitions. Report agreement and resolve differences; a third person decides disputes. (e) Check the scheme against the criteria Keaveney (1995) is reported to have used: a confirmatory sample adds no new categories, agreement between judges is high, fewer than 5 percent of incidents fall into "other", and categories do not overlap.
10. Step 5, interpret and report. Flanagan locates most errors here. State the aim, who reported, the recall window, how incidents were selected, and each bias these introduce. Report category counts as counts of incidents in this sample, with one or two typical incidents per category in the original wording. Separate positive and negative schemes.
11. Hand over. Categories become candidate items for a survey (S1-08, S3-01), inputs to S4-16 or S4-15, or a training list. Then return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. A Tashkent housing developer wants to know what causes buyers to warn friends against the company. Input: aim as in step 2; 40 buyers who received keys in the last 12 months, interviewed by phone in Uzbek or Russian; 73 incidents collected, 5 dropped as general opinions with no event, leaving 68 usable: 30 positive and 38 negative (30 + 38 = 68). ## Common mistakes
- Accepting opinions ("service is bad") as incidents. An incident has a time, a place, actions and a result. Asking for "your worst experience ever", which invites old, dramatic stories.
- Collecting only negative incidents when the brief also asks what to keep doing.
- One person building and applying the categories with no second judge.
- Fitting incidents into categories taken from a textbook before reading them.
- Reporting "32 percent of customers suffer late handover" from incident counts. Not reporting how many incidents were dropped and why.
- Concluding that everyday service is fine or poor; the method did not look at it.

## Quality checklist
- Is the aim of the activity written in one sentence the client accepts?
- Are the criteria for a usable incident written down, with a recall window?
- Was the question pretested, and does it ask for a specific recent event and what was done?
- Is every kept incident a specific event with situation, action and consequence?
- Did a second judge classify independently, and is agreement reported?
- Is "other" under 5 percent and do categories not overlap?
- Does the report give the number of incidents collected, dropped and kept?
- Does the report say that frequencies describe extremes in this sample only?

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Collect incidents in the respondent's language and keep the typical incidents in the original Uzbek or Russian in all working files.
- Polite or agreeing habits may make respondents reluctant to describe a bad experience to a stranger, especially if they think the interviewer represents the firm. Say who is asking, promise anonymity, ask for the positive incident first, and use an interviewer who is not the client's employee.
- Written collection by Telegram bot is cheap but gives short accounts and is likely to miss older and rural customers. Use it for volume only with a follow-up call for detail, and state the gap.
- Record Tashkent and regional incidents with a place column and compare before merging. If the client's owner knows customers personally, remove names, addresses and dates that identify a buyer before sharing incidents.
- Published category schemes come mostly from Western service studies. Build categories from the local incidents first and treat any borrowed scheme as a hypothesis.
