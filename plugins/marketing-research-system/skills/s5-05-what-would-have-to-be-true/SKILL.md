# "What would have to be true" reasoning (S5-05)

This method replaces the argument "which option is right?" with the question "what would have to be true for this option to be a good choice?". The team lists the conditions each option depends on, finds the conditions it trusts least, and designs tests for those before choosing. Roger Martin developed the question in his consulting work and set it out with A.G. Lafley in Playing to Win (2013) and, with Jan Rivkin and Nicolaj Siggelkow, in the Harvard Business Review article "Bringing Science to the Art of Strategy" (2012). The same logic applies to research explanations: abductive analysis (Timmermans and Tavory, 2012) starts from a surprising finding and asks which explanation would make it expected, then checks that explanation against data. Evidence grade: B, an established professional standard. It is widely used and described in detail by its authors, and it rests on sound logic, but it has not been tested as a technique in controlled studies. It makes assumptions visible; the quality of the final choice still depends on honest tests.

Always answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label facts and interpretations, and return to the research-orchestrator skill when the work is done.

## When to use it / When not to use it

Use it when:
- There are two or more real options (positionings, segments, channels, concepts) and people defend different ones.
- A team is stuck arguing about what the data "show".
- A surprising research finding has several possible explanations.
- A chosen strategy needs a list of conditions to monitor later.

Do not use it when:
- There is only one option and no willingness to drop it. The method needs real alternatives.
- The decision is small and cheap to reverse. Just try it.
- Nobody will run the tests. A conditions list that is never tested gives false comfort.

## What you need before starting (inputs)

- The decision to be made and who makes it.
- Two or more options, or one surprising finding with candidate explanations.
- Existing research and data (to be used at the test stage, not before).
- The people who will carry out the choice. Their belief in the tests matters.

## Procedure

1. Frame the choice. Turn the issue into at least two options that cannot both be chosen. Ask the user: "If we could only do one, which are the candidates?" Write each option as a short description of what the company would do and why it would win. This moves the team from describing a problem to choosing.

2. Widen the options. Ask for more possibilities, including ones people find unlikely and the "keep doing what we do" option. Do not judge yet. Aim for three to five. Give each a neutral name so it is not tied to the person who proposed it.

3. Specify conditions for each option. For each one ask: "What would have to be true for this to be a very good choice?" Rules:
   - Nobody may say "that is not true" at this step. The only question is what would have to be true.
   - Agree the logic first. Martin's point is that teams can usually agree on the logic and fight over data, so keep data out until step 5.
   - Cover four areas so nothing is missed:
     a. Industry: which segments exist, how big and attractive they must be.
     b. Customers: what end customers and channel partners must value.
     c. Relative position: which capabilities and costs the company must have compared with competitors.
     d. Competitors: how rivals must react, or not react.
   - Write each condition as a statement that could be checked ("At least a third of small bakeries would pay 5 percent more for stable flour").
   - Ask the supporters of an option to list conditions first, then ask the doubters: "What else would have to be true for you to be comfortable?" Add those.
   - Separate must-have conditions from nice-to-have. Keep only must-haves.

4. Identify barriers. For each option ask: "Which of these conditions do we believe least?" Have each team member mark their doubts privately, then compare. The least-believed must-have conditions are the barriers. Record who doubts what and why; the doubters are valuable.

5. Design tests. For each barrier condition:
   - Decide what evidence would settle it: existing data, a few interviews, a survey question, a small pilot, a call to distributors.
   - Agree in advance what result counts as "true" and what counts as "not true". Write the threshold down before collecting data.
   - Make sure the test would convince the people who doubt the condition most. A test only the supporters believe does not create commitment.
   - Prefer the cheapest test that would change someone's mind.

6. Run the tests, weakest first. Start with the condition the team trusts least. If it fails, the option is dropped or redesigned and its other tests are not needed. Report each result as fact, and the reading of it as interpretation.

7. Choose. Compare options on their tested conditions. Choose the option whose must-have conditions hold. If none hold, return to step 2 with what was learned. Do not average or vote away a failed must-have condition.

8. Keep the conditions list. The chosen option's conditions become a monitoring list. Agree who checks them and how often. If a condition stops being true, the strategy needs review.

9. Close. Give the user the conditions table, test results, the choice and the monitoring list. Then return to the research-orchestrator skill.

Variant for research explanations (abductive use):
- Start from a surprising finding, one that existing ideas do not explain.
- List several candidate explanations, drawing on more than one theory or lens. Timmermans and Tavory advise knowing many theories and being committed to none.
- For each explanation ask: "If this were true, what else would we see in the data?" and "What would we not see?"
- Go back to the data, or collect a little more, and look for those signs and for negative cases.
- Keep the explanation that survives. State the ones rejected and why.

Decision rules:
- A condition that cannot be tested in any way is a belief. Mark it "untested belief" and say what it would cost if wrong.
- If every condition for a favoured option is rated "surely true", the team is not being honest; ask an outsider to mark doubts.

## Worked example

INVENTED example for illustration only. A juice brand sells mainly in Tashkent supermarkets. The team disagrees about growth.

Options: A. "Regional shops": put a small, cheaper pack into small shops in regional towns. B. "HoReCa": sell 1-litre packs to cafes and restaurants in Tashkent. C. Continue as now.

Conditions for option A (extract):

| Area | What would have to be true | Team belief | Barrier? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Industry | Small regional shops sell enough packaged juice to justify a route | medium | no |
| Customers | Shop owners will give shelf space to a brand customers do not yet ask for | low | yes |
| Customers | Buyers accept a 200 ml pack at the planned price | medium | no |
| Relative position | A distributor will visit weekly and offer credit | low | yes |
| Competitors | The leading local brand will not cut price in response within six months | medium | no |

Tests agreed in advance:
- Shelf space: offer the pack to 30 shops in two towns through one distributor. "True" if at least 15 accept and 10 reorder within a month.
- Distributor: talk to three regional distributors. "True" if at least one agrees to weekly visits on the planned margin.

Result (invented): 2 of 3 distributors agreed (fact). 19 shops accepted, 6 reordered (fact). Reading (interpretation): the shelf-space condition holds for entry but not for reorder; option A needs redesign (for example demand support at the counter) before it can be chosen. Option B is tested next.

## Common mistakes

- Arguing about whether a condition is true while still listing conditions.
- Applying the question only to the option the boss dislikes, or only to the favourite.
- Writing vague conditions that cannot be checked ("customers will like it").
- Setting the pass mark after seeing the data.
- Letting supporters design tests that doubters do not accept.
- Starting with the easiest test instead of the weakest condition.

## Quality checklist

- Were at least two mutually exclusive options framed, including the status quo?
- Does every option have conditions in all four areas (industry, customers, relative position, competitors)?
- Is each condition a checkable statement?
- Were barrier conditions chosen from the team's own doubts, with doubters named?
- Was the pass threshold for each test written before data collection?
- Were the least-believed conditions tested first?
- Is the choice explained by test results, with facts and interpretations separated?
- Is there a monitoring list with an owner?

## Notes for Uzbekistan

- In mixed-seniority meetings people may not voice doubt about a senior person's option. Collect belief ratings privately (paper or a private message) and show them without names if needed.
- Run the session in the team's working language; write conditions so they read the same in Uzbek and Russian, with numbers where possible.
- Write customer and channel conditions separately for Tashkent and for regions when both are in scope; a condition can hold in one and fail in the other.
- When testing conditions through interviews, expect polite agreement. Prefer tests based on behaviour (orders, reorders, deposits) over stated intention.
