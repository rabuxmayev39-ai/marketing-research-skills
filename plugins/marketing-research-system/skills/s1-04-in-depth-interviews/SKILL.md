# In-depth interviews (S1-04)

An in-depth interview is a one-to-one research conversation, usually semi-structured, in which the interviewer follows a topic guide but lets the respondent tell their own story in their own words. The standard text is Steinar Kvale and Svend Brinkmann, InterViews: Learning the Craft of Qualitative Research Interviewing (Sage). Kvale's approach has two parts that this skill uses. First, an interview study has seven stages: thematising the topic and purpose, designing the whole study, interviewing, transcribing, analysing, verifying (validation and generalisation), and reporting. Second, interviewing is a craft: Kvale writes that it is "learned through practising interviewing", ideally alongside experienced interviewers. In marketing research the method is used for motives, purchase and switching stories, sensitive or private topics, and business buyers who cannot be gathered in a group.

Evidence grade: B. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. In-depth interviewing is a long-established standard across social and market research. It gives depth and explanation, not measurement. Put weight on what respondents describe concretely from their own experience; treat any statement about how common something is as a hypothesis for a survey. Quality depends on the interviewer more than on the guide.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (what a respondent said, with ID and quote in the original language) versus INTERPRETATION (what you think it means). The assistant helps plan, write the guide, train and debrief; a person conducts the interview. When the plan or the fieldwork summary is delivered, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- The question is why or how: motives, the story of a decision, the meaning of a product in someone's life or work.
- The topic is private, sensitive or tied to status (money, health, family decisions), so a group would inhibit people.
- Respondents are B2B buyers, owners or experts: busy, few, and unwilling to speak in front of competitors.
- Each person's full sequence of events matters and would be cut short in a group.

Do not use it when:
- The client needs to know how many or what share. Use S1-08.
- What matters is what people actually do, and they cannot report it reliably. Observe (S1-06) or use behavioural data (S1-23).
- The aim is to hear how people talk to each other and what a group treats as normal. Use S1-05.
- No trained interviewer is available and there is no time to practise. A weak interviewer produces short, agreeable answers that look like data.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. The decision, research questions and hypotheses written down (S2-01, S2-11).
2. A definition of who to interview and the contrasts that matter, with sample logic from S2-04, S2-05 and S2-06.
3. An interviewer who has practised, and a second person for notes where possible.
4. Recruitment route, incentive, and a quiet place or call set-up for 45 to 90 minutes (the length range given in this agency's method index; a working rule, not a sourced threshold).
5. Consent wording, recording tool, and a plan for storing recordings and transcripts (S2-10).

## Procedure
1. Check fit. Ask the user: "Do you need reasons and stories, or numbers?" and "Could these people speak freely in front of others?" Reasons plus low freedom in a group means interviews. If numbers are needed, redirect to S1-08; if both, plan S1-02.
2. Stage 1, thematise. Write the purpose of the study and what is already known. Turn each research question into two to four topics. Research questions are not asked directly; they are answered through topics.
3. Stage 2, design. Plan all seven stages before the first interview: who and how many (hand to S2-04 or S2-05), recruitment, guide, recording, transcription, analysis method (S4-01 or S4-05), how findings will be checked, and report format. Kvale stresses that many practical choices carry ethical consequences, so settle consent, confidentiality and what happens to recordings here.
4. Write the guide. An opened university guide describes a semi-structured schedule as: topics, the questions for each topic, issues within each, and prompts and probes. Rules for wording: simple vocabulary; one question at a time; no double-barrelled or hypothetical questions; no assumptions about what the person knows; open questions, not yes or no. Working rule: open with an easy question about the person's own recent experience, ask for specific past events (S3-04), and keep direct questions about the research topic for late in the conversation so they do not steer earlier answers.
5. Plan question types. Question types as listed in an opened library guide (the list follows Kvale's tradition, though the page does not name him): follow-up (ask to say more), probing (dig into an answer), specifying (ask for concrete detail of what was done or felt), direct, indirect (ask what others think, then the person's own view), structuring (signal a move to a new topic), interpreting (check your understanding). Working rule: also plan silence, a pause that lets the person continue. Put two or three ready probes under each topic.
6. Prepare the interviewer. The same guide lists the qualities of a good interviewer: knows the topic, structures the conversation, asks clear short questions, is gentle with pauses, listens sensitively, stays open to new directions, steers toward what must be learned, questions inconsistencies, remembers earlier answers, interprets without imposing, and handles confidentiality ethically. Run one or two practice interviews and review the recordings together.
7. Stage 3, interview. Start the recording by stating date, place and who is present; confirm consent. Then listen. Do not give opinions, signal approval or disapproval, answer for the respondent, or summarise too early. Check interpretations during the conversation ("Do I understand correctly that...").
8. Judge quality while fieldwork runs. Kvale's criteria, as cited in the university guide: answers are rich, specific and relevant; questions are short and answers long; the interviewer follows up and clarifies meanings; interpretations are verified during the interview. Working rule: after the first two or three interviews, review transcripts against these criteria and revise the guide or coach the interviewer before continuing.
9. Record a snapshot within an hour of each interview (S3-11): what was new, what contradicted expectations, what to ask next time.
10. Stage 4, transcribe verbatim in the original language with respondent IDs. Recording corrects memory and lets others check the analysis; it can also make some respondents guarded, so note where that seemed to happen.
11. Stages 5 to 7. Analyse (S4-01, S4-05), verify by looking for negative cases and checking claims against full transcripts (S4-07), and report in words, with quotes, without percentages. State who was not interviewed. Return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. Input: a Tashkent furniture maker wants to know how hotel and restaurant owners choose a furniture supplier. Buyers are few, busy and competitors of each other, so interviews fit; a group does not.

```
PLAN
Purpose: understand the last real supplier choice, start to finish.
Sample: 10 buyers - 5 hotels, 5 restaurants; within each, 3 Tashkent + 2 regions
  = 6 Tashkent + 4 regions. Review after 6; add interviews if new reasons still appear.
Length: 60 minutes each = 10 hours of recording.
GUIDE (extract)
Topic 1 Last purchase (20 min)
  Q: "Oxirgi marta mebel buyurtma qilganingizni boshidan aytib bering."
  Probes: Who else was involved? What happened next? (specifying, follow-up)
Topic 2 Suppliers considered (15 min)
  Q: "Кого ещё вы рассматривали и почему отказались?"
  Probe: silence, then "Give me an example." Indirect: "How do other owners choose?"
Topic 3 After delivery (15 min) | Close (10 min): "What did I not ask?"
AFTER INTERVIEW 3 - quality review
Answers average two lines; interviewer asked 4 yes/no questions.          FACT
Guide changed: yes/no questions rewritten as "tell me about the time...". INTERP
```
Arithmetic check: 5 + 5 = 10 buyers; (3 + 3) Tashkent + (2 + 2) regions = 6 + 4 = 10; 10 x 60 minutes = 600 minutes = 10 hours; guide time 20 + 15 + 15 + 10 = 60 minutes.

## Common mistakes
- Reporting "7 of 10 said" as if it were a market share.
- Asking the research question directly ("Why do you choose a supplier?") and getting a tidy rationalisation.
- Asking about the future or the typical case when a specific past event was available.
- A guide with 30 questions read out in order, so the interview becomes a spoken questionnaire.
- Talking more than the respondent, filling silences, or showing approval.
- Leading questions used without noticing; Kvale treats their validity as a real issue to decide on, not to ignore.
- No recording, late notes, or transcripts translated before analysis so the original wording is lost.

## Quality checklist
- Does the question need reasons or stories, not counts?
- Were all seven stages planned before fieldwork, including analysis and reporting?
- Does the guide use topics, open questions about past events, and prepared probes?
- Did the interviewer practise and get feedback on a recording?
- In the transcripts, are questions short and answers long and specific?
- Were interpretations checked with respondents during the interviews?
- Is every finding traceable to respondent IDs and quotes in the original language?
- Does the report avoid percentages and state who was not covered?

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Let the respondent choose Uzbek or Russian, and expect switching between them. The interviewer should be fluent in both. Transcribe and quote in the original language; translate only for the final report.
- Polite or agreeing answers are likely, especially toward a guest or someone linked to the client. Ask for concrete past events, use indirect questions, and note general praise without an example as "approval, no example".
- Do not send the client's own staff to interview their customers; respondents may avoid criticism. In regions, allow more time for introductions and hospitality before the recorded part, and agree in advance who may be present; a third person in the room changes answers on private topics.
- Record how each respondent was reached; recruiting through referrals narrows the sample.
- Kvale's guidance was developed in Western research settings. The craft transfers; expectations about how openly people speak to a stranger are hypotheses here.
