# Thematic analysis, six phases (S4-01)

Thematic analysis (TA) is a method for identifying, analysing and reporting patterns of meaning (themes) across a qualitative dataset. Virginia Braun and Victoria Clarke set out its six-phase form in 2006 (Qualitative Research in Psychology) and later renamed their version "reflexive TA" to stress that the analyst builds themes actively; themes are not lying in the data waiting to be found. It is used to turn interviews, group discussions and open survey answers into a small number of well-supported themes that answer a research question.

Evidence grade: A. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. The procedure is published in a peer-reviewed journal, is very widely used, and its authors have published follow-up papers on how it is misapplied. The grade covers the procedure. It does not make any single set of themes true: themes remain the analyst's interpretation and carry weight only as far as the data extracts and the checks in phase 4 support them.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (what a respondent said, with ID) versus INTERPRETATION (codes, themes, conclusions). When the theme report is done, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- The data are interviews, focus groups, open survey answers or documents, and the question is about what people experience, think, want or do.
- The team needs patterns across the whole dataset, not a portrait of one case.
- No theory has to be built and no fixed codebook has to be applied; flexibility is wanted.

Do not use it, or use something else, when:
- The aim is an explanatory theory of a process (S4-02), or to explain a surprising finding (S4-03).
- Several people must code against a fixed frame and compare cases side by side (S4-05), or categories must be counted (S4-21).
- The dataset is very small. The authors' FAQ treats five interviews as generally too few and suggests 6-10 for a small project, 10-20 for a medium one, 30 or more for a large one. These are their rough guides, not rules.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. The research question and the decision it feeds.
2. The full dataset: transcripts or verbatim answers in the original language, with respondent IDs and basic profile (segment, city, role).
3. How the data were collected (guide, interviewer, channel) and any field notes (S3-11 snapshots help).
4. Four design choices, written down before coding (Braun and Clarke ask that these be stated, not left implicit): inductive (codes grounded in the data) or deductive (an existing theory or brief used as a lens; their later work notes that pure induction is not possible); semantic (what was said) or latent (assumptions underneath it); rich description of the whole dataset or a detailed account of one aspect; talk read as a report of reality or as something shaped by the situation.

## Procedure
1. Confirm the inputs. Ask the user: "What is the question, who are the respondents, and do we read answers at face value or look underneath them?" Record the four design choices. If the user has no preference, default to mostly inductive, semantic first and latent second, and say so (working rule).
2. Phase 1, familiarisation. Read every data item fully at least twice before coding. Check transcripts against recordings where possible. Produce a short note per item: what stands out, what is odd, what the respondent seemed to take for granted. Do not skip this phase to save time; the authors advise against rushing it.
3. Phase 2, coding. Go through the entire dataset systematically, giving each item equal attention. Attach a short code to every extract relevant to the question. Rules from the 2006 paper: code inclusively (keep a little surrounding context), allow one extract several codes, and keep extracts that contradict the main story. Output: a code list with every extract collated under its code, each with respondent ID.
4. Phase 3, generating initial themes. Sort codes into candidate themes and sub-themes. Keep a "miscellaneous" group for codes that fit nowhere yet. For each candidate, write its central organising concept in one sentence. Decision rule: if the sentence only names a topic ("Price", "Experiences of delivery"), it is a topic summary, not a theme. A theme states a shared meaning ("Buyers treat a low price as a warning sign").
5. Phase 4, reviewing themes. Two checks from the source. Level 1: reread all extracts under each theme; if they do not form a coherent pattern, rework the theme, move extracts, or drop it. Level 2: reread the whole dataset; do the themes reflect it, and was anything missed? Code what was missed. Stop when further changes add nothing substantial.
6. Phase 5, defining and naming. For each theme write what it covers, what it does not cover, and how it relates to the others. Test from the source: can the theme be described in a couple of sentences? If not, refine. Give a concise, informative name; one-word names are a warning sign.
7. Phase 6, writing up. For each theme give the analytic claim, then extracts as evidence, then what it means for the research question. Extracts illustrate; the narrative must say something beyond paraphrase. Braun and Clarke suggest roughly two to six themes for one report; more than six often signals thin analysis.
8. Handling prevalence. The source sets no cut-off: a theme is judged by how much it matters to the question, not by a percentage. Report how many respondents an extract set comes from, apply one counting rule consistently, and never present counts as if the sample were representative.
9. Decide how to treat a second coder. Reflexive TA does not use inter-coder agreement scores; its authors see high agreement as a sign of similar training, not of accuracy. A second reader is still useful to challenge assumptions. If the team needs a shared codebook and agreement checks, say openly that this is a codebook approach and hand over to S4-05.
10. Report the method honestly: which choices were made, who coded, what was changed in phase 4. Say "we developed these themes", not "themes emerged". Then return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. A Tashkent furniture maker interviewed 12 cafe and restaurant owners about how they choose seating suppliers. Choices: inductive, semantic first.

Input (three of many coded extracts):
```
R3: "Bir marta arzon oldim, yarim yilda hammasi qimirlab qoldi."        FACT
R7: "Мне важно, чтобы через год приехали и подтянули, без разговоров."  FACT
R9: "Rasmda chiroyli, lekin o'tirib ko'rmasam olmayman."                FACT
```
Output:
```
Codes: burned by cheap purchase (R3, R5, R11) | after-sale repair expected
(R7, R2, R10) | must sit before buying (R9, R1, R4, R12) | photo distrust (R9, R6)
Candidate "Quality" -> rejected in phase 3: topic summary                INTERP
Theme 1: "Proof beats promise" - owners trust what they can test or what
  a supplier has already repaired, not catalogues. 8 of 12 respondents.  INTERP
Theme 2: "The first cheap mistake sets the budget" - one failed purchase
  shifts owners from price to durability. 3 of 12; thin, flagged.        INTERP
Phase 4 change: R6 moved out of Theme 1 (talked about delivery photos).
Contradicting case kept: R8 buys from catalogue only, replaces yearly.   FACT
```

## Common mistakes
- Using the interview questions as themes.
- Presenting topic summaries ("Benefits", "Barriers") as themes.
- Stringing quotes together or paraphrasing them with no analytic claim.
- Building a theme from a few vivid quotes (the source calls this anecdotalism).
- Themes that overlap, are not coherent, or make claims the extracts do not support; hiding contradicting extracts.
- Writing that themes "emerged", as if the analyst made no choices.
- Claiming reflexive TA while using a fixed codebook and agreement scores, or using frequency as the measure of importance.

## Quality checklist
Adapted from the authors' 15-point checklist.
- Was every data item read in full and given equal attention in coding?
- Are all relevant extracts collated under each theme, with respondent IDs?
- Does each theme have a central organising concept stated in one sentence?
- Were themes checked against the coded extracts and against the whole dataset?
- Does the write-up interpret, not just describe or paraphrase?
- Are contradicting cases reported?
- Are the design choices (inductive or deductive, semantic or latent) stated, and does the report match what was done?
- Are there about two to six themes, each distinct from the others?

## Works with
- S1-04 in-depth interviews and S1-07 JTBD switch interviews: produce the data. S3-11 interview snapshot: feeds phase 1.
- S4-04 coding cycles: a wider choice of code types for phase 2.
- S4-05 matrix displays: use instead when a team must code to a shared frame, or after TA to compare themes by segment.
- S4-03 abductive analysis: take a theme that contradicts expectations there.
- S4-07 negative case analysis: for contradicting cases found in phase 4.
- S2-04 saturation: Braun and Clarke question saturation as a rationale for reflexive TA; if it is claimed, define it and say how it was judged.
- S5-02 insight ladder and S5-03 insight tests: turn themes into insights. S5-14 member checking: optional check with respondents.

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Code in the language of the transcript. Keep extracts in the original Uzbek or Russian (and the original script); translate only for the client report, with the original beside it. If a respondent switches language mid-answer, keep the switch.
- Polite or agreeing answers can produce a false theme of satisfaction. In phase 2 give general approval with no example a separate code, and do not count it as support for a theme.
- Report Tashkent and regional respondents separately in the "spread" line before merging them into one theme.
- Open answers from a Telegram-bot survey come from people who use Telegram and chose to reply; older and rural people are likely to be missing. State this as a limit.
- Published examples of TA come mostly from Western samples. Themes from that literature are hypotheses here, not expectations.
