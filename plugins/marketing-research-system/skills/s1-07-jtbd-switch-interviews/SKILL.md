# JTBD switch interviews (S1-07)
A switch interview is a one-to-one conversation with a person who recently bought, changed supplier or cancelled. The interviewer rebuilds the story in time order: the first thought that something had to change, passive looking, active looking, deciding, buying and first use. The format comes from the Jobs to Be Done practice of Bob Moesta and Chris Spiek and is described in Moesta's Demand-Side Sales 101 (2020); the underlying idea that customers "hire" a product to make progress in a particular circumstance was set out by Christensen, Hall, Dillon and Duncan (2016) in Harvard Business Review. Evidence grade: C, a practitioner tool with little formal testing. The interview format rests on practitioner experience and published cases, not on controlled studies. Use what it produces as well-grounded hypotheses about triggers, barriers and competitors, and size or test anything important with another method.

Working rules for the assistant:
- Answer in the language the user writes in (Uzbek, Russian or English).
- Label every statement as FACT (what the respondent said happened, with a quote) or INTERPRETATION (your reading).
- When the work is finished, return to the "research-orchestrator" skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- The decision depends on why people start looking, what almost stops them, and what they compare the product with (including doing nothing).
- The team can reach people who made the choice recently. The practitioner guidance opened for this skill says within about 30 to 90 days, because recall fades after that.
- The purchase involved some thought: a supplier change, a durable, a service contract, a first purchase in a category.
Do not use it when:
- You need numbers (how many buyers, what share). Use S1-08.
- There are no recent buyers or switchers to recruit. Use S1-04 on current behaviour, or S1-06.
- The purchase is habitual and cheap with no story to tell; S1-06 or S1-17 fit better.
- The question is which feature or price wins; use S1-10, S1-11 or S1-12.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
- The decision this research serves and the purchase event to study (bought ours, bought a competitor's, left us, almost bought).
- A source of recent buyers with dates: CRM or order list, delivery records, shop staff referrals, a Telegram screener.
- Two people per interview (lead and note-taker), a recorder, consent wording (S2-10) and an incentive.
- About one hour per interview plus time for a debrief straight after.

## Procedure
1. Check fit. Ask the user: what will be decided, did a real switch happen, and can we reach people who did it in the last 90 days? If any answer is no, say so and propose another method from the list above. Record the answers.
2. Define the switch and write the screener. State the event precisely ("ordered flour from a new mill since 1 September"). Screen for: date of the event; that this person noticed the problem first or drove the choice, not only the one who paid; what they used before. In B2B a full story may need two or three people from one firm.
3. Build the sample. Moesta's talk reports that patterns usually appear after about 7 to 12 interviews; plan 10 and review with S2-04 or S2-05. Mix people who switched to you, to a competitor, and away from you. Add one or two who looked and did not buy; do not recruit only satisfied "best" customers. Pay for their time.
4. Prepare a timeline sheet, not a questionnaire. Columns: first thought, passive looking, active looking, deciding, buying, first use. Under each, leave room for date, place, who was there, what happened, and what was considered. Mark the events that moved the person from one stage to the next.
5. Open the interview. Say there are no right or wrong answers, that you are reconstructing what happened like a documentary maker, and that you will interrupt because you are curious. Ask permission to record.
6. Start at the purchase and walk back to the first thought. Ask: "When did you buy? Where were you? Who was with you?" Then: "When did you first think the old way was not good enough? What happened that day?" Keep asking "Why then and not a month earlier?"
7. Walk forward stage by stage. Passive looking: what did they begin to notice without searching? Active looking: what did they do, whom did they ask, what did they compare? Deciding: what did they give up, what nearly stopped them, who had to agree? Buying and first use: what surprised them?
8. Ask for events, not opinions. When the answer is general ("quality matters"), ask for the last concrete moment it mattered. Techniques described in the sources: ask the person to explain what seems obvious; restate the sequence slightly wrong so they correct you; write down their exact phrases; treat a contradiction as a detail to refine, not an error.
9. Do not ask about the forces directly. Pushes, pulls, anxieties and habits should surface from the events. Note everything they considered or used instead, including cheap substitutes and doing nothing; these are the real competitors.
10. Close. Read the timeline back in two minutes and ask what is wrong or missing. Thank and pay.
11. Debrief straight after with the note-taker. Fill the timeline, mark gaps, list verbatim quotes in the original language, and write an interview snapshot (S3-11). After the first interview, adjust the screener and prompts before running the rest.
12. Stop rule (working rule of this skill): stop when two interviews in a row add no new trigger, anxiety or competitor; if stories still differ widely at 12, the event was defined too broadly, so split it.
13. Hand over. Give the timelines and quotes to S4-09 for the analysis of forces. Do not classify forces here beyond rough notes. Report with the output template and return to "research-orchestrator".

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE for illustration. No real data.
Input: a Tashkent furniture maker wants to know why cafe owners replace seating. Screener: owner or manager who ordered sofas or chairs for a working cafe in the last 60 days and took part in choosing. Ten interviews planned: 4 who bought from the maker, 4 who bought elsewhere, 2 who asked for a quote and postponed.
Output for interview 3 (timeline, shortened):

| Stage | When | What happened (FACT, from the respondent) |
|---|---|---|
| First thought | early June | A regular guest photographed a torn seat and posted it; the owner saw the post that evening. |
| Passive looking | June | Noticed seating in other cafes; saved two Instagram posts; did nothing. |
| Event | 2 July | Landlord confirmed the lease for three more years. |
| Active looking | July | Asked two cafe owners; visited one showroom; requested three quotes by Telegram. |
| Deciding | late July | Dropped the cheapest quote after hearing of a 2-month delay; worried about closing the hall during replacement. |
| Buying | 1 August | Paid 50% deposit after the maker agreed to deliver on a Monday, the cafe's day off. |
| First use | mid August | Staff found the new covers easy to wipe; two chairs wobbled and were fixed in a day. |

Quote kept in the original language: "Zal yopilib qolsa, mijoz ketadi." FACT: considered alternatives were re-upholstering the old sofas, a cheaper workshop, and waiting until winter. INTERPRETATION (to pass to S4-09): the lease renewal, not the torn seat, released the money; delivery without closing the hall may matter more than price.

## Common mistakes
- Interviewing loyal long-term customers who cannot remember the switch, or prospects who have not decided anything.
- Asking "why did you choose us?" and collecting opinions instead of dated events.
- Reading a list of questions; skipping the debrief; letting one person both lead and take notes.
- Naming only same-category brands as competitors and ignoring repair, borrowing, or doing nothing.
- Counting stories ("6 of 10 said") and presenting the counts as market shares.

## Quality checklist
- Did every respondent complete the switch (or a clear near-switch) within the agreed window? (yes/no)
- Is each stage of each timeline filled with a date or an explained gap? (yes/no)
- Is the first thought tied to a specific event? (yes/no)
- Are alternatives outside the category recorded? (yes/no)
- Are at least some non-buyers or leavers in the sample? (yes/no)
- Are quotes kept verbatim in the original language? (yes/no)
- Was a debrief written on the day of each interview? (yes/no)
- Are FACT and INTERPRETATION separated in the report? (yes/no)

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Run the interview in the respondent's language (Uzbek or Russian) and keep quotes in that language; translate only in the analysis notes.
- Polite or agreeing answer habits can produce a smooth, flattering story. Ask for dates, places and who else was present, and ask what nearly went wrong.
- Ask who else had a say in the decision (family, business partners) and consider interviewing them too.
- Tashkent and regional buyers may have different alternatives available; do not pool them without checking.
- Recruiting through Telegram misses older and rural buyers; add referrals from shops or sales staff.
- The method was developed and described with Western, mostly US cases. Treat the stage model as a working frame, not a tested law, here.
