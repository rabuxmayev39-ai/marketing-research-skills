# Mystery shopping (S1-13)

Mystery shopping is a form of concealed participant observation. Trained or briefed people act as customers or prospective customers, carry out a set task (a visit, a call, a message, sometimes a purchase) and record what happened against fixed criteria straight afterwards. It has no single inventor; it grew up as a commercial practice, and Alan Wilson's studies (1998, 2001) are the standard academic descriptions of how service firms use it. Professional bodies (ESOMAR, the UK Market Research Society) publish rules for it because the people observed do not know they are part of a study. It is used to check whether service standards are delivered, to find training needs and to compare the sales process with competitors.

Evidence grade: B. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. This is a long-established professional method with published ethics guidelines, but the samples are small and formal reliability testing is thin. Trust it as a factual record of what happened in the visits made. Do not treat it as a measure of how customers feel, or as a statistically representative picture of all service encounters.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (what the shopper observed and recorded: times, words, actions) versus INTERPRETATION (why it happened, what it means for sales). When the plan or the report is delivered, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- The decision depends on what staff or dealers actually do in the sales process, not on what managers say they do.
- There is a written service standard, script or sales procedure to check against, or the team can write one.
- The client wants to compare its own sales process with competitors' on observable steps (greeting, needs questions, price given, follow-up).
- The encounters are routine, short and started by the customer, so a shopper can play them credibly.

Do not use it when:
- The question is about customer satisfaction, feelings or reasons for choice. Wilson (1998) and later writers stress that the method reviews the process, not the outcome. Use S1-08, S1-04 or S1-16.
- The client wants to punish or dismiss individual staff. ESOMAR says findings must not be used to discipline or disadvantage the individuals observed.
- A credible scenario needs expert knowledge or a long, costly engagement from the other side (for example a full custom design from a competitor). That wastes their resources and breaks the guidelines.
- A handful of visits would be reported as "the market". The sample cannot carry that.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. The management decision and the research question (from S0-02 and S2-01).
2. The service standard or sales script to be checked, or a description of the intended sales process.
3. A list of outlets: own branches, dealers or agents, competitors. For each, whether staff have been told that mystery shopping may happen.
4. Shopper profiles that match real customers (age, language, apparent budget), and people available to act as shoppers.
5. Budget for purchases or deposits, and a rule for cancelling bookings. Agreement on reporting level (network, branch, or individual) and on how results will be used.

## Procedure
1. Check fit. Ask the user: "Which decision will change depending on what we see? Is the question about what staff do, or about what customers think?" If it is about customer views, return to the research-orchestrator skill and propose S1-08 or S1-04. If it is about natural behaviour of real customers in the shop, consider S1-06.
2. Classify each outlet, because the rules differ (ESOMAR 2005; MRS 2011): Own organisation: staff, and any staff body, must be told in advance that mystery shopping may take place, its aims, whether individuals will be identified and whether anything is recorded. Exact dates need not be given. Dealers and agents: treat as own organisation only if they have a comparable direct relationship and have been told. Otherwise treat as competitors. Competitors: staff cannot be told, so no individual may be identified, no audio, photo or video recording of employees, results only in aggregate, time taken no longer than a normal customer enquiry, follow-up work for them kept minimal, and any booking cancelled quickly.
3. Decide whether it is research or a project. ESOMAR separates mystery shopping research (individuals anonymous, data confidential, research use only) from a mystery shopping project (data used for training, bonuses or sales reinforcement). The second must not be described as research. State which one this is in the plan.
4. Write the scenarios. Each should be a typical, routine enquiry that needs more than a yes or no answer, that a normal employee could answer, and that fits the shopper's profile. Bradbury and Milford used at least two scenarios per service area and rotated shoppers so that staff could not spot them. Working rule: write one page per scenario with the cover story, the questions to ask, the questions not to ask, and the exit line.
5. Build the checklist. Prefer observable items: was a greeting given, how long before contact, which questions were asked, was a price stated, was a contact taken, was a follow-up call made. Keep subjective items (friendliness, interest) few and put them on a graded scale; Bradbury and Milford found that all-or-nothing scoring fits such items badly. Keep the form short: long forms overload memory and raise the risk that the shopper is exposed. Add an open field for the staff member's words, in the original language.
6. Plan the sample. There is no sourced minimum number of visits. Working rules: at least two visits per outlet by different shoppers at different times or days; spread visits across the fieldwork period (Bradbury and Milford split visits across sub-periods so they did not all fall in a busy or quiet spell); report counts, not percentages, when there are fewer than about 30 visits in a group.
7. Select and brief shoppers. They should match real customers, follow the brief, stay neutral and not provoke staff. Train them on the scenario, the checklist, memory and observation, and how to avoid detection. Run one practice visit with feedback. Vary shoppers: age, gender and dress can change how staff respond.
8. Protect shoppers. No task that is illegal or risky; pay back purchases promptly; tell them their identity can leak through phone numbers or bank cards.
9. Run fieldwork. The shopper fills the form in private immediately after the encounter. Working rule: within one hour, and never from memory the next day. Collect receipts, price lists and business cards as evidence. For competitor visits, do not record staff.
10. Check quality. Compare the two shoppers' forms for the same outlet; where they differ on an observable item, treat it as variation between visits or as a recording error, and ask both shoppers before deciding. Drop visits where the shopper was recognised.
11. Analyse. For each checklist item, count the visits in which the behaviour occurred. Compare own network with competitors only on identical items and scenarios. Read the open fields for sales arguments and objections handled. Label all explanations as INTERPRETATION.
12. Report in aggregate, with limits. Then return to the research-orchestrator skill. If the client needs customer views to sit beside the process data, propose S1-08 or S1-16 as a second source (S2-07).

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. A Tashkent furniture maker wants to know why showroom visits do not turn into orders. Input: the company's 6-step sales standard; 5 own-dealer showrooms whose staff were told in the dealer agreement that mystery shopping may happen; 2 shoppers; 2 visits per showroom, so 5 x 2 = 10 visits; scenario "sofa for a new flat, budget not fixed". Output (extract):

```
Checklist item                               | Visits where done (of 10)
Greeted within 2 minutes                     | 8                      FACT
Asked about room size or who will use it     | 3                      FACT
Explained frame material                     | 4                      FACT
Stated a price without being asked twice     | 10                     FACT
Offered delivery and assembly terms          | 6                      FACT
Took a phone number or Telegram contact      | 2                      FACT
Follow-up message within 3 days              | 1                      FACT
Reading: staff give prices but rarely ask about needs (3 of 10) and
  almost never take a contact (2 of 10), so the lost orders may come
  from no follow-up rather than from price.                   INTERP
Limits: 10 visits, 2 shoppers, one scenario, weekday afternoons only.
Next: customer side unknown - propose S1-07 with recent buyers.
```

## Common mistakes
- Using the method to answer "are customers satisfied?" It records process only. Shopping own staff without telling them in advance that it may happen, or using results for discipline.
- Recording or naming competitor staff, or taking up far more of their time than a real enquiry would.
- Long checklists full of opinion items scored yes or no. One shopper, one visit per outlet, reported as that outlet's standard.
- Scenarios that no real customer would bring, so staff notice the shopper.
- Filling in the form hours later from memory.
- Reporting "70 percent of showrooms" from seven of ten visits as if it were a market figure.

## Quality checklist
- Is the question about what staff do, not about how customers feel?
- Is every outlet classified (own, informed agent, competitor) and treated under the matching rules?
- Have own staff been told in advance that mystery shopping may take place?
- Are most checklist items observable facts, with few graded opinion items?
- Did at least two different shoppers visit each outlet at different times (working rule)?
- Were forms completed straight after each encounter?
- Are results reported in aggregate, with no individual identifiable?
- Does the report state the number of visits and say that it measures process, not customer feelings?

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Run scenarios in the language a real customer of that outlet would use, Uzbek or Russian, and record staff wording in the original language. Plan both languages if the client serves both.
- Much selling may continue after the visit by phone or Telegram. Include the follow-up channel in the checklist and agree in advance how long the shopper keeps the conversation open before closing it politely.
- Tashkent and regional outlets should be reported separately; do not average them. In small towns and small shops there may be one seller, so a "branch" result identifies a person. Under ESOMAR rules such a study cannot be called research unless the result is merged into a larger group.
- Shoppers who are known locally will be recognised. Recruit shoppers from outside the neighbourhood or city.
- Polite or agreeing habits apply to shoppers too: a shopper may soften a negative record about a pleasant seller. Use observable items and compare two shoppers. Check local law on recording conversations before any recording, even of own staff. The guidelines cited are European and British; treat them as the working standard and say so.
