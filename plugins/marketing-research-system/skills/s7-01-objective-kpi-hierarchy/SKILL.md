# Objective and KPI hierarchy (S7-01)

An objective and KPI hierarchy is a short table that sets what a marketing effort must achieve at four levels and keeps the levels apart: business results (sales, market share, profit, price achieved), buyer behaviour (how many people buy, how often, how many try or enquire), brand measures (what people know and remember about the brand, such as awareness and associations) and activity measures (what the team did and delivered, such as reach, clicks and cost). A KPI, key performance indicator, is simply the measure chosen to judge success. The method rests on Les Binet and Peter Field's Marketing in the Era of Accountability (IPA, 2007), an analysis of 880 case studies from the IPA Effectiveness Awards. They separate hard business objectives, hard behavioural objectives and softer "intermediate" objectives such as awareness and image. Adding activity as a fourth, lowest level is this skill's working rule, so that delivery counts are never mistaken for results.

Evidence grade: A. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. The grade in the agency's database is A because the rule comes from a large data analysis and not from opinion; honestly stated, the source is an industry report published by the IPA, not a peer-reviewed journal article. As reported in the press summary opened for this skill, the analysis found that intermediate measures do not correlate reliably with business performance, that more than half of the highly effective campaigns showed little or no improvement in brand image, and that campaigns with profit as the first objective tended to do better although only 7% set it. Known limit: short-term measures used alone push a team towards activation only, because they move fast and are easy to link to activity. Published criticism: the cases are award entries, so they over-represent successful, large UK campaigns, and the results are reported by the entrants themselves; the marketing columnist Mark Ritson accepts both points and still judges the work nearer the truth than the alternatives. Put high weight on the direction (judge by business and behaviour, use several measures) and low weight on the exact percentages outside the UK.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (baselines taken from records or surveys) versus INTERPRETATION (targets, and the assumed links between levels). Never report an activity measure as a result. When the table is agreed and the measurement method is booked, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- A strategy or communication plan is being written and success has not yet been defined.
- A client's goals are a mix of sales, followers, awareness and leads with no order between them.
- A report is due and the team is tempted to show reach and clicks as the outcome.

Do not use it when:
- The problem itself is not diagnosed. Objectives written before the diagnosis are wishes; go to S0-01 and S0-05.
- The campaign has already ended and no baseline exists. Say that the hierarchy cannot be rebuilt honestly afterwards; record the lesson in S7-04.
- The client wants one number only. Explain why one number is not enough (step 6) before agreeing.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. The business goal in the client's words, and sales records for at least the last year (two if the category is seasonal).
2. The diagnosis of where growth should come from: more buyers, more purchases per buyer, or more value per purchase (S0-05).
3. Any baseline on awareness and associations (S1-22, S4-19), or a plan to collect one before launch.
4. The planned activity and budget (S9-07), and the list of data sources the client really has: sales system, CRM, call log, bot statistics.
5. The person on the client side who will own each number.

## Procedure
1. Write the business objective first: one measure, in units or money, with baseline, target and date. Binet and Field's material names profit, market share and price sensitivity (how strongly sales fall when the price rises) as the hard business measures. Ask the client: "Which number in your accounts must change, from what to what, by when?" If profit cannot be shared, use sales or share and say so.
2. Translate it into behaviour. Use the growth sum from S0-05 (buyers x purchases per buyer x value per purchase) and decide which term must move. In the 2007 analysis, as reported, penetration objectives (more buyers) were less popular than loyalty objectives but more effective. Write the behaviour measure with baseline, target and date, and check that the arithmetic reaches the business target.
3. Choose the brand measures that the behaviour change needs: for example, awareness among people who do not yet buy, or links between the brand and buying situations (S1-21, S4-19). Mark these as intermediate: they help explain a result and give early warning, but they are not the result.
4. List the activity measures: reach, frequency, cost per person reached, response counts, delivery of what was planned. These show whether the plan was carried out, not whether it worked.
5. Put the four levels in one table with measure, baseline, target, date, data source and owner. Working rule: success is judged from the top down. Business and behaviour decide whether the effort worked; brand and activity measures explain why or why not.
6. Limit the list and rank it. The source recommends several objectives with clear priorities, and reading a group of four or five measures that move together in preference to any single one. Working rule: one primary measure per level and at most eight in total; mark everything else "watch only".
7. Set the time scale for each level. The IPA summary of their later work says rational, response-type effects arrive fast and fade fast, while brand effects build over years, and that short-term measures alone can mislead. Working rule: read activity weekly, behaviour monthly, brand measures every three to six months, business results over the season and the year.
8. Guard against the short-term trap. Check that at least one measure can show a long-term effect (share, price achieved without discounting, or a brand measure among non-buyers), and that the brand-building layer (S6-05) is not judged on immediate response. A summary of their 2012 report warns that very short-term measures such as direct response rates should not be the main yardstick of long-term success.
9. Book the measurement now. For each primary measure, name how the effect will be separated from season and competitors: a controlled test (S7-02) or before and after tracking with a benchmark (S7-03). Take baselines before launch. A target without a baseline and a method is deleted.
10. Write the links between levels as hypotheses ("if reach X, then awareness Y, then Z new buyers") and send the weakest link to S5-05 for testing. Get the owner's agreement on the table. Return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. A flour mill near Tashkent sells 600 tonnes a month directly to 240 small bakeries (the rest goes through wholesalers). Diagnosis from S0-05: orders per bakery are stable; growth must come from more bakeries. Six-month budget: 180,000,000 soum.

```
LEVEL     MEASURE                              BASELINE   TARGET (Dec)  SOURCE / rabuxmayev39-ai
Business  Direct sales, tonnes per month        600        900          sales system / director
Behaviour Bakeries ordering each month          240        360          CRM / sales head
          (average order 2.5 t, held constant)                          INTERPRETATION
Brand     Bakers in the area who know the mill  30%        45%          survey of 150 bakers,
          when prompted, non-customers only                             before and after (S7-03)
Activity  Bakeries visited with a test bag      0          800          rep log / marketing
          Cost per new bakery                   -          1,500,000    budget / marketing
Watch only: Telegram channel subscribers, calls received
Link hypotheses: 800 visits -> 15% start ordering = 120 new bakeries
  -> 120 x 2.5 t = 300 t -> 600 + 300 = 900 t
Method booked: 4 districts visited first, 4 later as control (S7-02)
Long-term guard: share of direct sales sold without discount, read yearly
```
Arithmetic check: 600 / 240 = 2.5 t per bakery; 360 - 240 = 120 new bakeries; 120 / 800 = 0.15; 120 x 2.5 = 300; 600 + 300 = 900; 360 x 2.5 = 900; 900 / 600 = 1.5, a 50% rise; 180,000,000 / 120 = 1,500,000 soum per new bakery; 45 - 30 = 15 points.

## Common mistakes
- Reporting reach, views, clicks or followers as the result of the campaign.
- Setting only brand measures, or only sales, so that nothing explains the other.
- Choosing the measures after the campaign, from whatever moved.
- Targets with no baseline, no date or no data source.
- Twenty KPIs with no priority, so that something always "improved".
- Judging brand-building work on leads in the first two weeks.
- A behaviour target whose arithmetic does not reach the business target.
- Treating a rise in awareness as proof of sales without checking the business level.

## Quality checklist
- Are all four levels present, each with one primary measure?
- Does each measure have a baseline, a target, a date, a source and an owner?
- Does the behaviour target, multiplied through, reach the business target?
- Are activity measures kept out of the results section?
- Are there at most eight ranked measures, with the rest marked watch only?
- Is at least one long-term measure included, and is the brand layer protected from short-term judgement?
- Is a measurement method (S7-02 or S7-03) booked and the baseline dated before launch?
- Are the links between levels written as hypotheses, with FACT and INTERPRETATION marked?

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Practical caution: owner-led firms may be unwilling to share profit or exact sales. Agree on an index (baseline = 100) or on units, and record that the business level is partly hidden.
- Check which records really exist before writing a source into the table; if sales are logged on paper or in a messenger, plan the time to compile the baseline.
- Brand baselines collected by Telegram bot miss older and rural people; state the sample limits next to the number and keep the same method for the repeat wave (S7-03).
- Set separate rows for Tashkent and the regions when the plan covers both; a single national number can hide opposite movements.
- Polite agreement with targets in a meeting is not ownership. Ask each owner to state the number and the date in their own words, in Uzbek or Russian as they prefer.
- The findings behind this method come from UK award cases; treat the specific percentages as background and the levels as a discipline that still needs local results (S8-08).
