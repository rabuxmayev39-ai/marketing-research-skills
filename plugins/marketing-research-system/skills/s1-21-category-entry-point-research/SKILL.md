# Category Entry Point research (S1-21)
Category Entry Points (CEPs) are the cues buyers use to reach into memory when a buying situation arises: internal ones such as motives and feelings, external ones such as place, time or company. Jenni Romaniuk of the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute developed the approach; it is set out in her book Better Brand Health (2023) and in an open report for the LinkedIn B2B Institute (2022). CEP research has two stages: a qualitative stage that generates candidate CEPs with a set of W prompts, and a survey in which category buyers say how often they meet each situation and pick the brands they link to it. This skill covers generating, wording, prioritising and fielding CEPs; computing the brand metrics from the survey is S4-19. Evidence grade: B, an established professional standard. The published evidence is strongest in consumer goods and services; the B2B report adds business banking and insurance and was funded by a company with an interest in brand advertising. Treat the CEP list as a well-founded working tool, and treat any claim that winning a CEP will raise sales as a hypothesis.

Working rules for the assistant:
- Answer in the language the user writes in (Uzbek, Russian or English).
- Label every statement as FACT (said by buyers or counted in the survey) or INTERPRETATION (your reading).
- When the work is finished, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- The team must decide which buying situations to connect the brand with in communication.
- A brand health tracker or mental availability baseline is planned and needs a CEP list (S1-22, S4-19).
- Existing brand attributes are image words ("quality", "modern") and the team wants measures tied to buying.
Do not use it when:
- The category is new to buyers and they have no buying situations to recall; study the job first (S0-11, S1-07).
- The real barrier is distribution, price or product, already shown by other data.
- Category buyers cannot be reached for a survey in useful numbers. Run the qualitative stage only and label the list "not quantified".
- The client wants one unique positioning idea. CEPs are shared by the whole category; Romaniuk's point is that they belong to the buyer, not to the brand.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
- A category definition in buyers' terms and a rule for who counts as a category buyer.
- Access to category buyers who are not only the client's customers; for B2B, also the client's sales team.
- A brand list with large and small competitors and the client.
- Any existing interviews, search queries, sales notes or customer messages that describe when and why people buy.
- What the client can deliver now and in the near future (range, service, pipeline).

## Procedure
Part A. Decide whether the method fits.
1. Run the fit test with the user: (a) Will the result feed a message decision or a tracker? (b) Do buyers already buy the category, so that situations exist? (c) Can we reach category buyers beyond the client's own customer list? Three yes answers: run both stages. If (c) is no, run the qualitative stage only. If (a) is no, the list will not be used; stop.
2. Fix the category and the respondent. State what counts as buying the category and in what period. For B2B, the report asks for each prompt to be viewed from two sides: the business and the individual professional.

Part B. Generate.
3. Gather raw material from the three sources named in the report: category buyers, marketers, and sales teams where they exist. Aim for variety across buyer types and across time (season, weekday, stage of business). With buyers, ask about recent real purchases, not about brands: "Tell me about the last time you bought. What was going on?" (S1-04, S1-23).
4. Work through the seven W prompts so that no kind of cue is missed. When (time of day, season, how urgent); Where (location of the buyer, staff or customers); While (what else is going on before, during or after); With what (other products it must fit with); Why (the motive or benefit sought); How feeling (emotions before, during or after); With or for whom (other people involved or served).
5. Write each candidate as a short buyer-side phrase that completes a stem such as "Looking for a brand ...". Rules (working rules of this skill, consistent with the report's examples): one idea per CEP; the buyer's own words; no brand name; no quality claim that only one brand could make; a situation or need that would exist even if no brands did.
6. Clean the long list. Merge duplicates, keep the wording buyers used most, tag each CEP with its W and its source. Working rule: expect 20 to 40 candidates before screening. The assistant may suggest candidates, but must tag them "assistant suggestion, not heard from buyers"; Romaniuk notes that AI can help generate CEPs and cannot measure a brand's standing on them.

Part C. Shortlist and field.
7. Screen for internal credibility. With the client, drop CEPs the product range cannot serve now or in the near future. Keep a note of what was dropped and why.
8. Choose the survey list. The report sets no fixed number; its example tested 11 CEPs against 9 brands, and Romaniuk has spoken of about 20 cues. Working rule: 10 to 20 CEPs, covering at least five of the seven Ws, so a phone survey stays short.
9. Build the questionnaire (S3-01) in four parts. (a) Screener for category buyers, and which brands they buy and how often. (b) Prevalence: for each CEP, how often the respondent has met that situation in a stated period. The report measures commonality this way. Where order sizes differ a lot between buyers, it suggests weighting by value ("currency") instead of counting heads. (c) Brand links: each CEP shown with the full brand list and a "none" option; respondents pick as many or as few brands as they like. (d) Working rule: rotate the order of CEPs and of brands between respondents.
10. Pretest the wording with a few buyers (S3-02) and, if two languages are used, check that both versions mean the same (S3-03).
11. Field the survey among category buyers. The report gives no minimum sample; its examples had 311 to 609 respondents. State n and how respondents were found. Below about 100, report results as rough indications (working rule).
12. Prioritise with the report's three tests, a process of elimination. (a) Commonality: how many buyers meet the CEP, or how much value it carries. Romaniuk warns that winning a CEP that rarely arises wastes the message. (b) Competitiveness: CEPs where many brands hold a mental advantage are harder to win. The advantage calculation is done in S4-19. (c) Credibility: outside, whether buyers already link the client to it; inside, whether the client can deliver.
13. Produce the "long-short list". The report recommends 5 to 8 priority CEPs that are common and less contested, and one CEP per advertising execution, building a portfolio over time.
14. Hand over: the survey file to S4-19 for the metrics, the priority list to S9-02 for messages, the full list to S1-22 for tracking. Report with the template, state limits, and return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE for illustration. No real data.
Input: a flour mill sells to small bakeries. Ten baker interviews and a sales-team session produced 26 candidate CEPs; 4 were dropped for internal credibility (for example a flour type the mill does not make); 12 went into a Telegram and phone survey of 150 bakery owners with 6 flour brands.

| CEP (shortened) | W | Bakers who met it in the last month | Prevalence | Brands with a mental advantage | Client can deliver |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Stocking up before a holiday peak | When | 120 | 80% | 1 | yes |
| Need the dough to behave the same every day | Why | 105 | 70% | 3 | yes |
| Usual supplier is late and stock is running out | While | 96 | 64% | 0 | yes |
| Last batch disappointed, looking to change | Why | 75 | 50% | 1 | yes |
| Starting a new product line | With what | 45 | 30% | 0 | partly |

Check: 120/150 = 80%, 105/150 = 70%, 96/150 = 64%, 75/150 = 50%, 45/150 = 30%.
Output: FACT: "holiday peak" is the most common CEP (80%), and "supplier is late" is common (64%) with no brand holding an advantage. FACT: "same every day" is common (70%) but three brands already hold an advantage there. INTERPRETATION: first priorities are "holiday peak" and "supplier is late"; "same every day" is contested and should wait; "new product line" is too rare (30%) and only partly deliverable. Each priority CEP gets its own execution. Brand metrics and deviations go to S4-19.

## Common mistakes
- Writing CEPs from the brand's side: slogans, features, superiority claims.
- Generating the list only in an internal brainstorm, with no buyers. Covering only "why" and missing when, where, while, with what, with whom and feelings.
- Surveying the client's customers only, or showing only the main competitors.
- Choosing priority CEPs by brand strength alone, without checking how often they occur. Putting several CEPs into one advertisement.
- Changing CEP wording between survey waves, which breaks comparison.

## Quality checklist
- Does every CEP describe a buyer situation or need, with no brand and no claim? (yes/no)
- Is each CEP traced to a source (buyer, sales, marketer, assistant suggestion)? (yes/no)
- Are at least five of the seven Ws represented? (yes/no)
- Were CEPs the client cannot serve removed, with a record? (yes/no)
- Does the survey include prevalence, a full brand list and a "none" option? (yes/no)
- Is the sample made of category buyers, with n and recruitment stated? (yes/no)
- Is the priority list 5 to 8 CEPs chosen on commonality, competitiveness and credibility? (yes/no)

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Collect and write CEPs in the language buyers used, Uzbek or Russian; do not translate a list drafted in English. Keep original wording in the report.
- Check that the Uzbek and Russian versions of each CEP name the same situation before fielding.
- Agreeing answer habits can inflate prevalence ("yes, that happens"). Ask for frequency in a fixed recent period, and offer "none" in the brand question.
- Telegram-bot samples miss older and rural buyers; add phone or face-to-face interviews for them, and report Tashkent and regions separately where numbers allow. For B2B categories with few buyers, a census-style phone survey may be more realistic than a large sample; state n plainly.
- Evidence for CEPs comes mainly from Western consumer and business markets; treat the link to sales here as a hypothesis until tracking supports it.
