# Creative brief (S6-18)

A creative brief is the short document, by convention one page, that hands a finished strategy to the people who will make the advertising: designers, copywriters, video makers. It states the objective, the audience, the insight (a true observation about the audience that opens up the problem), a single proposition (the one thing the work must say), the proof that makes it believable, the tone, and the mandatories (things that must or must not appear). The format is an agency convention with no single author; the database entry for this method points to the UK's Institute of Practitioners in Advertising (IPA) and Account Planning Group (APG). The most detailed free guidance opened for this skill is the IPA's guide on how a client should brief an agency, written with BetterBriefs and Mark Ritson (2022). It defines a brief as a roadmap for creative thinking and treats it as an exercise in thinking more than in writing.

Evidence grade: C. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. The creative brief is an agency convention. No study opened for this skill tests whether one section list produces better advertising than another. The figures in the IPA guide come from the BetterBriefs surveys of marketers and agencies, so they measure opinions about briefs, not outcomes: for example, about 80% of marketers thought they wrote good briefs while about 10% of agencies agreed, and the estimate that roughly a third of budgets is wasted through poor briefs is the respondents' own judgement. The guide's authors campaign for better briefs, so they are advocates and not neutral evaluators. Use the format because it forces choices and makes them visible, not because it is proven.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (research findings, proof points) versus INTERPRETATION (the insight, the proposition). Do not write the advertisement inside the brief. When the brief is accepted by the creative team, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- Target audience, positioning and objectives are agreed and creative work must begin.
- Several people or an outside studio will make the work and need the same starting point.
- Past creative work was rejected repeatedly because nobody had agreed what it was for.

Do not use it when:
- The strategy is not decided. The IPA guide's rule is that without a clear marketing strategy there should be no brief. Go back to S6-01 and S6-02.
- The client wants to use the creative process to find the strategy. The guide reports this as a common and costly habit.
- The task is a media or channel plan; that is S9-03 and S9-07.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. The agreed strategy: target audience, positioning statement (S6-02), communication objectives (S9-01).
2. Research findings on the audience, with quotes (S5-02, S5-03).
3. Proof material: facts, tests, guarantees, customer numbers the client can document.
4. Budget, timing, formats and channels.
5. Brand rules: logo, colours, distinctive assets (S6-04), legal requirements.
6. How the work will be judged and by whom.

## Procedure
1. Confirm the strategy is finished. Ask: who exactly is the audience, what position should the brand hold in their mind, what must change? If the client cannot answer, stop and return to strategy.
2. One brief, one strategy. The IPA guide treats aims such as winning new customers and selling more to existing ones as separate choices that need separate briefs. Split the work if more than one aim is present.
3. Write the objective as a chain, following the guide's three levels: the commercial objective (the business result, such as sales), the behavioural objective (what people must do differently) and the attitudinal objective (what they must think or feel for that to happen). Give each a starting figure, a target and a date. Keep the number of objectives small. Check that objective, audience and budget fit each other; the guide calls these three the backbone and says changing one means adjusting the others.
4. Describe the audience as people, not only as demographics: who they are, what they do in the category now, and who is deliberately not targeted.
5. Write the insight. Working rule: one or two sentences giving a true observation from research, in the audience's own words where possible, plus the tension it reveals. Test it with S5-03. The guide's standard is that the whole brief should frame the problem in an interesting way, not merely contain a line labelled insight.
6. Write the single proposition: one message. The guide says a single-minded message is supported by proof points and is not a tagline. Working rule: one sentence, no "and", understandable by someone outside the company. If two candidates remain, choose; do not join them.
7. List the proof: the two or three strongest documented reasons to believe the proposition. Mark each FACT with its source. Unproven claims are removed.
8. Describe the tone: three or four plain words plus one "not" (for example: warm, direct, not jokey). Working rule.
9. List the mandatories: required brand assets, legal text, formats, languages, contact details, things to avoid. Then budget, deadlines and review dates. Put background detail in an appendix so the page stays short; the guide's test is everything needed and nothing more.
10. Agree how the work will be evaluated before any is made. The guide reports that only about 30% of brands had evaluation criteria in place. Write three to five criteria taken from the brief itself.
11. Edit. Use simple words; in the survey only about 5% of agencies said briefs were written in clear language. Do not prescribe the creative solution. Ask one person outside the project to read the page and say back what the work must achieve.
12. Brief in person. The guide separates the document from the briefing, the meeting where it is presented and questioned, and advises against sending it by email alone. Collect questions, revise, and get the creative team's explicit acceptance; the guide treats the brief as a shared responsibility from that point. Return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. Input: a juice brand in Uzbekistan launches a no-added-sugar range. Strategy agreed: win new buying households in Tashkent.

```
CREATIVE BRIEF - no-added-sugar range - six-month campaign
OBJECTIVES                                              FACT (targets agreed)
Commercial:  monthly sales 400,000 -> 460,000 litres (+15%) by month 6
Behavioural: households buying in a month 12% -> 15% (+3 points)
Attitudinal: agree "this juice has no added sugar" 20% -> 35% (+15 points)
AUDIENCE: parents of school-age children who buy packaged juice weekly.
  Not targeting: buyers who choose only on lowest price.
INSIGHT                                                        INTERPRETATION
Parents told us they read the pack but do not trust the word "natural";
  they want to give juice without feeling they are giving sweets.
SINGLE PROPOSITION: The juice with nothing added, so you can say yes.
PROOF (FACT): 1) ingredients list: juice only  2) laboratory certificate on
  file  3) sugar figure printed on the front of the pack
TONE: warm, plain, confident; not lecturing.
MANDATORIES: logo and bottle shape; Uzbek and Russian versions; legal text.
BUDGET: 900 mln soum = production 180 (20%) + media 720 (80%)
EVALUATION: one message? proof visible? brand recognisable in 2 seconds?
```
Arithmetic check: 460,000 - 400,000 = 60,000; 60,000 / 400,000 = 0.15; 15 - 12 = 3; 35 - 20 = 15; 180 + 720 = 900; 180 / 900 = 0.20; 720 / 900 = 0.80.

## Common mistakes
- Writing the brief before the strategy is decided, hoping the creative work will settle it.
- Two or three propositions joined with "and".
- A proposition written as a slogan, or a brief that dictates the visual idea.
- Objectives with no starting figure, target or date.
- An insight that is only a statistic or a description of the audience.
- Sending the page by email with no conversation and no agreed evaluation criteria.

## Quality checklist
- Does the brief serve one strategy and one audience?
- Are commercial, behavioural and attitudinal objectives linked, each with baseline, target and date?
- Is there exactly one proposition, in one sentence, that is not a slogan?
- Is every proof point documented?
- Does the main brief fit on one page, with detail in an appendix?
- Were evaluation criteria agreed before creative work started?
- Was it briefed in person and accepted by the creative team?

## Output template
```
CREATIVE BRIEF - [client / project] - [date] - [author]
WHY NOW (the problem or opportunity in two lines): ...
OBJECTIVES: commercial ... | behavioural ... | attitudinal ... (baseline, target, date)
AUDIENCE: who ... | what they do now ... | not targeting ...
INSIGHT (INTERPRETATION, with source): ...
SINGLE PROPOSITION: ...
PROOF (FACT): 1 ... 2 ... 3 ...
TONE: ... , ... , ... ; not ...
MANDATORIES: assets ... | legal ... | formats ... | languages ... | avoid ...
BUDGET AND TIMING: ... | EVALUATION CRITERIA: ...
ACCEPTED BY: [creative lead, date]
```

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Write the brief in the language the creative team works in, and state which languages the final work needs. Working rule: brief Uzbek and Russian versions as separate pieces of writing, not as a translation of one another (S3-03).
- Keep audience quotes in the original language in the insight line, with a translation beside them.
- If the insight comes from a Telegram survey, note that the sample misses many older and rural people, and say whether it covers Tashkent only.
- In meetings, people may agree politely with a brief they find unclear. Ask the creative lead to repeat the proposition in their own words.
- The guidance and survey figures used here come from the United Kingdom and other markets surveyed by BetterBriefs; how local briefs compare is unknown.
