# Gioia methodology (S4-06)

The Gioia methodology is a systematic way of doing inductive qualitative analysis so that a reader can see every step from raw quotes to concepts. It was set out by Dennis Gioia, Kevin Corley and Aimee Hamilton (2013, Organizational Research Methods) for building new concepts in organisation studies. It works in three levels: first-order concepts in the respondents' own words, second-order themes in the researcher's terms, and a small number of aggregate dimensions. These are drawn as one figure, the data structure, and then turned into a model that shows how the dimensions relate. It starts from the assumption that respondents are knowledgeable about what they do and why, so their terms come first and the researcher's theory second.

Evidence grade: A. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. The method was published in a peer-reviewed methods journal and is widely used in management research. That makes the procedure trustworthy as a way of showing rigour; it does not make any particular finding true. Critics, as reported by Magnani and Gioia (2023), say it is often used as a template that replaces reasoning. Give weight to the traceability it produces, not to the look of the diagram.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (quotes and first-order concepts taken from the data) versus INTERPRETATION (second-order themes, dimensions, model arrows). When the data structure and model are delivered, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- The question is open ("how do bakers decide which flour to trust?") and no ready framework fits.
- The data are semi-structured interviews, with field notes or documents as support.
- The client or a sceptical reader will ask "where did this conclusion come from?"
- The team wants a new concept or a process model, not a list of topics.

Do not use it when:
- The codes are fixed in advance and the task is counting (use S4-21).
- There are fewer than about 6 interviews or only short survey answers; the three levels will be forced. This number is a working rule of this skill, not a sourced threshold.
- The deadline allows only a quick topic summary; use S4-01 and say so.
- The interviews were heavily led by the interviewer; first-order concepts would then reflect the guide, not the respondents.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. The research question in one sentence.
2. Full transcripts in the original language, with respondent IDs and basic profile (segment, role, city).
3. The interview guide and any changes made to it during fieldwork.
4. Supporting material if any: field notes, photos, documents, chat logs.
5. The frameworks or literature the team already has in mind, so that their influence is visible.
6. Who will act as second reader or devil's advocate.

## Procedure
1. Confirm the question and the stance. Ask the user: "What do we want to explain, and whose experience is it?" Remind the team that the first pass records how respondents see things, not how the team sees them.
2. First-order coding. Read each transcript and label every relevant passage with a short phrase that stays close to the respondent's wording; a short sentence is acceptable. Keep the original language for the label and add a translation only as a gloss. Do not merge yet. A large number is expected: summaries of the 2013 paper give roughly 50 to 100 first-order concepts from the first 10 interviews.
3. Reduce by constant comparison. Compare labels across respondents and merge those that mean the same thing; keep a cross-reference list (concept, respondent IDs, quote locations). Aim for a manageable set. Sources differ on the number: one summary of the 2013 paper gives about 25 to 30, and Magnani and Gioia (2023) say fewer than about 20. Treat both as orientation, not as a rule.
4. Second-order themes. Now take the researcher's view. For each cluster of first-order concepts ask: what is going on here that explains the phenomenon? Name the theme in analytical terms. At this point consult existing theory and earlier research; the reasoning becomes abductive (data and theory considered together, see S4-03). If an existing concept already covers the cluster, use its name instead of inventing a new one. Mark every theme INTERPRETATION.
5. Check each theme. Decision rules (working rules of this skill): a theme needs concepts from at least three respondents or it is reported as a single-case observation; it must be supported by quotes that are representative, not only the most vivid; compare segments and note where respondents converge and diverge. Send cases that contradict a theme to S4-07.
6. Aggregate dimensions. Group the second-order themes into a few overarching dimensions; summaries of the method give about 3 to 5. A dimension should say something a reader could not have guessed from the interview guide.
7. Draw the data structure. One figure or table, three columns left to right: first-order concepts, second-order themes, aggregate dimensions. Every theme must connect to at least two first-order concepts and every first-order concept must trace to quotes. Magnani and Gioia describe the data structure as a still picture of the analysis.
8. Build the model. Arrange the dimensions and themes and draw the relationships between them: what leads to what, under which conditions. The arrows are the contribution; each arrow needs evidence (quotes where respondents themselves link the two things) or must be marked as a proposition to test. Hand untested arrows to S5-05.
9. Challenge it. Have the second reader take the devil's advocate role, which the authors recommend to counter over-identifying with respondents. If two coders worked independently, report how disagreements were settled. Ask: which theme is weakest, and what would remove it?
10. Stopping rule. If new interviews are still possible, collect until no new second-order themes appear. If fieldwork is finished, state plainly that saturation was not tested.
11. Write the findings. Present the model first, then walk through each dimension and theme with quotes in the original language, and attach a table of additional supporting quotes. State what was done differently from the standard procedure. Return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. A flour mill interviewed 12 bakery owners (novvoy) in Tashkent and Fergana about why they change flour supplier.
Input: 12 transcripts in Uzbek and Russian; 74 first-order labels after the first pass.

## Common mistakes
- Naming first-order concepts in the team's jargon instead of the respondents' words. Second-order themes that only rename the questions in the interview guide.
- A neat three-column figure with no quotes behind it. Stopping at the data structure and never building the model.
- Keeping too many categories, which the authors call paralysis by analysis. Choosing the most colourful quote instead of the most typical.
- Claiming that the themes "emerged" while hiding the theory the team brought in. Presenting the result as proportions of the market; the method supports transferable concepts, not statistics.

## Quality checklist
- Is every first-order concept in respondent language and traceable to respondent IDs?
- Does every second-order theme rest on at least two first-order concepts?
- Is each level labelled FACT or INTERPRETATION?
- Are the aggregate dimensions few and not copies of the interview guide?
- Does each model arrow have evidence or a "proposition" label?
- Did a second reader challenge the structure, and is the result recorded?
- Are contradicting cases reported, and is the saturation claim honest?
- Are quotes kept in the original language?

## Output template
```
GIOIA ANALYSIS - [project] - [date]
Research question: ...
Data: [n] interviews | segments | languages | supporting material
Deviations from standard procedure: ...

DATA STRUCTURE
1st-order concept (respondent words, IDs) [FACT] | 2nd-order theme [INTERP] | Aggregate dimension [INTERP]

MODEL
Dimension A -> Dimension B: evidence (IDs) / PROPOSITION
Conditions and segment differences: ...

SUPPORTING QUOTES TABLE
Theme | quote (original language) | respondent ID

CHALLENGE LOG
Weakest theme: ... | Contradicting cases: ... | Second reader changes: ...
Saturation: tested / not tested - why
```

## Works with
- S4-01 thematic analysis: the lighter alternative when traceability to this level is not needed.
- S4-03 abductive analysis: the reasoning used at the second-order stage.
- S4-04 coding cycles: first cycle supplies first-order codes, second cycle the themes.
- S4-05 matrix displays: compare themes by segment before building the model.
- S4-07 negative case analysis: test each theme and arrow against cases that do not fit.
- S2-08 trustworthiness criteria and S5-14 member checking: credibility checks on the result.
- S5-02 insight ladder and S5-05 what would have to be true: turn dimensions and propositions into insights and tests.

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Code first-order concepts in the language the respondent used (Uzbek or Russian, including mixed speech). Translate only at the second-order level and keep the original quote beside it.
- Polite or agreeing answers can produce first-order concepts that echo the interviewer's wording. Check each concept: did the respondent introduce the term, or repeat it from the question?
- Record city or region for every respondent and do not merge Tashkent and regional respondents into one theme without checking that both support it.
- The method was developed in Western organisational research. Its steps transfer as a procedure; any imported theory used to name second-order themes is a hypothesis here, not a fact.
- If respondents were recruited through a Telegram bot, note that older and rural people are likely missing from the data structure.
