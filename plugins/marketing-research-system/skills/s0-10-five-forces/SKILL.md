# Five forces (S0-10)

The five forces framework explains why some industries are more profitable than others. It was introduced by Michael Porter of Harvard Business School in a 1979 Harvard Business Review article and restated by him in 2008. The idea: the money an industry earns is shared out between the companies in it, their suppliers, their buyers, possible newcomers and makers of substitute products. Five forces decide who keeps how much: rivalry among existing competitors, the threat of new entrants, the threat of substitutes, the bargaining power of buyers and the bargaining power of suppliers. Strong forces leave little profit for the companies inside the industry; weak forces leave more. In marketing work it is used to judge whether a category is worth entering or staying in, and where the pressure on margin comes from.

Evidence grade: B. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. The framework is a long-standing standard in strategy teaching and consulting, built on economic reasoning about industry structure. It is not a measuring instrument: two analysts can rate the same force differently, and no test of the accuracy of its forecasts was found among the sources opened for this skill. Its listed limits: it works at the level of the whole industry and says little about consumers or about communication. Published criticism, summarised in an open-access review (Isabelle and colleagues, 2020): it is called too static, hard to turn into measures, tilted towards large firms, and focused on a supply chain instead of a network of partners; those authors propose adding innovation, globalisation, digitalisation and regulation as extra forces, and admit that their extended version is itself untested. Use the framework to organise evidence and argue about attractiveness; do not present a rating as a measured result.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (a number or event with a source and date) versus INTERPRETATION (every rating of a force is an interpretation). Never rate a force with no evidence line under it. When the analysis is written, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- A client is deciding whether to enter a new category, region or channel, or whether to keep investing in one.
- Margins are falling and the team must find out who is taking the money: retailers, suppliers, rivals or substitutes.
- A situation analysis (S0-07) needs a structured competitor and environment part.

Do not use it when:
- The question is about consumers: why they buy, what they think, which message works. Use S0-11, S1-04 or S1-21.
- The "industry" cannot be defined (a brand-new product with no comparable sellers). Start with S0-11 to find what it would replace.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. The decision to be made and by whom (S0-02).
2. A first definition of the industry: which products, which geography.
3. Desk data (S1-03): number and size of competitors, prices, trade margins, main suppliers and input prices, import rules, category growth. Sources for Uzbekistan: S8-07, S8-04.
4. People who know the trade from inside: the client's sales head, purchasing manager, one or two distributors (S0-12).
5. A rough split of one unit's retail price into who receives what, even if estimated.

## Procedure
1. Define the industry. Following the steps a university library guide attributes to Porter (2008): state which products are in, which belong to a separate industry, and the geographic area in which competition takes place. Working rule: if buyers, suppliers or rivals differ sharply between two products or two areas, analyse them as two industries (for example, juice in cartons sold in Tashkent chains versus juice sold loose at bazaars).
2. List the participants and group them: buyer groups (end consumers, chains, small shops, wholesalers), supplier groups, competitors, substitutes, possible entrants. One industry may have several buyer groups with different power; keep them apart.
3. Rate rivalry. According to the Harvard Institute for Strategy and Competitiveness page, rivalry is fiercer when competitors are many or similar in size, growth is slow, fixed costs are high, leaving the industry is costly, or rivals are strongly committed. Collect a fact for each condition.
4. Rate the threat of new entrants. It depends on barriers: economies of scale, the cost of building brand awareness, access to distribution channels, and government restrictions. The threat exists before anyone enters, because it caps the prices incumbents can charge. Ask the client: who entered in the last three years, and what did it cost them?
5. Rate the threat of substitutes. A substitute meets the same need in a different way (the source's example is videoconferencing in place of travel). It is strong when the substitute offers an attractive price for its performance and switching is cheap. Do not confuse substitutes with rival brands. S0-11 helps to find them.
6. Rate buyer power. It is highest when buyers are large relative to sellers, products are little differentiated, the purchase is a significant cost for the buyer, and switching is cheap. Powerful buyers push prices down or demand more service.
7. Rate supplier power. It is greater when one or two suppliers control an essential input or when changing supplier is costly or slow.
8. Working rule (this skill's own, not from Porter): give each force a rating of weak, medium or strong, each with at least two evidence lines marked FACT or ASSUMPTION. Do not add ratings into a single score; one strong force can decide profitability on its own.
9. Put the forces together and test against money. Explain the level of industry profit and name the one or two forces that control it. Then check the story against real long-run profitability where figures exist, and ask whether the more profitable firms are better protected from the forces. Working rule: build a "who gets what from one unit sold" table to make this concrete.
10. Look at change. For each force note recent and likely shifts, good and bad. The 2008 article's summary stresses three points: fast growth does not guarantee profit; government policy matters through its effect on the forces; complements (products used together with yours) belong in the analysis.
11. Draw implications. The source gives two routes: defend against the strong forces, or find a position where they are weaker. Also note which parts of the structure the client, rivals or entrants could reshape. Write open assumptions into S0-13 and return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. A juice brand in Tashkent considers entering bottled cold tea (1-litre bottles). All figures are invented.

```
INDUSTRY: bottled cold tea, 1 litre, sold through Tashkent retail
WHO GETS WHAT from one bottle at 9,000 soum retail (invented)
  Retailer margin 25%                        2,250
  Producer receives                          6,750
    Ingredients and packaging (60%)          4,050
    Production and delivery (20%)            1,350
    Marketing (10%)                            675
      Producer profit (10%)                      675
FORCES                                       rating (INTERPRETATION)
  Rivalry: 2 large brands, 6 small (assumed) strong
  New entrants: any juice plant can bottle   strong
  Substitutes: home-brewed tea, water, juice strong
  Buyer power: 2 chains = 55% of volume      strong
  Supplier power: bottle preforms, 2 sources medium
SCENARIO: the 2 chains demand a 30% margin
  Producer receives from chains 9,000 x 0.70 = 6,300 (450 less)
  Average loss per bottle 450 x 0.55 = 247.5
  Profit 675 - 247.5 = 427.5; share of receipts 427.5 / 6,502.5 = 6.6%
CONCLUSION (INTERPRETATION): buyer power controls profit; entry pays
  only with a channel where chains matter less (cafes, own delivery).
  To verify: chain share of volume, real trade margins (S1-13, S0-12).
```
Arithmetic check: 9,000 x 0.25 = 2,250; 9,000 - 2,250 = 6,750; 6,750 x 0.60 = 4,050; 6,750 x 0.20 = 1,350; 6,750 x 0.10 = 675; 4,050 + 1,350 + 675 = 6,075; 6,750 - 6,075 = 675; 9,000 x 0.70 = 6,300; 6,750 - 6,300 = 450; 450 x 0.55 = 247.5; 675 - 247.5 = 427.5; 6,750 - 247.5 = 6,502.5; 427.5 / 6,502.5 = 0.0657.

## Common mistakes
- Defining the industry too broadly ("drinks") or too narrowly (the client's own product).
- Listing rival brands under substitutes.
- Rating forces from opinion with no fact under each rating.
- Averaging the five ratings into one number.
- Treating a fast-growing category as attractive without checking who keeps the profit, or describing today only and ignoring how the forces are shifting.

## Quality checklist
- Is the industry defined by product and geography?
- Are buyer groups and supplier groups listed separately where their power differs?
- Does every rating have at least two evidence lines marked FACT or ASSUMPTION?
- Are substitutes true substitutes, not competing brands?
- Is the controlling force named and tested against a money table or real profit figures?
- Are implications written as choices (defend, reposition, reshape, stay out)?
- Are unverified points passed to the S0-13 register?

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Published figures on company profits and trade margins may be hard to find. Practical caution: expect to rely on interviews with distributors and the client's own invoices, and mark such numbers as estimates.
- Tashkent and the regions may differ in channels and buyer power; analyse them separately when the evidence differs.
- Hypothesis to check, not a fact: informal sellers and bazaar trade may act as rivals or substitutes that official statistics do not show. Ask sales staff what they see on the ground.
- Customs duties, licences and certification can change the entry barrier quickly. Record the date of every regulatory fact and re-check before the report.
- Run expert interviews in Uzbek or Russian and keep quotes in the original language. Polite agreement is not confirmation; ask for an example or a number. The framework was developed on Western industries, so treat its transfer to a local category as a hypothesis.
