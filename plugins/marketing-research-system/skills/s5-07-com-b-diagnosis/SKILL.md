# COM-B behavioural diagnosis and the Behaviour Change Wheel (S5-07)

COM-B is a model of behaviour published by Susan Michie, Maartje van Stralen and Robert West in 2011. It says a behaviour (B) happens only when a person has the capability (C), the opportunity (O) and the motivation (M) for it, and that these interact. The Behaviour Change Wheel (BCW) places COM-B at the centre, surrounds it with nine intervention functions, and adds an outer ring of seven policy categories. The authors built it from a review of 19 existing frameworks, none of which covered everything. It is used to work out what must change for a behaviour to happen and which kinds of action fit that diagnosis. Evidence grade: A. The framework is peer-reviewed, open access, and its categories were tested for coder agreement. Two limits matter for weight: the testing was done in public health (tobacco and obesity policy), not in commercial marketing, and the authors themselves say research is still needed on whether using the wheel produces better interventions. So rely on it as a well-founded structure for diagnosis; treat every diagnosis as a hypothesis until checked with data.

Working rules for the assistant:
- Answer in the language the user writes in (Uzbek, Russian or English).
- Label every statement as FACT (observed in data) or INTERPRETATION (your inference).
- When the work is finished, return to the "research-orchestrator" skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it

Use it when:
- The team wants a specific group to do a specific thing (try, switch, reorder, visit, recommend, pay on time) and it is not happening enough.
- The team's default answer is "we need more advertising" and nobody has checked whether the barrier is ability or access.
- Research data exist and need sorting into barriers and enablers.

Do not use it when:
- The goal is an outcome with no behaviour defined ("increase brand love", "grow sales"). Define the behaviour first.
- The question is which group to target (use S5-09) or what motive sits behind a preference (use S5-06).
- There are no data at all about the target group. A COM-B table filled from team opinion is a list of assumptions; label it so and plan research.

## What you need before starting (inputs)

- The business problem and the desired result.
- Who the target group is.
- Evidence about that group: interviews, observation, survey answers, sales or CRM data, complaints.
- What the company can and cannot change (price, product, distribution, service, communication).

## Procedure

The steps follow the published BCW design sequence (Michie and colleagues; summarised openly in Atkins and Michie 2015), adapted for a marketing team.

1. Define the problem as behaviour. Ask: "Who needs to do what differently?" Rewrite an outcome ("sales in the regions are low") as behaviours of named actors ("shop owners in Samarkand do not reorder within 30 days").
2. List candidate behaviours and select one or two. List all behaviours of all actors that affect the problem (buyer, seller, installer, family member who decides). Rate each on: likely impact if it changed, how likely it is to change, and spillover on other behaviours. Pick one or two. Decision rule: if the team cannot choose, pick the behaviour closest to the money that the company can influence directly.
3. Specify the target behaviour. Write it as: who, what, when, where, how often, with whom. If any element is unknown, mark it as a research gap. A vague behaviour produces a vague diagnosis.
4. Run the COM-B diagnosis. For the specified behaviour, go through the six components and ask what the evidence shows. Use these definitions from the original paper:
   - Physical capability: bodily ability and physical skill to do it.
   - Psychological capability: knowledge, understanding, reasoning and mental skills needed.
   - Physical opportunity: what the environment allows or prompts (time, money, access, location, tools, cues).
   - Social opportunity: what the social and cultural setting makes thinkable and acceptable (norms, what others do, shared language).
   - Reflective motivation: conscious evaluations, beliefs about results, plans, intentions.
   - Automatic motivation: emotions, impulses, habits, learned associations.
   For each component record: evidence (quote, observation or number with source), whether it is a barrier, an enabler or unknown, and your confidence (high: several independent data points; medium: one data source; low: team assumption).
5. Ask the diagnostic questions the team tends to skip:
   - Capability: do they know the option exists, know how to do it, and remember it at the right moment?
   - Opportunity: is it physically available where and when they decide? Can they afford it at that moment? Does anyone around them do it?
   - Motivation: do they believe it will work for them? Is there an existing habit they must break? What do they feel at the moment of choice?
6. Decide what needs to change. Mark the one to three components where (a) the evidence of a barrier is strongest and (b) the company can act. Decision rules:
   - If capability or opportunity is a clear barrier, fix that first. Persuasion aimed at motivation will not help a person who cannot find, afford or operate the product.
   - If all three look fine but the behaviour still does not happen, look harder at automatic motivation (habit) and at whether the behaviour was specified correctly.
   - If evidence is "unknown" for more than half the table, stop and propose research instead of interventions.
7. Map to intervention functions. The paper links components to the nine functions as follows:
   - Physical capability: training, enablement.
   - Psychological capability: education, training, enablement.
   - Physical opportunity: restriction, environmental restructuring, enablement.
   - Social opportunity: restriction, environmental restructuring, enablement.
   - Reflective motivation: education, persuasion, incentivisation, coercion.
   - Automatic motivation: persuasion, incentivisation, coercion, environmental restructuring, modelling, enablement.
   Meanings in brief: education adds knowledge; persuasion uses communication to create feeling or prompt action; incentivisation creates expectation of reward; coercion creates expectation of cost; training builds skill; restriction uses rules to limit options; environmental restructuring changes the physical or social setting; modelling gives an example to copy; enablement adds means or removes barriers.
8. Translate functions into marketing actions. For each selected function write one to three concrete actions. Examples of translation: environmental restructuring = distribution, shelf position, pack size, order process; enablement = instalment payment, delivery, free samples; training = demonstrations for buyers or sales staff; modelling = showing a credible peer using the product. Coercion and restriction are mainly policy tools; a commercial team will rarely use them, and should say so rather than force a fit.
9. Note the delivery level. The outer ring of the wheel lists seven policy categories (communication/marketing, guidelines, fiscal, regulation, legislation, environmental/social planning, service provision). For an agency project, most actions sit under communication/marketing and service provision. Mention others only if the client is a public body or an association.
10. Screen options with APEASE. Rate each action yes / no / unsure on: Affordability, Practicality, Effectiveness and cost-effectiveness, Acceptability, Side-effects and safety, Equity. Drop actions that fail on any criterion the client treats as fixed.
11. State how each action will be tested. For every kept action write the behaviour measure, the comparison (before/after, test area versus control area) and the time frame.
12. Report with the output template and return to "research-orchestrator".

## Worked example

INVENTED EXAMPLE for illustration. No real data.

Input: a juice brand wants small neighbourhood shops in regional towns to keep its 1-litre pack in the refrigerator. Evidence: 12 shop-owner interviews and visit notes from 30 shops.

Target behaviour: shop owner (who) places at least one row of the brand's 1-litre pack in the fridge (what) at each restock (when), in the shop's own fridge (where), every week (how often), with the distributor's agent present (with whom).

| Component | Evidence | Status | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Physical capability | Owners stock fridges daily (FACT, visit notes) | Enabler | High |
| Psychological capability | 7 of 12 did not know chilled juice sells faster for this brand (FACT, interviews) | Barrier | Medium |
| Physical opportunity | In 22 of 30 shops the only fridge belongs to a soft-drink company and its agent checks it (FACT) | Barrier | High |
| Social opportunity | "Hamma shunday qiladi" - neighbours also keep juice on the shelf (FACT, 4 mentions) | Barrier | Medium |
| Reflective motivation | Owners expect fridge space to earn more from soft drinks (FACT, 6 mentions) | Barrier | Medium |
| Automatic motivation | Restocking follows a fixed routine (INTERPRETATION) | Unknown | Low |

What needs to change: physical opportunity first (no fridge space the owner controls), then psychological capability.
Functions: environmental restructuring and enablement; education.
Actions: small branded counter cooler on loan (enablement); agent places the first row at each visit (environmental restructuring); one-page sales comparison from pilot shops (education).
APEASE: cooler loan is unsure on affordability; pilot in 20 shops against 20 comparison shops for 8 weeks, measure weekly units sold and share of visits with juice in a fridge.
INTERPRETATION: a consumer advertising campaign would not address the main barrier.

## Common mistakes

- Starting from the intervention ("let's do an influencer campaign") and filling COM-B afterwards to justify it.
- Diagnosing an outcome or an attitude instead of a specified behaviour.
- Putting everything under motivation. Knowledge is capability; price and availability are opportunity.
- Filling cells with team opinion and not marking confidence.
- Treating the component-to-function links as proof that an action will work. They show which types fit, not that they are effective here.
- Diagnosing only the end consumer when a seller, installer or family decision-maker controls the behaviour.

## Quality checklist

- Is the target behaviour specified by who, what, when, where, how often and with whom? (yes/no)
- Were several candidate behaviours listed before one or two were chosen? (yes/no)
- Does each of the six components have evidence or an explicit "unknown"? (yes/no)
- Is each piece of evidence labelled fact or interpretation, with a source? (yes/no)
- Were only one to three components selected as needing change, with reasons? (yes/no)
- Do the chosen intervention functions match the paper's links for those components? (yes/no)
- Was each action screened with APEASE? (yes/no)
- Does each action have a behaviour measure and a comparison for testing? (yes/no)

## Works with

- S1-07 JTBD switch interviews and S1-15 laddering interviews: supply evidence for the motivation and opportunity rows.
- S4-03 abductive analysis: use when the evidence contradicts the team's expected barrier.
- S5-06 means-end value map: details reflective motivation; COM-B checks whether motivation is the real barrier.
- S5-08 behavioural economics lens: gives specific ideas for automatic motivation and for restructuring the choice setting.
- S5-02 insight ladder and S5-03 insight tests: turn the main barrier into an insight and test it.
- S5-09 needs-based segments: run a separate diagnosis per segment when barriers differ.
- S6-15 persuasion principles: use only after the diagnosis shows that persuasion is the fitting function.
- S6-03 growth through penetration: opportunity barriers often correspond to weak physical availability.

## Notes for Uzbekistan

- Collect and keep evidence in the original language (Uzbek or Russian); quote as spoken.
- Polite or agreeing answers can make motivation look stronger than it is ("ha, albatta olaman"). Give more weight to observed behaviour and purchase records than to stated intention.
- Check who actually performs the behaviour. Ask rather than assume who decides, who pays and who uses.
- Diagnose Tashkent and the regions separately when distribution, payment options or shop formats differ; physical opportunity may not be the same.
- COM-B was developed and tested in UK public health. Its categories are general, but which barriers dominate here is an open question. Treat each transferred conclusion as a hypothesis.
