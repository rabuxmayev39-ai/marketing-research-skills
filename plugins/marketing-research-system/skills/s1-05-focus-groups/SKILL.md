# Focus groups (S1-05)

A focus group is a planned discussion among a small number of carefully recruited, similar people, led by a moderator through a prepared sequence of questions in a relaxed setting, and repeated with several groups. The standard guide is Richard Krueger and Mary Anne Casey, Focus Groups: A Practical Guide for Applied Research (Sage). Their free handouts, used here, set out the core practice: groups of 5 to 10 with 6 to 8 preferred, participants alike on what matters for the topic, a moderator who creates a "permissive" atmosphere, an assistant who takes notes, and a questioning route that moves from general to specific. The method is for hearing how people talk about a subject among themselves: their words, what they treat as normal, and how they react to ideas, packs, adverts or concepts.

Evidence grade: B. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. Focus groups are a long-established professional method with detailed practitioner guidance. They show range, language and shared norms. They do not give numbers, and what people say in front of others is shaped by the group. Use the output as hypotheses and wording, and check anything decisive with another method.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (what was said, by which group, with quotes in the original language) versus INTERPRETATION (themes and readings). The assistant helps plan, write the route and analyse; a person moderates. When the plan or the report is delivered, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- You need the vocabulary and shared ideas people use about a category, for example before writing a survey or a message.
- You want reactions to stimuli (pack, concept, advert, price presentation) and the discussion they provoke.
- Norms matter: what the group treats as acceptable, expected or embarrassing.
- Participants can meet, are willing to speak in front of each other, and are similar enough to feel at ease.

Do not use it when (Krueger and Casey's list, plus this agency's index limit):
- Statistically representative data are needed, a more efficient method would get the information, or the real aim is consensus, teaching or testing knowledge.
- Sharing could harm participants; a moderator cannot promise that others will keep things confidential. Use S1-04.
- The topic is private or tied to status (income, debts, family conflict), where group pressure pushes answers toward what looks respectable. Use S1-04.
- People are polarised on the topic, or there are power differences in the room (boss and staff, supplier and buyer).
- Each person's full decision story is needed; a group gives each person only a few minutes.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. The decision, research questions and what is already known (S2-01).
2. Participant definition: the shared characteristics, and the types to be compared in separate groups.
3. A moderator and an assistant moderator; neither should represent the client in the participants' eyes.
4. A comfortable room where people can sit in a circle, recording equipment, name cards with first names, refreshments, stimuli if any, and the incentive.
5. Budget and calendar for several groups per participant type.

## Procedure
1. Check fit. Ask the user: "Is the topic something people would discuss in front of strangers of their own kind?" and "Do you need words and reactions, or numbers?" If the first answer is no, switch to S1-04; if numbers, S1-08.
2. Define participant types and keep them apart. Recruit people who share the experience relevant to the topic, and put different audiences in separate groups so their answers can be compared. Avoid power differences inside a group.
3. Set the number of groups. Krueger and Casey: plan three or four groups for each participant type; do not use a statistical formula; continue until no new insights appear, which usually happens after three or four groups with one audience. Every extra type multiplies the count, so limit types to those the decision needs.
4. Set size and length. Six to eight people per group. About two hours for adults. No more than two groups a day for one moderator.
5. Write the questioning route. Use the five question types in order: opening (a quick round so everyone speaks), introductory, transition, key, and ending. About a dozen questions fit a two-hour group, and about half the time should go to the key questions. Beginners who write 20 to 30 questions get shallow answers. Wording rules from Krueger: open-ended; no yes or no questions; rarely ask "why" (ask what influenced the choice or what features mattered); use "think back" questions about past experience, not the future; move from general to specific. Useful devices: lists, choosing among alternatives, rating on a card before speaking, sentence completion, drawing.
6. Plan the ending. Three ending questions: an all-things-considered question, the assistant's short summary with "is this an adequate summary?", and "have we missed anything?".
7. Recruit. Build a pool of qualified people (lists, nominations, on-site recruiting, snowball, adverts) and select from it. Invite personally, send a written confirmation, and call the day before. Krueger and Casey advise avoiding the term "focus group" in invitations because it can intimidate. Incentives can be money, food or gifts; an easy location and help with transport or childcare also raise attendance. Working rule: invite one or two more people than needed per group to cover no-shows.
8. Moderate. Open with welcome, topic overview, ground rules (no wrong answers, recording, first names, one person at a time, talk to each other), then the first question. Use a five-second pause after a question or comment, and neutral probes: "Would you explain further?", "Can you give an example?". Keep reactions neutral; avoid "that's good". Manage experts, dominant talkers, shy people and ramblers with mild control. Working rule against group pressure: before discussing a key question or stimulus, have each person write their answer on a card, then discuss.
9. Assistant's job. Take notes on a standard form: key points per question, notable quotes with speaker initials, non-verbal signs, a seating diagram, and check the recorder.
10. Analyse on Krueger's timetable. In the group: probe vague or inconsistent comments and summarise for confirmation. Immediately after: moderator and assistant debrief and note themes and surprises. Within hours: write up that group question by question with quotes. Within days of the last group: compare across groups and types. Weigh comments by words, context, internal consistency, frequency, extensiveness (how many different people), intensity and specificity; concrete first-person experience counts for more. Frequency alone does not show importance.
11. Report three or four main findings first, in words, with a few quotes, and state the limits. Do not report percentages. Return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. Input: a juice brand has three new pack designs and wants to hear how mothers of school-age children talk about juice for children and react to the packs. The topic is not private and reactions to stimuli are needed, so groups fit.

```
DESIGN
Types: 2 (Tashkent mothers; mothers in one regional city). Groups: 3 per type = 6 groups.
Size: 7 seated per group = 42 participants. Invite 9 per group = 54 invitations.
Length: 120 minutes; 12 questions; 60 minutes on the 6 key questions (Q4-Q9).
Schedule: 2 groups per day = 3 fieldwork days.
QUESTIONING ROUTE (extract)
Opening      1. Ismingiz va bolangiz nechanchi sinfda o'qiydi?
Introductory 2. Think back to the last time you bought a drink for your child. What happened?
Transition   3. What makes a drink "good for a child" in your view?
Key          4. [Packs A, B, C on table] Write on your card which you would pick up first. Then discuss.
Key          5. What on this pack tells you something about what is inside?
Ending       10. All things considered, what matters most? 11. Is this summary adequate?
             12. Have we missed anything?
REPORT EXTRACT
All 6 groups used "tabiiy" / "натуральный" and argued about what it means.   FACT
Pack B was called "like medicine" in 4 of 6 groups, both types.              FACT
"Natural" is the shared entry word but has no agreed meaning.                INTERP
Limit: cards for Q4 show first reactions only; 42 people are not a sample
  for shares. Test pack choice in S1-09.
```
Arithmetic check: 2 x 3 = 6 groups; 6 x 7 = 42; 6 x 9 = 54; half of 120 minutes = 60; 6 groups / 2 per day = 3 days.

## Common mistakes
- Running one group and treating it as the answer; a single group can be unusual.
- Mixing types (ages, incomes, bosses with staff) so that some people go quiet; too many questions, so nothing is discussed in depth.
- Asking "why" and "would you buy" instead of past experience and influences.
- Counting hands and reporting percentages; taking the loudest participant's view as the group's view; not checking how many different people said it.
- A moderator who teaches, defends the product or is known to work for the client.
- Using groups for private or status topics where people will say what looks proper.

## Quality checklist
- Is the topic suitable for discussion in front of similar strangers?
- Are participant types kept in separate groups, with three or four groups per type planned?
- Does each group have six to eight participants without power differences?
- Does the route have about a dozen open questions, with half the time on key questions?
- Did participants record individual views before key discussions?
- Was each group debriefed and written up within hours?
- Does the report weigh extensiveness and specificity, not only frequency, and avoid percentages?

## Works with
- S1-01 research design types: groups are an exploratory method. S1-02: groups often supply wording for a survey.
- S1-04 in-depth interviews: use instead for private, status or B2B topics, or alongside for individual stories. S1-06 observation: when behaviour matters more than talk.
- S2-04 saturation and S2-06 purposive sampling: number and make-up of groups. S2-10 ethics and consent.
- S3-06 indirect questioning and S3-10 projective and metaphor techniques: devices for the route. S3-07 response styles: reading agreement. S4-01 thematic analysis and S4-05 matrix displays (rows as groups): analysis. S5-01 affinity diagram: team synthesis.
- S1-08 surveys, S1-09 experiments, S9-06 creative pre-testing: verify reactions with numbers. S1-21 Category Entry Point research: groups can supply candidate wording.

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Run each group in one language, Uzbek or Russian, chosen at recruitment; mixed-language groups leave some people silent. Keep quotes in the original language.
- Polite or agreeing habits and respect for older or higher-status people can strengthen group pressure. Keep groups close in age and status, consider separate groups for men and women where the topic calls for it, and always collect written individual answers before discussion.
- The moderator should not be seen as the client's representative; participants may avoid criticism of a host.
- Plan groups in at least one region as well as Tashkent if the decision covers both, and budget for the extra groups this adds.
- Recruiting through Telegram channels reaches active users and tends to miss older and rural people; add offline recruitment where they matter. Krueger and Casey's guidance grew from US practice. The procedure transfers; how freely people disagree in a group here is a hypothesis to watch in the first group.
