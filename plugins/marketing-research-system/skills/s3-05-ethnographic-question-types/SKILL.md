# Ethnographic question types (S3-05)

Ethnographic question types are a set of interview questions designed to make a person describe their own world in their own terms, instead of answering inside the researcher's categories. They come from the anthropologist James P. Spradley's book The Ethnographic Interview (1979). He grouped the questions into three families. Descriptive questions collect a long sample of the person's own language (grand-tour, mini-tour, example, experience and native-language questions). Structural questions find out how the person organises that knowledge into categories. Contrast questions find out which differences matter to the person inside a category. In the book the three families are steps in a longer sequence in which asking alternates with analysis: descriptive questions, then a domain analysis, then structural questions, then a taxonomic analysis, then contrast questions.

Evidence grade: B. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. The question types are a long-standing standard in qualitative teaching, and open textbooks still teach them from Spradley. They are a craft standard, not an experimentally tested technique: no study opened for this skill measured whether they give better data than other openings. Use them with confidence as a way to structure an interview; do not present their output as tested fact.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (what the respondent said, the terms they used) versus INTERPRETATION (the categories and contrasts the team infers). Keep respondents' terms in the original language. When the guide or the term lists are delivered, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- The team does not yet know how customers name, group and compare things in the category (early exploratory work).
- An interview guide needs an opening that gets long, concrete answers before any evaluative question.
- Later work (a questionnaire, segmentation, messages) needs the customers' own words and categories, and people can give a long session or a second one.

Do not use it when:
- The interview is short. The sequence takes time; working rule: under about 40 minutes, use one grand-tour question and two or three mini-tour questions only, and skip the structural and contrast families.
- The goal is to reconstruct one purchase decision. Use S1-07 or S3-04.
- The goal is to count how common something is. Use S1-08 with S2-03.
- The respondent is not a regular participant in the activity; there is no everyday routine or vocabulary to tour.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. The research question and the scene to be explored: a place, a time period, a task or a group of people (S2-01).
2. Respondents who do the activity regularly and can give enough time (S2-06, S2-04).
3. Permission to record, so the exact words are kept (S2-10).
4. The interview length and whether a second session is possible.
5. Any terms the team already uses for the category, written down so they can be kept out of the questions (S2-11).

## Procedure
1. Explain the purpose in plain words and repeat it during the interview. The open textbook chapters describe this as telling the person that the interviewer wants to learn from their point of view and is not testing them. Say openly what you do not know; expressing ignorance and interest builds rapport.
2. Open with one grand-tour question about the scene. A grand-tour question asks for a verbal tour of a place, a period of time, a sequence of events, people, activities or objects. Choose one of four forms. Typical: the usual pattern ("Describe a usual working day from the start"). Specific: one particular recent occasion, in order ("Tell me about yesterday, from when you arrived until you left"); asking about the most recent occasion helps memory. Guided: the person shows the interviewer around the real place. Task-related: the person does a task and explains it as they go, with simple clarifiers ("What are you doing now?", "What is this?") to keep it going.
3. Write down every term the person uses for things, people, steps and problems. Do not translate them into the team's words. Restate the person's points in their own words to show interest and check understanding.
4. Follow with mini-tour questions. They have the same form as a grand tour but cover one smaller part the person mentioned ("You said you check the delivery; describe what you do when you check it").
5. Ask example questions for any term or activity that is still abstract ("Can you give me an example of ...?"), and experience questions for notable events ("What experiences have you had with ...?"). Working rule: experience questions tend to bring out unusual events, so use them after the routine has been described.
6. Ask native-language questions whenever the person seems to be translating for you. Three forms: direct-language ("What do you call that?"), hypothetical-interaction ("If you were talking to another baker, what would you say?"), and typical-sentence ("Give me a sentence where you would use that word"). The aim is to learn how a term is used, in the person's own setting.
7. Between sessions, or in a short pause, group the collected terms. Look for a cover term (a name for a category) and the terms included in it. This is a small version of Spradley's domain analysis and is the team's INTERPRETATION until checked.
8. Ask structural questions to check and fill the categories. Types: verification ("So X is a kind of Y?"), cover-term ("What are all the kinds of Y?"), included-term ("Are A and B both the same kind of thing?"), substitution frame (repeat the person's sentence with one word left out for them to fill), and card sorting (terms on cards, sorted by the person). Repeat structural questions at different moments, because people do not recall all members of a category at once, and explain why you are asking.
9. Ask contrast questions inside one category. Types: contrast verification (confirm a difference you think you heard), directed contrast (start from one known feature and ask which other items share it), dyadic (how do these two differ?), triadic (which two of these three are alike and which is different?), contrast-set sorting (sort cards into piles and explain the piles), twenty questions, and rating or ranking items on a quality the person named.
10. Mix the families. Introduce new kinds of question gradually and return to descriptive questions and ordinary friendly talk; a long run of structural or contrast questions feels like an interrogation and harms rapport.
11. Decide the time split before the interview. Working rule for a 60-minute first interview: about one third on descriptive questions before any structural question is asked.
12. Produce the outputs: the guide, a term list in the original language, a table of categories with their included terms, and the contrasts the person used. Mark which were confirmed by the respondent (FACT that they confirmed it) and which are the team's grouping (INTERPRETATION). Return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. Input: a flour mill wants to understand how bakers who make bread in a tandir judge flour. One 60-minute interview at the bakery, in Uzbek. The terms below are invented for illustration.

```
TIME PLAN  5 intro + 20 descriptive + 15 structural + 15 contrast + 5 close = 60 min
DESCRIPTIVE
Grand tour (specific): "Kechagi ish kuningizni boshidan oxirigacha aytib bering."
Mini-tour: "Xamir qorishni aytdingiz. Shu jarayonni batafsil tasvirlab bering."
Native-language: "Boshqa novvoy bilan gaplashsangiz, bunday unni nima deb atardingiz?"
TERMS HEARD (original language)                                         FACT
"kuchli un", "bo'sh un", "ko'taradigan un", "suv ichadigan un"
STRUCTURAL
Cover-term: "Unning qanday turlari bor, hammasini sanab bering."
CONTRAST
Dyadic: "'Kuchli un' bilan 'bo'sh un'ning farqi nimada?"
OUTPUT
Category: "un turlari" -> 4 included terms; respondent confirmed 3.        FACT
Contrast used by respondent: how much water the dough takes; whether
  the bread keeps its shape on the tandir wall.                           FACT
Team reading: this baker judges flour by dough behaviour, not by the
  grade printed on the sack. One interview; check with others.   INTERPRETATION
```
Arithmetic check: 5 + 20 = 25; 25 + 15 = 40; 40 + 15 = 55; 55 + 5 = 60. One third of 60 is 20, the descriptive block.

## Common mistakes
- Opening with "why" or with an evaluation ("Is our flour good?") before the routine has been described.
- Replacing the person's term with the team's term in the next question or in the notes.
- Asking what a word means in the abstract, when asking for an example or a typical sentence would show how it is used.
- Running structural and contrast questions back to back until the person tires.
- Presenting the team's grouping of terms as the respondent's own categories without verification questions.

## Quality checklist
- Did the interview open with a grand-tour question about a concrete scene?
- Were mini-tour, example or native-language questions used to follow up the person's own words?
- Is there a term list in the original language, written as spoken?
- Was at least one category checked with a verification or cover-term question?
- Were contrasts asked inside one category, using the person's terms?
- Were the purpose and the reason for unusual questions explained to the respondent?
- Are respondent-confirmed items separated from the team's interpretation?

## Output template
```
ETHNOGRAPHIC QUESTION GUIDE - [project] - [date]
Scene: place / time period / task / people | Respondent type: ... | Length: ... min | Language: ...
Purpose statement to read out: ...
DESCRIPTIVE
Grand tour (typical / specific / guided / task-related): ...
Mini-tour, example and experience prompts: ...
Native-language prompts (direct / hypothetical interaction / typical sentence): ...
TERM LIST (original language): term | said about what | interview no.
STRUCTURAL: cover term | included terms | question used | confirmed by respondent? (y/n)
CONTRAST: items compared | difference named by respondent | question type
Team interpretation (labelled): ... | Open points for next interview: ...
```

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Interview in the respondent's stronger language and write terms exactly as said, in Uzbek or Russian, including mixed speech. Translate only in a separate column.
- A respondent may shift to formal or "correct" language for a visitor from an agency. Hypothetical-interaction questions ("how would you say it to a colleague?") are the tool for this.
- Polite or agreeing habits make verification questions risky: a "yes" may be courtesy. Working rule: verify with a cover-term or sorting question, which needs content, not agreement.
- Tashkent and regional respondents may use different words for the same thing; keep separate term lists until this is checked. People recruited through Telegram are not the older and rural part of the market; say whose vocabulary the list represents.
- Spradley's examples and the textbook chapters opened here come from the United States. Whether long tours and card sorting feel natural to respondents here is a hypothesis to test in the first two interviews.
