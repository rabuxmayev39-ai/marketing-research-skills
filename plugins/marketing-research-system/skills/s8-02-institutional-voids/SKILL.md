# Institutional voids (S8-02)

An institutional void is a gap where a specialist intermediary that helps buyers and sellers find, trust and pay each other is absent or works poorly. The idea comes from Tarun Khanna and Krishna Palepu of Harvard Business School, set out in Harvard Business Review articles (1997, 2005) and the book Winning in Emerging Markets (2010). They define an emerging market by this feature: a place where buyers and sellers cannot easily and efficiently come together. Their list of intermediary types includes information analysers and advisers (for example market research firms, rating agencies), transaction facilitators, credibility enhancers (for example certifiers, auditors), aggregators and distributors, and adjudicators, with government supplying the rule of law around them; the bracketed examples are this skill's own. They ask managers to map these institutions in product, labour and capital markets, set against the country's political and social system and its openness, and then to choose a response. In this skill no void is assumed. Each possible void is a question to check for one category and one place.

Evidence grade: B. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. The framework is a well-established professional and teaching standard built from company cases (the listed limit: it is case-based). Cases show that a response worked for one firm at one time; they do not show how often it works. There is also published criticism. Bothello, Nason and Schnyder (2019) argue that the concept has been stretched until it is unclear what it measures, that it describes non-Western economies mainly by what they lack, and that it treats developed-market institutions as the standard while undervaluing the informal and non-market institutions that do the work locally. This skill takes that criticism on board: for every apparent void, ask what local arrangement fills the job today. Use the output as a structured set of hypotheses for entry and distribution choices, not as a diagnosis of the country.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (a named institution exists or not, with source and date) versus INTERPRETATION (what its absence means for the client). Do not state that a void exists in Uzbekistan without current evidence. When the void map and response options are written, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- A client plans to enter a new region, channel or customer group and the plan copies a model from elsewhere.
- Distribution, payment terms or trust (will the buyer believe the quality claim?) look like the real obstacle, not demand.
- A foreign partner asks why a standard approach needs changing here, or a local client wants to know which gaps it could profitably fill itself.

Do not use it when:
- The decision is about message or creative work only.
- The team wants a general country briefing. Use S0-09 and S8-04 to S8-07.
- The category runs on well-working intermediaries and the client confirms no transaction problems. Record that and move on.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. The business model to be used, written as steps: how the client finds buyers, proves quality, delivers, gets paid, hires and finances.
2. The category and the place (city, region, channel). A void map is specific to these.
3. Current sources (S8-04 to S8-07), sector registers and directories, and the client's own records: bad debts, delivery delays, returns, staff turnover.
4. Two or three people who work in the channel (a distributor, a wholesaler, a store owner) for key-informant interviews (S1-18).

## Procedure
1. Write the business model as a chain of transactions. For each step note which outside party the model relies on: a data provider, a bank, a courier, a certifier, a recruiter, a court.
2. Ask Khanna and Palepu's first question: which market institutions work here and which are missing? Go through four working groups (a simplification of their product, labour and capital market map; Working rule):
   - Information: is there reliable data on market size, buyers, prices and media audiences? Independent reviews or ratings? Credit histories?
   - Credit and payment: can buyers and channel partners get credit? Are electronic payments, instalments and enforceable invoices usable?
   - Logistics and distribution: are there distributors, cold chain, warehousing and delivery services that reach the target places?
   - Trust and enforcement: are there certifiers, standards, warranties and a practical way to settle disputes? How do buyers judge quality today?
3. For each item record three things: the formal intermediary (exists, weak, absent, unknown), the evidence and its date, and the local substitute that does the job now, such as personal networks, prepayment, family reputation, bazaar relationships or messenger groups. A job done by an informal substitute is not empty; note its cost and limits.
4. Ask their second question: which steps of the client's model does each gap hurt? Estimate the cost where possible: working capital tied up, extra staff, lost sales. Mark estimates INTERPRETATION.
5. Ask their third and fourth questions: how could the client gain an advantage by working around the gap, and could it profit by filling the gap for others (for example offering delivery, quality testing or buyer credit as a service)?
6. Choose a response for each damaging gap. The 2005 article gives three broad options: adapt the business model to the context, try to change the context, or stay away. Working rule: add "use a partner who already has the substitute" as a form of adapting, and write the cost and risk of each option.
7. Check the downside. Filling a void yourself (own credit, own fleet) moves risk onto the client. State the worst case in numbers.
8. Plan small tests before commitment, in line with the authors' advice to experiment because the first strategy is rarely right (S7-02).
9. Write the void map, the options and the open questions. Return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. A flour mill near Tashkent wants to sell direct to small bakeries in another region, copying its Tashkent model (phone orders, bank transfer, hired trucks).

```
VOID MAP (category: bakery flour; place: one region)       FACT/INTERPRETATION
Information: no bakery register found (checked two directories)   FACT, dated
  Substitute: local wholesalers know the bakers personally
  Response: own census - 2 people x 10 days x 15 visits = 300 bakeries
Credit: in 20 pilot interviews, 8 bakers said they buy only on
  deferred payment (8 / 20 = 40%)                                 FACT (small n)
  Substitute: wholesalers give 14 days on trust
  If 40% of 300 want it: 120 bakeries x 1.5 t x 4,000,000 soums
  per tonne = 720,000,000 soums owed to the mill at any time      INTERPRETATION
Logistics: hired trucks take loads of 10 t or more; bakers want 1.5 t
  Response options: own small truck, or a local wholesaler partner
Trust: bakers judge flour by a trial bake, not by certificates
OPTIONS: A adapt - partner with one wholesaler who carries the credit
         B adapt - own truck and own 14-day credit (risk: 720 mln soums)
         C stay away for now
TEST: option A with 30 bakeries for 8 weeks (S7-02)
```
Arithmetic check: 2 x 10 x 15 = 300; 8 / 20 = 0.40; 300 x 0.40 = 120; 1.5 x 4,000,000 = 6,000,000 soums per order; 120 x 6,000,000 = 720,000,000.

## Common mistakes
- Declaring a void from a general impression of the country, with no dated evidence for this category and place.
- Seeing only what is missing and not the informal arrangement that does the job.
- Treating every void as an opportunity to fill, without counting the capital and risk taken on.
- Mapping the country once; gaps differ by region, channel and customer size.
- Copying a response from a famous foreign case without checking the conditions.
- Forgetting that intermediaries appear and improve; an old map misleads.
- Skipping the option of staying away or waiting.

## Quality checklist
- Is the business model written as transaction steps with the outside party each relies on?
- Were information, credit, logistics and trust each checked for this category and place?
- Does each gap have evidence with a date, or the label Unknown?
- Is the local substitute recorded for each gap, with its cost and limits?
- Is the damage to the client's model estimated, with estimates marked INTERPRETATION?
- Were adapt, change the context and stay away all considered?
- Is the worst case of filling a void in-house stated in numbers?
- Is a small test planned before full commitment?

## Output template
```
INSTITUTIONAL VOIDS MAP - [client] - [category, place, channel] - [date]
Business model steps: find buyers -> prove quality -> deliver -> get paid -> ...
GROUP        | formal intermediary | status (works / weak / absent / unknown)
             | evidence + date | local substitute | cost to our model
Information  | ...
Credit       | ...
Logistics    | ...
Trust        | ...
RESPONSE per damaging gap: adapt / change context / stay away -> cost, risk
COULD WE FILL IT FOR OTHERS? ...
WORST CASE (numbers): ...   TEST: ...   UNKNOWNS: ...
```

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- No void is asserted here for Uzbekistan. The sources draw on other countries; each gap is a question for current sources and the client.
- Practical caution: ask channel partners how they actually handle credit, delivery and disputes, with a recent example (S3-04), since formal rules and daily practice can differ.
- Check Tashkent and each region separately; treat any transfer between them as a hypothesis.
- Interviews run in Uzbek or Russian; keep the partner's own words for the substitute arrangement in the original language.
- Payment, delivery and data services change fast. Date every entry and recheck a map older than 12 months (Working rule).
