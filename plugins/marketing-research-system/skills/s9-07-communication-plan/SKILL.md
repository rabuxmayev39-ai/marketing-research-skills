# Communication plan (S9-07)

A communication plan is one table that states, for each layer of a campaign, who it is for, what it must achieve, what it says, where and in what form it runs, when, for how much money, and how success will be counted. The usual layers are brand building (wide, memory-building advertising that pays back slowly) and sales activation (narrower messages to people ready to buy now, which pay back quickly). The plan has no single author. It is an agency convention from the practice of account planning: the UK Account Planning Group (APG) describes planners as the people who agree campaign aims with the client, build the strategy, write the brief, decide which media reach the audience and how they fit together, and monitor effectiveness; the plan is where those decisions are written down for the teams who will make and place the work.

Evidence grade: C. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. No study was found, among the sources opened for this skill, that tests whether campaigns with such a table do better than campaigns without one. Parts of its content rest on stronger ground: the brand and activation layers come from Binet and Field's analyses of IPA case studies (S6-05), and an IPA guide on briefing treats objectives, audience and budget as one linked backbone. Its limits: the plan is only as good as the strategy behind it; a fully filled table can hide a missing strategy; and formats differ from agency to agency, so the columns here are a convention and not a standard. Treat the plan as a coordination and checking tool. Do not present a completed plan as evidence that the campaign will work.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (budget, dates, baselines, prices quoted by media sellers) versus INTERPRETATION (expected reach, expected response, why a channel was chosen). Do not fill a cell by guessing; write "open" and name who will supply it. When the plan is checked and handed over, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- The marketing and communication strategy are approved and work must pass to creative, media or the client's own team.
- Several channels or teams are involved and nobody holds the whole picture.
- A running campaign needs a review: which rows are spending money without an objective or a measure.

Do not use it when:
- There is no agreed strategy, target or message. The table would only list activities; go back to S6-08, S9-01 and S9-02.
- The task is a single creative assignment. Use the creative brief (S6-18); the plan sits above it.
- The client wants a content calendar only. Say that a calendar is the timing column, not a plan.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. The approved strategy: target, positioning, growth logic (S6-01, S6-02, S6-03).
2. Communication objectives with baselines (S9-01) and the objective and KPI hierarchy (S7-01).
3. The message for each layer (S9-02) and the creative brief if written (S6-18).
4. The total budget and how it was set (S9-05), and the planned split between layers (S6-05).
5. Real prices and reach figures from media sellers or past campaigns (S8-08), and the dates that matter: season, launches, holidays, production lead times.

## Procedure
1. Check the inputs. If any of target, objective, message or budget is missing, stop and list what is missing. The IPA briefing guide reports that 60% of marketers use the creative process to clarify strategy that should have been settled before; the plan must not become that process.
2. Set the rows. Start with two layers, brand building and sales activation. In Binet and Field's summary, brand building means broad reach, emotional associations, slower effects and bigger paybacks; activation means narrower reach, rational product and price messages, earlier effects and smaller paybacks. Add a row only when the audience or the task really differs (for example trade buyers in B2B, S6-17). Working rule: more than five rows usually means the plan is a list of channels, not of tasks.
3. Fill the audience for each row. Their analysis found that campaigns aimed at the whole market produced the most business effects, so the brand row normally addresses all buyers of the category, and the activation row addresses those in the market now (S6-06). Write who is excluded and why.
4. Write one objective per row, tied to a level of the KPI hierarchy (S7-01), with baseline, target and date. The IPA briefing guide asks for linked commercial, behavioural and attitudinal objectives with benchmarks and a timeframe.
5. Write one message per row with its proof. Both rows must be recognisably the same brand: same name treatment, colours and other distinctive assets (S6-04).
6. Choose channels and formats by the job, reach first (S9-03). For each channel write its role in one line (reach, reminder near purchase, response). Working rule: a channel with no role and no measure is removed.
7. Set the timing on a week-by-week calendar. Working rule: brand activity runs as continuously as the budget allows; activation is placed around the moments when people buy; the pre-test (S9-06) and the baseline measurement (S7-03) are booked before the first media date.
8. Allocate the budget so that the rows add up to the total, with production, testing and measurement shown as their own lines. Compare the media split with the starting point from S6-05 (the IPA presentation gives about 60% brand and 40% activation as the optimum in its data) and write the reason for any departure. Working rule: divide each row's money by the number of people it should reach; if the cost per person reached is far from what past campaigns paid, the objective, the audience or the budget must change. The IPA guide makes the same point: change one of the three and the others must be adjusted.
9. Give each row one main KPI, its data source, its owner and the method that will read it: a controlled test (S7-02) or before and after tracking (S7-03). Activity counts such as impressions go in a separate column and are never the KPI of a row.
10. Run the consistency checks in the checklist below, then hand over in a meeting, not only by file. The IPA guide advises briefing through conversation until the receiving team confirms it understands. Record open cells and owners. Return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. A juice brand plans a 12-week summer campaign with a total budget of 1,200,000,000 soum. Strategy input: grow the number of buying households; message from Category Entry Points: "the cold drink for the family table".

```
ROW 1 BRAND BUILDING                                       690,000,000 soum
Audience: all adults 18-45 who buy soft drinks, Tashkent and three regions
Objective: prompted awareness among non-buyers 38% -> 46% by September  (brand level)
Message: the cold drink for the family table | proof: 100% juice, no added sugar
Channels: online video 420,000,000 (reach) + outdoor 270,000,000 (reminder)
Timing: weeks 1-12 continuous | KPI: awareness among non-buyers, survey (S7-03)
Planned reach: 2,000,000 people -> 345 soum per person reached      INTERPRETATION
ROW 2 SALES ACTIVATION                                     450,000,000 soum
Audience: shoppers in 300 partner stores and people near them
Objective: buying households 210,000 -> 240,000 by September      (behaviour level)
Message: 1-litre family pack, try it this weekend | proof: price on shelf
Channels: in-store displays 290,000,000 + targeted social ads 160,000,000
Timing: weeks 3-5 and 8-10 | KPI: sales in test vs control stores (S7-02)
OTHER: production in rows above; pre-test and measurement 60,000,000 soum
TOTAL 1,200,000,000 | media split brand 60.5% : activation 39.5%
Open cells: outdoor prices for regions (media agency, by Friday)
```
Arithmetic check: 420 + 270 = 690; 290 + 160 = 450; 690 + 450 + 60 = 1,200 (million soum); 690 / (690 + 450) = 690 / 1,140 = 0.605; 450 / 1,140 = 0.395; 690,000,000 / 2,000,000 = 345; 46 - 38 = 8 points; 240,000 - 210,000 = 30,000 households; 60 / 1,200 = 5% for testing and measurement.

## Common mistakes
- Writing the plan before the strategy, so the table is a list of channels with objectives added afterwards.
- One row per channel instead of one row per task, or the same objective and KPI copied into every row.
- Impressions, views or followers written as the KPI.
- Rows that do not add up to the budget, or no money left for production, testing and measurement.
- Narrow targeting in the brand row because narrow audiences look cheaper per click.
- No baseline, so the target cannot be checked later.
- Sending the table by email with no conversation.

## Quality checklist
- Is there an approved strategy, target and message behind every row?
- Does each row have one audience, one objective with baseline, target and date, and one message?
- Does every channel have a stated role, and has any channel without one been removed?
- Do the budget lines add up to the total, including production, testing and measurement?
- Is the split between brand and activation stated and justified against S6-05?
- Does each row have one main KPI that is not an activity count, with a data source, an owner and a reading method?
- Are the pre-test and the baseline measurement dated before the first media date?
- Was the plan handed over in a meeting, with open cells and owners recorded?

## Output template
```
COMMUNICATION PLAN - [client / campaign] - [dates] - version [n]
Strategy in one line: ... | Total budget: ... | Set by (S9-05): ...
ROW [n]: [brand building / sales activation / other task]
Audience: ... | excluded: ...
Objective: [measure] from [baseline] to [target] by [date] | level (S7-01): ...
Message: ... | proof: ...
Channel -> role -> format -> budget: ... | ... | ...
Timing (weeks): ...
Main KPI: ... | source: ... | owner: ... | method: S7-02 / S7-03
Activity counts (not KPIs): reach ... frequency ... cost per person reached ...
OTHER LINES: production ... | pre-test (S9-06) ... | measurement ...
CHECKS: rows add to total yes/no | split brand:activation ..:.. | reason: ...
FACT / INTERPRETATION marked: yes/no | OPEN CELLS: [cell] -> [owner] -> [date]
```

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Write the plan in the language the receiving teams work in; if creative and media teams use different languages, keep one master version and translate it with a check (S3-03). State for each row whether the creative is in Uzbek, Russian or both.
- Practical caution: published reach and audience figures for local channels may be missing or come from the seller. Mark seller figures as such, and treat planned reach as INTERPRETATION until a campaign of your own confirms it (S8-08).
- Plan Tashkent and the regions as separate lines when channels, prices or languages differ; do not assume a Tashkent media mix transfers.
- Hypothesis to check for each category: buying moments may cluster around holidays and seasons, which would shift activation timing. Confirm with the client's own sales calendar, not from assumption.
- The 60:40 starting point and the whole-market finding come from UK award case studies; treat them as hypotheses here and record your own results. Polite agreement in a hand-over meeting is not confirmation; ask the receiving team to restate each row in their own words.
