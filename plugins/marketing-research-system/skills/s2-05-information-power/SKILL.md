# Information power (S2-05)

Information power is a way of reasoning about qualitative sample size proposed by Kirsti Malterud, Volkert Siersma and Ann Dorrit Guassora of the University of Copenhagen in Qualitative Health Research (2016). Its core statement is that the more information relevant to the study a sample holds, the fewer participants are needed. The authors name five things that decide this: the aim of the study, the specificity of the sample, the use of established theory, the quality of the dialogue, and the analysis strategy. They offered it as an alternative to saturation, which they describe as tied to one methodology and applied inconsistently, and they present a model for using the five items both when planning and again during data collection.

Evidence grade: A. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. The model is published in a peer-reviewed journal and is discussed in later methods papers, so it carries weight as an accepted line of argument. It is a conceptual model, though: the sources opened for this skill do not report an empirical test that links ratings to a correct number, and the authors reject formulas. It is a reasoning tool, not a number. Any figure in this skill is this agency's working rule and must be labelled so.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (features of the study and of the interviews done so far) versus INTERPRETATION (the rating of each item and the resulting judgement). Never present a number as "calculated by information power". When the appraisal is delivered, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- Planning an interview study and the team needs a reasoned starting number before any data exist.
- The sample will be small (experts, B2B buyers, rare customers) and the client asks whether so few can be enough.
- The design is case-focused or interpretive, where counting new themes (S2-04) does not fit.
- Fieldwork is under way and the first interviews are weaker or richer than expected.

Do not use it when:
- The client needs shares or frequencies. Use S1-08 with S2-03.
- The team wants a formula to hide a budget decision. If the number is set by money or time, say so; a later methods paper asks that such practical limits be reported as limitations.
- The study covers several different segments with one broad question. Split it first (S2-01, S2-06), then appraise each part.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. The aim and research questions as written in the brief (S2-01).
2. The definition of who will be interviewed and how they will be recruited (S2-02, S2-06).
3. Any theory or framework the study will use (for example S1-07, S1-15, S4-09, S5-07), or a note that there is none.
4. Who will interview, their experience, the channel (in person, call), language and planned length (S1-04).
5. The planned analysis: in-depth reading of a few cases, or comparison across many cases (S4-01, S4-05, S1-19).

## Procedure
1. Explain the logic to the user in one sentence: the sample is judged by how much relevant information each interview is likely to carry, not by a standard count.
2. Rate item 1, aim. A narrow, focused aim gives higher information power; a broad aim, or one that compares subgroups, needs more participants. Ask: "Can the aim be said in one sentence with one group and one behaviour?" Record the evidence and the rating: higher, mixed or lower.
3. Rate item 2, sample specificity. Participants who hold experience highly specific to the aim (described as dense specificity) need to be fewer than a loosely matching, varied group (sparse specificity). Ask: "Has every planned respondent personally done or decided the thing we study, recently?"
4. Rate item 3, established theory. A study that applies an existing theory or framework to design questions and read answers needs fewer participants than one that starts with none or aims to build theory. Name the framework or write "none".
5. Rate item 4, quality of dialogue. Clear, trusting, well-probed conversation yields more per interview; vague or guarded dialogue yields less. Before fieldwork this is a forecast from the interviewer's experience, the topic's sensitivity, the channel and the time available. It must be re-rated on real transcripts.
6. Rate item 5, analysis strategy. In-depth analysis of a few informants or cases needs fewer participants than an exploratory cross-case analysis that looks for patterns across people.
7. Write the appraisal as a short argument, item by item, not as a score. A critical paper by Sim and colleagues points out that the items may interact (for example, strong theory does not rescue poor dialogue), so read them together.
8. Turn the appraisal into a provisional number. Working rule of this agency, not from Malterud and colleagues: take the published saturation range for a homogeneous segment from S2-04 (9 to 17 interviews) as the reference band. Mostly higher ratings: plan at or below the lower end and review early. Mixed: plan mid-range. Mostly lower: plan the upper end or beyond, or better, narrow the aim or tighten the sample so the ratings improve. Always give a starting number, a review point and a provisional maximum.
9. Improve power before adding people. Each item is also a lever: narrow the aim, recruit more specific respondents, adopt a framework, train or change the interviewer, lengthen the conversation, or focus the analysis. State which levers were used.
10. Reappraise during fieldwork. The authors' model applies during data collection as well, and the opened sources report that they expect sample size to be revisited. After the first three to five interviews (working rule), re-rate dialogue quality and specificity from the transcripts using the quality signs in S1-04 (short questions, long specific answers). Raise or lower the provisional number and record why. A later methods paper recommends making the final decision by team consensus.
11. Use it with saturation, not against it. Information power helps before data exist; a saturation check (S2-04) helps once coding has started. Where both are possible, report both.
12. Write the justification for the report: the five ratings with evidence, the changes made during fieldwork, the final number, practical constraints, and who was not covered. Return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. Input: a furniture maker wants to know why restaurant owners who requested a quotation in the last six months did not place an order. The client list has 40 such owners.

```
INFORMATION POWER APPRAISAL
1 Aim: one group, one event (quote requested, no order).            -> higher
2 Specificity: all had the exact experience, within 6 months.       -> higher
3 Theory: forces of progress (S4-09) guides guide and analysis.     -> higher
4 Dialogue: experienced interviewer, but busy owners, phone,
   30 minutes planned.                                              -> mixed (forecast)
5 Analysis: themes compared across all owners (cross-case).         -> lower
Tally: 3 higher, 1 mixed, 1 lower = 5 items.                        INTERPRETATION
Provisional plan (agency working rule): start 8, review after 5, maximum 12.
  8 of 40 listed owners = 20% of the list.
REVIEW AFTER 5 INTERVIEWS
Average length 18 minutes against 30 planned; answers general.      FACT
Item 4 re-rated lower -> tally 3 higher, 0 mixed, 2 lower.
Levers used: switch to in-person visits at the restaurant; ask for the
  story of the last purchase from another supplier.
Number changed: 8 + 3 = 11 (within maximum 12).                     INTERPRETATION
```
Arithmetic check: 3 + 1 + 1 = 5 items; 8 / 40 = 0.20; after re-rating 3 + 0 + 2 = 5; 8 + 3 = 11, and 11 is not above 12. The 20% is a share of the client's list, not of the market.

## Common mistakes
- Quoting information power as the source of a number ("information power says 8").
- Rating every item "higher" to defend a sample that was fixed by budget.
- Rating dialogue quality before fieldwork and never checking it on transcripts.
- Calling a mixed convenience group "specific" because everyone is a customer.
- Claiming theory use when the framework appears only in the report's introduction.
- Using the model for a broad, multi-segment study without splitting it.
- Dropping the saturation check where coding as you go was possible.

## Quality checklist
- Is each of the five items rated with written evidence, not just a label?
- Is the number presented as a provisional judgement with a review point and a maximum?
- Are any numeric bands marked as the agency's working rule?
- Were levers to raise information power considered before adding interviews?
- Was dialogue quality re-rated from real transcripts during fieldwork?
- Are budget or time limits that shaped the sample stated openly?
- Does the report say who was not covered and whether saturation was also checked?

- S2-04 qualitative sample size and saturation: gives the empirical ranges and the in-field check; this skill gives the reasoning before data exist.
- S2-06 purposive sampling strategies and S2-02 sampling: raise sample specificity. S2-01 research brief: narrows the aim.
- S1-04 in-depth interviews, S3-04 story-based guides, S3-11 interview snapshot: raise and monitor dialogue quality.
- S1-07 JTBD switch interviews, S1-15 laddering, S1-16 critical incident technique, S4-09 forces of progress, S5-07 COM-B: frameworks that count as established theory when really used.
- S1-18 B2B key-informant interviews and S1-19 case study research: typical small, case-focused designs. S2-08 trustworthiness criteria and S2-09 qualitative literacy indicators: where the justification is reported.

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Dialogue quality is the item most at risk. Polite or agreeing answers and reluctance to criticise in front of a guest lower the information in each interview; rate this item from transcripts, in the original Uzbek or Russian, not from the interviewer's impression.
- Let respondents choose the language; an interview held in the respondent's weaker language has lower dialogue quality. Keep quotes in the original language.
- A sample recruited through the client or through referrals can look highly specific and still be narrow. Record how each person was reached, and check whether Tashkent and regional respondents answer one aim or two.
- Recruiting through Telegram misses older and rural people; if they matter to the aim, specificity is lower than it looks.
- The model was developed by health researchers at the University of Copenhagen, and the frameworks counted as established theory were mostly tested on Western samples. Treat their fit here as a hypothesis, which weakens item 3.
