# B2B growth principles (S6-17)

The B2B growth principles are five rules for marketing to business buyers, set out by Les Binet and Peter Field in The 5 Principles of Growth in B2B Marketing (2019), a report commissioned by the LinkedIn B2B Institute. The five are: invest in share of voice; balance brand building and sales activation; expand the customer base; maximise mental availability (how easily the company comes to mind when a need arises); and use emotion for long-term effects. The authors took the business-to-business cases out of the IPA Databank, a UK collection of advertising effectiveness case studies, and asked whether the rules already found for consumer brands hold there. Their answer was that B2B works much like consumer marketing, with a somewhat different budget balance.

Evidence grade: B. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. The report is widely used in the profession, but it is not peer-reviewed and its base is thin. The authors themselves say there were fewer than 50 B2B cases among almost 1,500 campaigns, and call the findings tentative and the estimates rough. Further limits stated in the report or visible from it: every case was an entry to an effectiveness award, so successful campaigns are over-represented; most cases are from the UK; budgets tend to be large; and the sponsor sells B2B advertising. The numbers show associations in past cases, not proven cause. Treat each number as a starting point for discussion, never as a target.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (the client's spend, share and customer counts) versus INTERPRETATION (what the principles suggest). Always show the caveat next to any number taken from the report. When the strategy outline is delivered, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- A B2B client (supplier, manufacturer, distributor, business service) needs a communication strategy for a year or longer.
- The client spends almost everything on short-term lead generation and asks why growth has stalled.
- A budget must be set or split between long-term and short-term work.

Do not use it when:
- The client sells to a handful of known accounts through personal relationships or tenders. Reach and share of voice mean little there; use S1-18 and account planning.
- A single short campaign must be judged; the principles concern periods longer than six months.
- No estimate of the category's size or competitors' activity can be made at all. Then do S4-23 and S4-22 first.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. The client's sales and an estimate of market share (S4-23), with the source of each figure.
2. The client's marketing spend by activity for the last 12 months.
3. A rough picture of competitors' communication (S4-22): who advertises, where, how much.
4. Customer counts: number of active customers, new customers per year, share of sales from the largest customers.
5. Who takes part in the buying decision (S1-18) and how long a purchase takes.
6. What is measured today and over what period.

## Procedure
1. Check fit. Count the possible buyers. Working rule: if the whole market is under about 100 companies that the sales team can name, treat this skill as background and plan by account. Otherwise continue.
2. Principle 1, share of voice. Share of voice (SOV) is the client's share of all advertising spend in the category; share of market (SOM) is its share of sales. Extra share of voice is ESOV = SOV - SOM. The report finds that brands with SOV above SOM tend to grow, and estimates that 10 points of ESOV go with about 0.7 points of market share growth a year in B2B (0.6 in consumer markets). Estimate SOV as well as the data allow (S4-22), state the estimate's weakness, and compute the implied growth and the spend needed for the client's growth goal (S9-05). The rule holds only with other things equal, so present the output as a range. Working rule: show the result at 0.5 times and 1.5 times the published rate.
3. Principle 2, brand and activation. Brand building is broad, memorable communication that makes future sales easier; activation is targeted communication that converts people who are ready to buy now. The report's rough optimum for B2B is about 46% brand and 54% activation, against about 60 to 40 for consumer brands, and the authors warn that the split varies by category and should not be followed exactly. Classify the client's current spend into the two groups, compare, and propose a direction of change (S6-05). Brand effects become the main sales driver only after about six months, so agree a measurement period of at least a year.
4. Principle 3, expand the customer base. In the report's B2B cases, campaigns aimed at winning new customers produced large business effects more often than loyalty campaigns, and campaigns aimed at broad reach did best. Check where growth has come from (S0-05) and whether communication reaches companies that are not yet customers. Plan two layers: brand communication for the whole category including future buyers (S6-06), and activation for those buying now.
5. Principle 4, mental availability. Campaigns that set out to make the company famous in its category had the largest effects in the report, ahead of awareness and other goals. Define the buying situations in which the client should come to mind (S1-21), measure the starting point (S4-19), and make sure the brand is easy to recognise (S6-04).
6. Principle 5, emotion. Emotional campaigns built long-term effects in the report's cases and rational messages worked better for short-term activation. Brief brand work to create a feeling about the company (S9-04) and keep product facts, prices and terms for activation.
7. Write the strategy outline: budget level with its range, split, audiences for each layer, message roles, measures and time frame (S9-07, S7-01).
8. State the limits beside the plan: fewer than 50 cases, award entries, mostly UK, large budgets, commercial sponsor.
9. Deliver the outline. Return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. Input: a flour mill in Uzbekistan sells to bakeries and wants to add one point of market share a year. All figures are invented, in million soum.

```
INPUTS                                                         FACT (invented)
Category advertising spend, estimate: 2,000 | Mill's spend: 300
SOV = 300 / 2,000 = 15% | SOM = 9% | ESOV = 15 - 9 = +6 points
IMPLIED GROWTH                                                  INTERPRETATION
Published rate: 6 / 10 x 0.7 = 0.42 points a year (9% -> 9.42%)
Range: at half the rate 0.21 points; at 1.5 times the rate 0.63 points
SPEND FOR +1 POINT A YEAR
ESOV needed = 1 / 0.7 x 10 = 14.3 points -> SOV needed = 9 + 14.3 = 23.3%
Others spend 2,000 - 300 = 1,700. Spend x where x / (1,700 + x) = 0.233:
  x = 0.233 x 1,700 / 0.767 = 516 (rounded), an increase of 216
SPLIT OF THE CURRENT 300
Now: brand 45 (15%), activation 255 (85%)
Guide 46 / 54: brand 138, activation 162 -> move about 93 toward brand
CAVEAT: rates come from fewer than 50 award cases, mostly UK. The category
spend figure is the weakest input; improve it before deciding (S4-22).
```
Arithmetic check: 300 / 2,000 = 0.15; 15 - 9 = 6; 0.6 x 0.7 = 0.42; 0.42 x 0.5 = 0.21; 0.42 x 1.5 = 0.63; 10 / 0.7 = 14.29; 9 + 14.3 = 23.3; 0.233 x 1,700 = 396.1; 396.1 / 0.767 = 516.4; 516.4 / (1,700 + 516.4) = 0.233; 516 - 300 = 216; 45 / 300 = 0.15; 0.46 x 300 = 138; 300 - 138 = 162; 138 - 45 = 93.

## Common mistakes
- Presenting 46 to 54 or 0.7 as laws; the authors call them rough guides.
- Reading the share of voice link as proof that spend causes growth in every case.
- Counting only the client's own customer list as the audience and never reaching future buyers.
- Cutting activation to zero; the principle is balance.
- Judging brand communication on leads in its first month.
- Applying the rules to a market of a few named accounts.
- Hiding that the share of voice figure is a rough estimate.

## Quality checklist
- Was the fit check on market size done and recorded?
- Are SOV and SOM shown with their sources and weaknesses?
- Is implied growth given as a range and not as a single figure?
- Is current spend classified into brand and activation with the rule used?
- Does the plan reach non-customers as well as customers?
- Is the measurement period at least a year, with measures named?
- Are the report's limits written next to the numbers?

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Published advertising spend data by B2B category may not exist. Expect to estimate share of voice by counting competitors' visible activity (S4-21) and say plainly that it is an estimate.
- Many B2B sales may run through personal contact, dealers and messengers such as Telegram; where that is so, count sales staff and trade events as activation when classifying spend. Verify with the client; do not assume.
- The report's cases are mostly British with large budgets. For a small local supplier every number is a hypothesis; the agency's own cases (S8-08) should replace them over time.
- Reach among business buyers in the regions may need different channels from Tashkent; ask buyers where they notice suppliers.
- Prepare materials in Uzbek and Russian and keep customer quotes in the original language.
