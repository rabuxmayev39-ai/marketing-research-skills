# Reach-first media planning (S9-03)

Reach is the share of a defined group of people who are exposed to a campaign at least once in a period; frequency is how many times each of them is exposed. Reach-first planning spends the brand budget to reach as many buyers of the category as possible, including light buyers (people who buy the brand rarely) and non-buyers, and only then adds repeat exposures or narrow targeting. The principle comes from Byron Sharp's How Brands Grow (2010) and the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute, whose buying data show that most of a brand's customers are light buyers and that growth comes mainly from gaining more buyers. Les Binet and Peter Field's Media in Focus (IPA, 2017) reached a matching conclusion from campaign case data: scale of reach is the main driver of effectiveness, and tight targeting helps short-term efficiency but not market share.

Evidence grade: A. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. The buyer-behaviour patterns behind the method (many light buyers, growth through penetration) have been reported repeatedly in journals, including long-run panel studies the Institute cites; those papers were not opened for this skill. The grade applies to that pattern, not to every planning step below. Honest limits: the campaign evidence is IPA awards data, which is self-reported and skewed to large UK brands; Media in Focus was produced in association with Google and Thinkbox, and Thinkbox promotes television; Sharp himself calls awards data a weird data set while agreeing on reach. An Institute-hosted article adds that targeting is not obsolete, especially close to purchase. The published work concerns mostly large consumer brands, so for a small local budget the method is a direction, not a formula. Reach data in Uzbekistan are weak: every reach figure must be marked ESTIMATE with its source.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (prices paid, platform-reported figures, named as such) versus INTERPRETATION (overlap assumptions, combined reach). Always show reach as a range. When the channel plan is written, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- A brand-building budget must be split across channels.
- A plan is built on narrow audiences or high frequency and nobody has asked how many category buyers it reaches.
- Two channel mixes must be compared on one measure.

Do not use it when:
- The task is the activation layer: retargeting, search, offers to people already in the market. Tight targeting is appropriate there (S6-05).
- The product cannot be bought where the advertising runs. Fix distribution first (S6-03).
- The budget has not been sized. Do S9-05 first, or run both together.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. The category buyer definition and an estimate of how many there are in the chosen area (S4-23).
2. The budget for the brand layer and the period (S6-05, S9-05).
3. Candidate channels with, for each: estimated reach among category buyers, cost, and where the figure comes from (S8-04, platform planners, media owners, the client's past campaigns).
4. Where the product is actually available (cities, shops, delivery zones).
5. The communication objectives and the creative formats planned (S9-01, S9-02).

## Procedure
1. Define the audience as all buyers of the category in the area served, not as a persona. Sharp's argument on the Institute's site is that the lightest buyers are by far the largest group and that ignoring light category buyers may raise return on investment yet stops a brand from moving to a higher share. Ask the user: "Who buys this category at all, and where can they buy us?"
2. Exclude only people who cannot buy: outside the distribution area, outside the category (for example no children for baby food). Working rule: every other exclusion must be justified in writing.
3. List candidate channels and record for each an estimated reach among category buyers, the cost, and the source of the estimate. Working rule: platform "reach" counts accounts or devices, not people, and media owners' figures are sales claims; record both as ESTIMATE and cut them to a cautious low and high.
4. Start with the channel that reaches the largest share of category buyers per unit of money. An Institute-hosted article advises including the medium the audience uses most, and treats niche media as an addition to broad channels, since small media alone cannot cover the group.
5. Add channels by incremental reach: for each candidate, estimate how many new people it adds to those already reached, and divide its cost by the added reach points. Take the cheapest addition first. Working rule for overlap when no data exist: assume channels overlap at random, so combined reach = 1 - (1 - a) x (1 - b); state that real overlap is usually higher among heavy media users, and show a lower figure as the cautious case.
6. Stop adding frequency early. Working rule: cap frequency per person where the platform allows, and move the money saved to people not yet reached. Do not set a required frequency such as three exposures unless there is evidence for this category.
7. Spread reach over time. The Institute's planning review asks whether a plan delivers reach across time as well as across buyers. Sharp's advice in a 2022 interview was to stay on air continuously, about a twelfth of the yearly budget each month adjusted for season, instead of short bursts. Working rule: if the budget cannot cover every month, cover the months when the category is bought.
8. Use targeting only where it widens or protects reach: language versions, places where the product is sold, groups the main channels miss. Sharp's wording is that targeting should be used sparingly, where it helps expand reach.
9. Small budgets. Working rule: when the budget cannot reach a meaningful share of all category buyers nationally, narrow the geography, not the people: reach nearly all category buyers in one city or district where the product is on sale. Sharp's point that low reach is not a recipe for growth or even for holding share applies, but the local scale is this skill's adaptation.
10. Write the plan with combined reach (low and high), cost per reached person, monthly spread and all assumptions. Plan one check of real reach (S7-03 or a short survey question on ad recall by channel, read with caution). Return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. A juice brand sold in Tashkent city only. Category buyers in the city: 1,000,000 adults (ESTIMATE). Brand-layer budget: 600 million soums for six months. All reach figures are invented estimates of the share of category buyers reached at least once.

```
Channel               Reach   Cost (m soums)   Source of estimate
Instagram/Facebook     45%        180          platform planner (accounts, not people)
Telegram channel ads   35%        150          channel owners' view counts
Outdoor boards         40%        240          media owner's claim
Local TV               50%        420          media owner's claim
STEP-BY-STEP (random-overlap working rule)
1 Instagram/Facebook                 45.0%                 180
2 + Telegram   1 - 0.55 x 0.65     = 64.25%  (+19.25 pts)   150 -> 7.8 m per point
3 + Outdoor    1 - 0.3575 x 0.60   = 78.55%  (+14.30 pts)   240 -> 16.8 m per point
  or + TV      1 - 0.3575 x 0.50   = 82.13%  (+17.88 pts)   420 -> 23.5 m per point,
               total 750 m: over budget
PLAN A (reach-first): three channels, 570 m, reach about 785,500 people,
  about 726 soums per person reached; 30 m kept in reserve
PLAN B (narrow, high frequency): 570 m on one tightly targeted audience,
  20% reach = 200,000 people at high frequency, 2,850 soums per person reached
CHOICE: A. Cautious case with heavier overlap: report reach as 65% to 79%.  INTERPRETATION
```
Arithmetic check: 0.55 x 0.65 = 0.3575; 1 - 0.3575 = 0.6425; 64.25 - 45 = 19.25; 150 / 19.25 = 7.8; 0.3575 x 0.60 = 0.2145; 1 - 0.2145 = 0.7855; 78.55 - 64.25 = 14.30; 240 / 14.30 = 16.8; 0.3575 x 0.50 = 0.17875; 1 - 0.17875 = 0.82125; 82.125 - 64.25 = 17.875; 420 / 17.875 = 23.5; 180 + 150 + 240 = 570; 180 + 150 + 420 = 750; 600 - 570 = 30; 0.7855 x 1,000,000 = 785,500; 570,000,000 / 785,500 = 726; 570,000,000 / 200,000 = 2,850. The 65% lower bound is a judgement, not a calculation.

## Common mistakes
- Defining the audience as a narrow persona and paying for the same people many times.
- Comparing channels by cost per thousand impressions instead of cost per person reached.
- Adding up channel reach figures as if there were no overlap.
- Presenting platform or media-owner figures as facts about people.
- Spending the whole budget in one two-week burst.
- Advertising in regions where the product is not on the shelf.
- Applying reach-first to retargeting and offers, where narrow targeting is the point.
- Spreading a small budget thinly over the whole country.

## Quality checklist
- Is the audience all category buyers in the area served, with a size estimate?
- Is every exclusion justified?
- Does each channel have a reach estimate, a cost and a named source?
- Was combined reach calculated with a stated overlap assumption and shown as a range?
- Is cost per reached person shown for each option?
- Is spending spread over the buying months?
- Is the activation layer planned separately?
- Is a check of real reach planned?

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Independent audience measurement may be missing or not available to a small agency. Practical caution: ask each media owner how the figure was counted and write the answer next to the number.
- Telegram view counts and platform reach figures count views or accounts. Treat them as upper limits for people.
- Tashkent and the regions may differ in channels used and in where the product is sold. Plan each area separately and treat transfers as hypotheses.
- Plan Uzbek and Russian versions as a way to widen reach; this is targeting that adds people, which fits the method.
- Very small budgets: keep the plan local, as in step 9, and say so to the client.
- The evidence comes from Western markets and large brands; treat expected effects here as hypotheses and measure.
