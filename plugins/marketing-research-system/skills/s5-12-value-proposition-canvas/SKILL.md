# Value proposition canvas (S5-12)

The value proposition canvas (VPC) is a two-part chart. The right side, the customer profile, describes one customer segment: the jobs they are trying to get done, the pains they meet, and the gains they want. The left side, the value map, describes one offer: its products and services, how they relieve pains, and how they create gains. "Fit" is the match between the two sides. Alexander Osterwalder created it with Yves Pigneur and Alan Smith as a zoom-in on two blocks of the Business Model Canvas (Value Proposition and Customer Segment); it is set out in the book Value Proposition Design (Osterwalder, Pigneur, Bernarda, Smith, 2014) and on strategyzer.com.

Evidence grade: C. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. The canvas is a widely used design aid, not a validated instrument. A filled canvas is a set of hypotheses. Strategyzer itself says fit is "a claim until customers confirm it". Put weight on the customer evidence behind each note, not on the neatness of the chart.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Mark every note on the canvas as FACT (seen in research data) or INTERPRETATION (team's or your assumption). When done, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- Research on a defined segment is done and the team must design or sharpen an offer for it.
- An existing offer underperforms and the team needs to see which customer pains and gains it misses.
- Sales or marketing staff describe the product by features and need to restate it in customer terms.
- Preparing hypotheses and questions for the next round of interviews.

Do not use it when:
- The segment is not defined. Do S5-09 first. One canvas covers one segment.
- The task is to choose which problem to work on among many (use S5-11) or to find the insight itself (S5-01 to S5-03).
- The client needs a viable business model check (costs, channels, revenue). The canvas covers only two blocks of the Business Model Canvas; say so.
- There is no customer data at all and no plan to collect any. A canvas made only from internal opinion should be labelled as such and treated as an interview guide.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. One named customer segment (if payer and user differ, treat them as two segments).
2. Customer evidence: interview transcripts or notes, observations, open survey answers, sales conversations. Note the volume and date.
3. A description of the current or planned offer: products, services, terms, price, delivery, support.
4. Optional: main competing alternatives the customer uses today, including "do nothing" or home-made workarounds.

If evidence is thin, proceed but mark the canvas DRAFT - ASSUMPTIONS and list what must be checked.

## Procedure
1. Fix the segment and the context. Ask: "Which customer group is this canvas for, and in what situation are they buying or using?" Write it at the top. If the user names several groups, open one canvas per group and start with the one most important to the client.
2. Build the customer profile first, with the offer set aside. Fill this side only with things that can be observed about customers and that the client does not control. Do not look at the product while doing this; otherwise the team lists only the jobs its product already serves.
3. List customer jobs. A job is what the customer is trying to get done in their work or life. Label each as:
   - Functional: a task or problem to solve.
   - Social: how they want to be seen by others (status, reputation).
   - Emotional: how they want to feel (secure, proud, calm).
   Push beyond functional jobs; they are the most visible, and teams tend to stop there. Use "why?" several times on each job to reach the deeper driver. Attach the source and a short quote in the original language to every job that comes from data.
4. List pains. Pains are what annoys or blocks the customer before, during and after the job: unwanted costs, lost time, frustration, poor performance of current solutions, risks they fear, barriers to trying something new. Make each pain concrete ("waits 3 days for delivery", not "slow service"). Rate intensity (high / medium / low) and note how often it occurs.
5. List gains. Gains are the results and benefits the customer expects, wants or would be pleasantly surprised by: savings, better results, easier work, positive feelings, social approval, their own measures of success. A gain is not simply the opposite of a pain; if it is, keep only one of the two. Rate relevance (high / medium / low) and frequency.
6. Rank. Order jobs by importance to the customer, pains by intensity, gains by relevance. Use counts across interviews as a rough guide and mark the ranking INTERPRETATION unless a survey supports it. Ask the user to confirm the top three in each box.
7. Build the value map. Now bring in the offer:
   - Products and services: list what is offered (physical, digital, intangible, financial) and rank by importance to this segment.
   - Pain relievers: for each, state exactly how it reduces or removes a specific listed pain.
   - Gain creators: for each, state exactly how it produces a specific listed gain.
   Write these as mechanisms ("delivery slot confirmed by 18:00 the day before"), not slogans ("reliable").
8. Check fit line by line. Make a table: each top-ranked job, pain and gain on the left; the reliever or creator that addresses it on the right. Mark each row:
   - Addressed, with evidence that customers value it (FACT).
   - Addressed on paper only (INTERPRETATION - to test).
   - Not addressed (gap).
   Also list value-map items that match nothing in the profile (waste or unproven).
9. Decide focus. Do not try to cover every pain and gain. Choose the few high-ranked ones the offer will do very well, and state openly what it will not address. Ask the user to confirm the trade-offs.
10. Compare with alternatives (optional). For the two or three main alternatives the customer uses, note how well each addresses the same top pains and gains. Mark where the client is clearly better, equal, or worse.
11. List what must be tested. Every "on paper only" row becomes a hypothesis. For each, propose a simple check with real customers (interview question, mock-up, trial offer) and what result would support it. The canvas is a design tool to be combined with testing and revision.
12. Write the fit summary in two or three sentences: for whom, which job, which top pains removed and gains created, by what. Hand over and return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. A Tashkent furniture maker sells seating to cafe and restaurant owners.

Input: segment = owners of small cafes opening a new site; six interviews; offer = custom banquettes and chairs, 30-day production, 1-year warranty.

Output (shortened):

```
CUSTOMER PROFILE - small cafe owner opening a new site
Jobs
 F  furnish the hall before opening day (6/6) FACT
 S  look established to guests and to other owners (3/6) FACT
 E  not worry that the opening will slip (4/6) FACT
Pains (intensity)
 H  supplier misses the date: "Открытие перенесли на две недели" (R2)
 H  fabric stains and wears in the first season (R1, R4, R5)
 M  cannot see the result before paying the deposit (R3)
Gains (relevance)
 H  hall ready a week before opening
 M  guests photograph the interior
VALUE MAP
 Products: banquettes, chairs, layout drawing
 Pain relievers: fixed date in contract with penalty -> missed date
                 (INTERPRETATION - not offered yet)
                 cleanable fabric options + sample swatch -> stains (FACT, R4 praised)
 Gain creators:  layout render before deposit -> see result (on paper only)
FIT
 Top pain 1 (missed date): GAP today
 Top pain 2 (stains): addressed, FACT
 Unmatched in value map: "200 fabric colours" - nobody asked for range
TO TEST: would owners pay a deposit sooner with a dated contract? Ask 5 owners.
```

## Common mistakes
These five are named by Strategyzer; the rest follow from the method steps.
- Blurring the two sides: writing product features into the customer profile, or customer wishes into the value map.
- Mixing several segments (for example payer and user) in one profile.
- Building the profile through the lens of the existing product.
- Listing only functional jobs and missing social and emotional ones.
- Trying to answer every pain and gain instead of choosing a few.
- Vague notes ("quality", "convenience") that cannot be checked.
- Treating a filled canvas as proof of fit without customer testing.
- Gains written as mirror images of pains, which doubles the list and adds nothing.

## Quality checklist
- Does the canvas cover exactly one segment in one named context?
- Was the customer profile completed before the value map?
- Are jobs labelled functional, social or emotional, with at least one non-functional job explored?
- Are pains and gains concrete, ranked, and sourced?
- Does each pain reliever and gain creator point to one specific pain or gain?
- Are gaps and unmatched offer items listed openly?
- Is every note labelled FACT or INTERPRETATION?
- Is there a list of hypotheses to test with customers?

## Output template
```
VALUE PROPOSITION CANVAS - [client] - [date] - status: DRAFT/TESTED
Segment and context: ...
Evidence base: [n interviews / other], dates

CUSTOMER PROFILE (observed, outside client's control)
Jobs:   rank | job | F/S/E | source + quote (original language) | FACT/INTERP
Pains:  rank | pain | intensity H/M/L | frequency | source | FACT/INTERP
Gains:  rank | gain | relevance H/M/L | frequency | source | FACT/INTERP

VALUE MAP (designed by client)
Products and services: rank | item
Pain relievers: mechanism -> pain it addresses
Gain creators:  mechanism -> gain it addresses

FIT TABLE
Customer item | Addressed by | Status: evidence / on paper / gap
Unmatched offer items: ...
Chosen focus (what we will do very well): ...
What we will not address: ...
Alternatives comparison (optional): ...

HYPOTHESES TO TEST
Hypothesis | Check | Result that would support it

FIT SUMMARY (2-3 sentences): ...
```

## Works with
- S5-09 needs-based segments: defines the segment before the canvas.
- S0-11 JTBD framing: gives the job language for the profile.
- S5-01 affinity diagram, S5-02 insight ladder, S5-03 insight tests: supply and check pains and gains.
- S5-11 opportunity solution tree: selects the target opportunity; the canvas then shapes the offer for it, and the tree's assumption tests check the "on paper" rows.
- S6-02 positioning statement and S6-18 creative brief: take the fit summary and focus choices.
- S6-08 strategy choice cascade: uses the trade-offs.
- S5-13 pyramid storyline: reports the result.

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Keep customer quotes in the language they were spoken (Uzbek or Russian). Translate box labels (jobs, pains, gains) once for the team and use the same terms throughout the project.
- With an owner-founder in the room, the value map tends to be filled first and with pride. Keep the order: profile first, with the product covered, then the map.
- Because respondents may answer politely, rate pain intensity from what people did (switched supplier, paid extra, built a workaround) rather than from how strongly they agreed with a prompt.
- For B2B clients, check whether buyer, user and decision-maker are different people; if so, make separate profiles.
