# Within-interview contradiction and say-do analysis (S4-08)

Say-do analysis reads each interview twice: once for what the person claims (attitudes, reasons, general habits, intentions) and once for what the person reports actually doing in specific past episodes. It then lines the two up and treats every mismatch as data. It is not one author's named method. It rests on two research lines and one practice tradition. Nisbett and Wilson (1977, Psychological Review) reviewed evidence that people often have little direct access to the causes of their own judgments and answer "why" questions from plausible theories about causes. The intention-behaviour literature, summarised by Sheeran and Webb (2016), concludes that intentions turn into action only about half of the time. User-research practice (Portigal, Interviewing Users; Nielsen Norman Group) builds interview technique around the same gap.

Evidence grade: A for the underlying findings. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. The existence of the gap is well tested. The analysis procedure below is this skill's own working method built on those findings; it has not itself been validated. Note also that an interview contains reported behaviour, not observed behaviour, so this analysis narrows the gap and does not close it.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (what was said, quoted) versus INTERPRETATION (what the mismatch means). Never call a respondent a liar; the research says people are usually unaware of the gap. Return to the research-orchestrator skill when done.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- Findings rest on stated reasons ("I buy for quality") or stated intentions ("I would switch").
- The client plans to act on what customers said they want.
- Interviews include at least some concrete episodes ("last time I bought...").
- Observation, purchase records or mystery shopping exist and can be compared with the interviews.

Do not use it when:
- Transcripts contain only opinions and no described episodes. Report that the data cannot support the analysis and recommend follow-up questions.
- Only short survey answers are available.
- The aim is to judge the honesty of individual people, for example staff. That is not what this method is for.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. Full transcripts in the original language with respondent IDs; summaries are not enough.
2. The interview guide, to see which questions asked for generalities and which for episodes.
3. Any behavioural evidence: receipts, CRM or sales records, photos of shelves or homes, observation notes.
4. The list of claims the client currently believes about customers.
5. The decision the findings will feed.

## Procedure
1. Tag every relevant statement as SAY or DO. (a) SAY: general claims, values, self-descriptions, reasons given for behaviour, "usually / always" statements, intentions and hypotheticals; (b) DO: a specific past episode with detail (when, where, what, how much, who else), or something shown or observed during the interview. Working rule: if the statement has no time and place, it is SAY.
2. Grade the DO statements. Nisbett and Wilson list conditions under which reports go wrong, including long delay since the event and influences that people do not find plausible as causes. Nielsen notes that memory fills gaps with tidy stories and that "how do you usually..." produces an idealised version. So rate each DO as strong (recent, specific, with checkable detail or shown) or weak (old, vague, reconstructed). Only strong DO statements can overrule a SAY.
3. Build the say-do table per respondent. One row per topic (price, quality, brand, channel, who decides). Columns: SAY quote, DO quote, match or mismatch.
4. Find within-interview contradictions. Three types: (a) SAY versus DO: claims quality comes first; in the last three purchases chose the cheapest; (b) SAY versus SAY: opposite claims in different parts of the interview; (c) DO versus DO: different behaviour in different episodes. Check wording around each one: was the first statement an answer to a leading question?
5. Treat stated reasons with care. When a respondent explains why they did something, record the reason as their theory, not as the cause. Nisbett and Wilson found that observers who had not been through the experience gave much the same explanations as the people who had, which suggests the explanation comes from shared beliefs about causes. Their work also indicates reports are more accurate when the influence is salient and a plausible cause. Decision rule (working rule): a stated reason is promoted to a finding only if the described sequence of events supports it or another data source does.
6. Treat intentions as hypotheses. "I would buy", "I plan to switch" are SAY. Sheeran and Webb report that a medium-to-large change in intention produced only a small-to-medium change in behaviour, and that the stability of an intention is the best indicator of whether it is acted on. Look for evidence of steps already taken (asked the price, visited, saved the contact). No steps taken means the intention is reported as unconfirmed.
7. Interpret each mismatch. Do not pick a winner automatically. Candidate explanations to test against the text: (a) an ideal the person holds versus a constraint they face (money, time, another decision-maker); (b) a general rule versus a specific situation; (c) an answer shaped by the question or by wanting to look good; (d) a real change over time; (e) poor recall. State the chosen explanation as INTERPRETATION and name the quote that supports it.
8. Compare across respondents. Count how many respondents show the same mismatch on the same topic and in which segments. A mismatch shared by several respondents is a candidate insight: it often marks a tension between what people value and what they can do. Count respondents, not quotes, and do not convert to market percentages.
9. Check against outside evidence where it exists (S2-07): sales records, observation, mystery shopping (S1-13), online traces (S1-14).
10. If fieldwork is still open, propose follow-up questions that ask for episodes: the last time, a time it went badly, "show me". Nielsen recommends the critical incident approach, asking for a specific case that was especially hard or went especially well.
11. Report three lists: claims supported by described behaviour; claims contradicted by it; claims with no behavioural evidence either way. Return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. A juice brand interviewed 10 mothers in Tashkent about buying juice for children.
Input (respondent M3, extract):
- "Men faqat tabiiy, shakarsiz sok olaman." (answer to "What matters to you in juice?")
- "O'tgan hafta bozorda bolam yig'lagani uchun rangli qutidagisini oldim, tarkibini o'qimadim." (answer to "Tell me about the last time you bought juice.")
Output:
```
M3 | topic: composition
SAY: "faqat tabiiy, shakarsiz sok olaman"                         FACT
DO (strong, last week): bought what the child asked for, label unread  FACT
Mismatch type: SAY vs DO
Explanation: ideal vs constraint - child's demand at the shelf
    overrides the rule; supported by "yig'lagani uchun"           INTERP
Across sample: same mismatch in 6 of 10 (M1, M3, M4, M6, M8, M9);
    4 with strong DO evidence                                     FACT
Finding status: "mothers choose by composition" = CONTRADICTED
    by described behaviour in this sample                         INTERP
Candidate insight: the label is read at home, the choice is made
    by the child at the shelf                                     INTERP
Intention "keyingi safar o'qiyman" (M3): no steps taken - unconfirmed
```

## Common mistakes
- Reporting stated reasons as causes of behaviour. Reporting "would buy" answers as demand.
- Treating "I usually..." as a description of behaviour. Assuming DO always beats SAY, even when the DO is an old, vague memory.
- Calling respondents dishonest instead of analysing the tension. Resolving a contradiction by deleting one side from the report.
- Counting quotes instead of respondents. Forgetting that described behaviour is still self-report.

## Quality checklist
- Is every key statement tagged SAY or DO with the quote kept?
- Are DO statements graded strong or weak?
- Does each respondent have a say-do table by topic?
- Are stated reasons marked as the respondent's theory unless supported?
- Are intentions reported as unconfirmed unless steps were taken?
- Does each mismatch have an explanation tied to a quote?
- Are claims sorted into supported, contradicted and no evidence?
- Was outside behavioural evidence used where available?

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Polite or agreeing answer habits mean SAY statements given to an interviewer may lean towards what seems proper or pleasing. This is a reason to weight episodes more, not a claim about any group.
- Keep both sides of a mismatch in the original language (Uzbek or Russian). A contradiction can disappear or appear in translation; check doubtful ones with a native speaker.
- When a household decision involves several people, ask who was present and who paid in the described episode; the speaker's SAY and the household's DO may belong to different people.
- Telegram-bot survey answers are almost all SAY and the sample misses older and rural people. Do not run this analysis on them alone.
- The studies behind this method used mostly Western samples. The size of the gap here is unknown; treat the direction as a hypothesis and check it in each project.
