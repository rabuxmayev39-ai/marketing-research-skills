# Qualitative literacy indicators (S2-09)

Qualitative literacy is the ability to tell well-executed interview and fieldwork research from weak work by reading the finished text. The indicators come from the sociologists Mario Luis Small and Jessica McCrory Calarco, Qualitative Literacy: A Guide to Evaluating Ethnographic and Interview Research (University of California Press, 2022). They argue that quality shows in what happened in the field, not in what was planned, and they name one precondition and five indicators. The precondition is exposure: the amount of direct contact the researcher had with the people and setting. The indicators are cognitive empathy (understanding people's perceptions, meanings and motives close to the way they understand them), heterogeneity (showing people and places as varied, with ambiguity and contradiction), palpability (concrete evidence instead of generalisations), follow-up (gathering more data to answer questions that arose during fieldwork) and self-awareness (understanding how the researcher's presence and assumptions affected what people said and did). This skill turns them into a review of the agency's own interviews and reports.

Evidence grade: B. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. The book is a recent standard from two established methodologists and has been reviewed in academic journals; the indicators are expert judgement, not a tested scoring instrument. The authors say it is meant for judging research, not for teaching how to carry it out; it covers data collection and leaves analysis aside; and they say the indicators are not exhaustive and need not all appear to the same degree. It was written for academic reviewers, so the counts and thresholds below are this skill's working rules.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (what the transcript or report contains, with ID and line) versus INTERPRETATION (your rating). When the review is delivered, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- The first two or three interviews are transcribed and the team can still coach the interviewer or change the guide.
- Fieldwork is finished and the team must decide whether the material is good enough to analyse.
- A qualitative report is drafted, or the team must judge a study bought from someone else.

Do not use it when:
- The work is a survey or experiment. Use S2-13 and S3-09.
- The question is whether the analysis and conclusions are sound. The authors set analysis outside the book's scope; use S2-08, S4-07 and S5-03.
- Only a summary exists and no transcripts, notes or quotes can be read. Say the review is not possible.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. Transcripts or detailed notes in the original language, with respondent IDs and the interviewer's questions included.
2. The field log: who, where, how long, how recruited (S1-04, S2-06).
3. Interview snapshots (S3-11) and any notes on the interviewer's own reactions.
4. The draft report, if one exists, and the written expectations from before fieldwork (S2-11).

## Procedure
1. Establish exposure first. From the field log record number of interviews or visits, total hours, and over how many days. The authors note that long contact with a few people can match or exceed a large sample in hours, and that exposure is necessary but not sufficient: "Exposure is the foundation." Working rule: if average interview length is under 30 minutes or key groups were met once and briefly, state "low exposure" at the top of the review; high ratings on the indicators are then unlikely.
2. Pick the material. For a mid-fieldwork check, read every transcript so far. For a finished study, read all transcripts if there are 12 or fewer; otherwise read at least 8, chosen across interviewers and respondent groups (working rule).
3. Cognitive empathy. Ask of each transcript and of the report: does the text show how this person sees the situation, in their own terms, including what they wanted and what they believed at the time? Strong: the reader could explain why a choice made sense to the person, even a choice the client dislikes. Weak: motives are named in the researcher's or client's vocabulary ("price-sensitive", "low awareness") with no account from the person.
4. Heterogeneity. Ask: are people shown as differing from each other, and does any one person appear mixed or contradictory? The authors' point is that a fieldworker with enough exposure is likely to meet ambiguity and contradiction; a reviewer in the opened symposium adds that uniformly confirming evidence should make a reader suspicious. Strong: differences within a segment and exceptions are reported. Weak: "bakers think", "mothers want", with every quote pointing the same way.
5. Palpability. Ask: is the evidence concrete, that is, particular events with "specific people in specific settings" at particular times, or is it people's generalisations about themselves? Working count: take the report's claims (or each topic in a transcript) and mark whether each rests on at least one specific instance. Strong: most claims rest on instances. Weak: claims rest on "usually", "always", "people like us".
6. Follow-up. Ask: when something unexpected or unclear came up, did the researcher pursue it? The authors include both asking the person to elaborate during the interview and collecting further data later. Working count: mark each surprise or vague answer in a transcript and note whether the next question pursued it. Also check at study level: did the guide, the sample or the method change in response to what was found? The authors say follow-up is often what produces palpable data.
7. Self-awareness. Ask: does the researcher show understanding of how their presence, identity, link to the client, and assumptions shaped the data? Look for notes on who the respondent thought the interviewer was, who else was present, and which answers may have been given to please. The authors add that what counts as surprising depends on the researcher's background, so self-awareness also affects what gets followed up.
8. Rate each indicator STRONG, MIXED or WEAK as INTERPRETATION, each with two or three located examples as FACT. Do not add the ratings into a total; the indicators are not ranked or weighted.
9. Turn ratings into actions. Mid-fieldwork: coach the interviewer on the weakest indicator, rewrite guide questions toward specific past events (S3-04), add interviews for missing variety (S2-06). Finished fieldwork: state limits in the report, send unclear points back to respondents, or add a small round. Report draft: replace general statements with instances, restore exceptions, add a short note on the researcher's position.
10. Working addition suggested by a reviewer in the symposium, not one of the five indicators: note where what people said differs from what they were seen to do, and hand it to S4-08. Return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. Input: a furniture maker's study of how families chose their last sofa. 10 interviews done by one interviewer; draft report with 40 claims.

```
EXPOSURE [FACT]: 10 interviews, average 50 min = 500 min = 8 h 20 min; all in
  showroom, each person met once. Moderate; no home visits.
INDICATOR          RATING [INTERP]  EVIDENCE [FACT]
Cognitive empathy  MIXED    R03, R07 explain the choice in their own terms; report
                            section 2 uses "price-sensitive" with no account.
Heterogeneity      WEAK     all 10 recruited in the showroom after buying; no one
                            who left without buying; every quote in 3.1 agrees.
Palpability        WEAK     14 of 40 claims rest on a specific instance = 35%;
                            26 of 40 rest on "usually / always" = 65%.
Follow-up          MIXED    20 surprises or vague answers marked; 8 pursued = 40%;
                            12 not pursued = 60%. Guide unchanged across 10 interviews.
Self-awareness     WEAK     interviewer wore the company badge; no note on its effect.
ACTIONS: add 4 interviews with people who visited and did not buy, at home,
  interviewer without badge; rewrite 5 guide questions as "tell me about the
  day you..."; mark the 26 general claims as unsupported until instances found.
```
Arithmetic check: 10 x 50 = 500 minutes; 500 / 60 = 8 hours with 20 minutes left. 14 / 40 = 0.35 = 35%; 40 - 14 = 26; 26 / 40 = 0.65 = 65%. 8 / 20 = 0.40 = 40%; 20 - 8 = 12; 12 / 20 = 0.60 = 60%.

## Common mistakes
- Reviewing the research plan instead of the transcripts; the indicators are about execution.
- Treating the five indicators as a score to add up, or the working counts as if they came from the book.
- Mistaking a long quote for palpability; a long generalisation is still a generalisation.
- Mistaking a varied sample for heterogeneity; the test is whether variety and contradiction appear in what is reported.
- Reading empathy as sympathy or agreement. It means understanding how the person sees things.
- Writing a self-awareness paragraph that lists the researcher's background and says nothing about its effect on answers.
- Using the review to judge the conclusions; that needs S2-08 and the analysis skills.

## Quality checklist
- Was exposure recorded in interviews, hours and settings before any rating?
- Were transcripts read with the interviewer's questions visible?
- Does each rating cite located examples in the original language?
- Was palpability checked claim by claim, not by overall impression?
- Were missed follow-ups marked at specific places in transcripts?
- Does the review say how the interviewer's identity and client link may have shaped answers?
- Does every WEAK or MIXED rating lead to an action or a stated limit?

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Review transcripts in Uzbek or Russian as spoken; palpable detail and the person's own terms are easily flattened in translation. Quote examples in the original language.
- Polite and agreeing answers can make a set of interviews look uniform. Weak heterogeneity may reflect the interview situation, not the people; check who the interviewer was taken to be.
- For self-awareness, note the interviewer's age, gender, language and visible link to the client, and who else was in the room; in regions, also whether the visit was treated as hosting a guest.
- Samples recruited through Telegram channels or the client's own lists narrow variety before the first question; record this under heterogeneity.
- A Tashkent-only study cannot show regional variety however good the interviews are; state it as a limit.
- The book's examples come from Western academic sociology. The indicators are used here as a working review tool; how they play out in local interviews is something to learn from each project (S8-08).
