# Value-based pricing (S6-13)

Value-based pricing sets price from the value an offer creates for a defined group of customers compared with what they would otherwise do, and uses cost only as a floor. Its main working tool is economic value estimation (EVE), credited to Thomas Nagle and set out in The Strategy and Tactics of Pricing (later editions with Georg Müller). EVE has two parts. Reference value is the price of the customer's next best alternative. Differentiation value is the money value of what the offer does better (positive) minus what it does worse or what it costs to switch (negative). Reference value plus net differentiation value gives total economic value, the most a fully informed buyer should be willing to pay.

Evidence grade: B. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. The method is the standard taught in pricing textbooks and used by pricing consultancies; it is a reasoning framework, not a tested prediction model. Its limits are practical. It needs data on willingness to pay and on customer economics (S1-10, S1-12), which is hard to get. A summary of Hinterhuber (2008) opened for this skill reports that across published surveys value-based pricing was the least used approach (about 17% on average, against 44% competition-based and 37% cost-based) and that the first obstacle managers named was assessing value at all. Total economic value is a ceiling, not a price: one practitioner source notes that the seller never knows exactly how buyers think. Value also differs by segment and changes when the alternative changes.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (alternative's price, measured savings, survey results) versus INTERPRETATION (money value given to a benefit, share of value to charge). Show every assumption as a visible line the client can challenge. When the price range and test plan are delivered, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- A price must be set or changed for a new or improved offer.
- The offer differs from alternatives in ways that can be expressed in money, time or risk. This is easiest in B2B, where the buyer has costs and revenues.
- The team is designing an offer and wants to know which features are worth their cost.

Do not use it when:
- The product is an undifferentiated commodity; then the reference price is the price.
- The price is fixed by regulation, tender rules or a contract.
- Benefits are mainly emotional and no willingness-to-pay research is possible; use S1-12 or S1-10 first, and treat any EVE as rough.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. The customer segment and the use case (S6-01, S5-09).
2. The next best alternative for that segment and its real price, including discounts and delivery.
3. Evidence on differences: tests, customer records, interviews about how the product is used (S1-04, S1-06).
4. The customer's own numbers needed to turn a difference into money (volumes, margins, labour cost).
5. The client's unit cost, as the floor.
6. Any willingness-to-pay research (S1-10, S1-12).

## Procedure
1. Pick one segment and one use case. Value is specific to a segment, so one model per segment. Working rule: start with the two or three segments that matter most to revenue.
2. Name the next best alternative. Ask what this customer would really do without the offer. A practitioner source stresses that this is not always the closest competitor: it can be doing it themselves, staying with the current supplier, waiting, or combining partial fixes.
3. Set the reference value: the price actually paid for that alternative, in the same unit and period as the offer. Get it from invoices, price lists or mystery shopping (S1-13), not from memory.
4. List positive differentiation drivers. One consultancy source groups economic drivers as more revenue, lower cost, less working capital, lower capital spending, lower risk and more flexibility. For each driver write the mechanism, the quantity and the money value per unit. Use the customer's numbers. Keep each as a separate line.
5. List negative differentiation: switching and learning costs, extra work, added risk, and anything the alternative does better. A practitioner source warns that leaving these out damages trust later.
6. Calculate. Net differentiation value = positive minus negative. Total economic value = reference value + net differentiation value. If net value is zero or negative for a segment, the choices are to change the offer, change the segment or price at or below the reference.
7. Attach proof to each driver: test data, a pilot, a customer case, a guarantee. Working rule: count a driver at full value only if there is proof the buyer would accept; otherwise show it separately as unproven.
8. Set the price range between the cost floor and total economic value. The sources say the price belongs below total economic value and that how far below depends on proof, risk and strategy; none gives a fixed share. Working rule: begin with the seller taking 30% to 50% of net differentiation value, and lower the share when proof is weak, the buyer bears switching risk, or fast adoption is the goal.
9. Check against willingness-to-pay evidence. Compare the range with S1-12 or S1-10 results and with buyers' reactions in interviews. If stated willingness to pay is far below the range, the value is not perceived: fix communication or proof before cutting price.
10. Check the competitor's likely response and the client's margin at the chosen price (S0-10).
11. Prepare the value story for sales: one page with the reference, each driver in the buyer's own units, and discount rules tied to the model. The Hinterhuber summary names unnecessary discounting by sales staff as a source of lost value.
12. Plan a test and a review date (S7-02). Return to research-orchestrator.

## Worked example
INVENTED EXAMPLE - not real data. Input: an invented flour mill in Tashkent region sells a stronger bread flour to small bakeries in 50 kg bags. Next best alternative: a rival's flour at 5,200 soum per kg. Baker figures come from six bakery visits.

```
PER 50 KG BAG, soum                                              label
Reference value: 5,200 x 50                         = 260,000   FACT
Positive differentiation
  8 more loaves per bag (180 against 172),
    3,000 soum contribution per loaf: 8 x 3,000     = +24,000   INTERPRETATION
  Fewer spoiled batches (bakers' estimate)          =  +6,000   INTERPRETATION
Negative differentiation
  Recipe trials 400,000 soum over first 50 bags     =  -8,000   INTERPRETATION
Net differentiation value: 24,000 + 6,000 - 8,000   =  22,000
Total economic value: 260,000 + 22,000              = 282,000
Working-rule range (seller takes 30% to 50% of 22,000):
  260,000 + 6,600 = 266,600   to   260,000 + 11,000 = 271,000
Chosen test price: 270,000 per bag = 5,400 per kg
  Premium 10,000 = 3.8% above reference; baker keeps 12,000 (54.5% of net)
Cost floor 235,000: margin 35,000 against 25,000 at the rival's price
PROOF NEEDED: side-by-side bake at 10 bakeries; yield measured, not claimed.
```
Arithmetic check: 5,200 x 50 = 260,000. 180 - 172 = 8; 8 x 3,000 = 24,000. 400,000 / 50 = 8,000. 24,000 + 6,000 - 8,000 = 22,000. 260,000 + 22,000 = 282,000. 0.30 x 22,000 = 6,600; 0.50 x 22,000 = 11,000. 270,000 / 50 = 5,400. 270,000 - 260,000 = 10,000; 10,000 / 260,000 = 0.038. 22,000 - 10,000 = 12,000; 12,000 / 22,000 = 0.545. 270,000 - 235,000 = 35,000; 260,000 - 235,000 = 25,000.

## Common mistakes
- Starting from cost plus a margin and calling the result value-based.
- Comparing with the nearest competitor when the customer's real alternative is something else.
- Counting only benefits and leaving out switching costs.
- Pricing at total economic value, leaving the buyer no reason to switch.
- Using one model for all segments.
- Valuing features the buyer does not use or does not believe.
- Hiding assumptions inside one total so the client cannot challenge them.

## Quality checklist
- Is the model for one named segment and use case?
- Is the next best alternative what the customer would really do, with its real price?
- Is every driver a separate line with mechanism, quantity and money value?
- Are negative differences included?
- Does each driver have proof, or an "unproven" mark?
- Is the price below total economic value and above cost, with the share stated?
- Was the range compared with willingness-to-pay evidence?
- Is there a test and a review date?

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Build the model with the customer's own figures, gathered in Uzbek or Russian, and keep their units (per bag, per shift, per order).
- Polite or agreeing answers overstate willingness to pay. Prefer observed purchases, test prices and side-by-side trials.
- List prices may differ from prices actually paid because of bargaining, cash terms or credit. Treat the reference value as unverified until an invoice or a purchase confirms it.
- Small business customers may not keep the records the model needs. Plan visits to measure, and mark estimates as estimates.
- Alternatives and prices may differ between Tashkent and the regions; one model per region is a sensible working rule.
- Payment terms (instalments, deferred payment) may matter as much as price; show them as a separate driver. This is a hypothesis to check with buyers.
