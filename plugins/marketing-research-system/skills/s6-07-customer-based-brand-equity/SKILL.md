# Customer-based brand equity (S6-07)

Customer-based brand equity is Kevin Lane Keller's idea, published in the Journal of Marketing in 1993, that a brand's value sits in what buyers hold in memory about it. He defines it as the difference that brand knowledge makes to how people respond to the brand's marketing: equity is positive when people react more favourably to a product, price or advertisement because it carries the brand name than they would to the same thing under an invented or unnamed brand. Brand knowledge has two parts: awareness (can the buyer recognise and recall the brand) and image (the set of associations, meaning the things linked to the brand in memory). Keller's condition for equity is that the buyer is familiar with the brand and holds associations that are strong, favourable and unique. He later arranged the idea as the brand resonance pyramid, a ladder of six building blocks from being known to being actively loved.

Evidence grade: A. Grades are A = tested in peer-reviewed research, B = established professional standard, C = practitioner tool with little formal testing. The 1993 paper is a peer-reviewed conceptual model and one of the most used in brand research, and its parts (awareness, associations) are measured in many published studies. Three cautions keep the weight honest. It is hard to measure fully: a general reference opened for this skill says there is no agreed way to quantify brand equity and that all calculations are approximations. The pyramid's step-by-step order is only partly supported: one open-access test (Gordon and James, 2017, 787 sports fans) found that associations and judgements did not lead to resonance directly, only through feelings, and that some blocks could not be told apart statistically. And the requirement of unique associations is disputed: the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute reports that most users of a brand do not see it as different from rivals, yet buy it anyway. Use the model to organise what to measure and plan; do not treat the pyramid as a proven causal chain.

Working rules for the assistant: answer in the user's language (Uzbek, Russian or English). Label FACT (measured awareness, counted associations) versus INTERPRETATION (what the brand "stands for", which block is weak). Keep respondents' words for associations in the original language. When the audit and plan are delivered, return to the research-orchestrator skill.

## When to use it / When not to use it
Use it when:
- The team must decide what the brand should be linked to in buyers' minds, or set brand goals for a year.
- Brand tracking or survey data exist and need a structure for reading them.
- A client asks "how strong is our brand" and needs an answer in buyer terms, not a money figure.

Do not use it when:
- The client needs a financial valuation of the brand for a sale or for accounts. That is a different task with different methods.
- The brand is new and almost unknown. Then awareness is the only block worth measuring; go to S6-03 and S6-04.
- The data would come from five or six interviews only. Associations can be explored that way (S1-04) but not counted.

## What you need before starting (inputs)
1. The brand, the category and three to five named competitors.
2. Who counts as a category buyer (S6-01) and a sample of them, not only the brand's own customers.
3. Existing data: tracking, surveys, reviews, sales and repeat-purchase records.
4. The situations in which the category is bought or used (S1-21), to check breadth of awareness.
5. What the client wants the brand to stand for, written down before the data are read (S2-11).

## Procedure
1. Fix the frame. Write the brand, category, competitors and buyer definition. Awareness and image only mean something relative to a category and its rivals.
2. Measure awareness in two forms. Recognition: does the buyer confirm knowing the brand when shown the name or pack. Recall: does the buyer name it unprompted when given the category or a buying situation. Keller describes salience as having depth (how easily the brand comes to mind) and breadth (in how many buying and usage situations). Ask recall by situation, not only by category name (S1-21, S4-19).
3. Collect associations before rating them. Ask aware buyers what comes to mind for the brand, in their own words (free association), and use projective questions where direct ones give thin answers (S3-10). Code the answers into a list (S4-21).
4. Rate each association on Keller's three properties. Strength: what share of aware buyers mention or endorse it. Favourability: is it a reason to choose or to avoid. Uniqueness: do competitors get the same association as often. Working rule: because uniqueness is the disputed property, record it but do not drop a strong, favourable association only because a rival shares it.
5. Place the evidence on the resonance pyramid. Keller's four levels, bottom to top, answer four buyer questions: identity (salience), meaning (performance, the functional side; imagery, the intangible side such as history and personality), response (judgements, the "head"; feelings, the "heart") and relationships (resonance). Sources describe resonance as intense, active loyalty, with four parts in one account: repeat buying, attachment, sense of community and active engagement. For each block write the evidence and the gap. A block with no data is an unknown (S0-13), not a weakness.
6. Test the differential effect where possible. The definition is a comparison, so run one: show the same product, price or advertisement with and without the brand name to matched groups and compare choice or rating (S1-09, S1-10). Without such a test, say that equity was described, not demonstrated.
7. Decide the desired links. Working rule: choose three to five associations the brand should own, each tied to a buying situation, each backed by something true about the product. Compare with the client's written expectations from the inputs.
8. Plan from the bottom up, as Keller advises: awareness first, then meaning, then response, then relationship. Working rule: do not set a loyalty or community goal while unprompted recall among category buyers is low.
9. Set measures of two kinds, following Keller's split: sources (what is in buyers' minds: the pyramid blocks) and outcomes (sales, price premium, repeat buying). He recommends several measures read together (S2-07). Report, and return to research-orchestrator.

## Common mistakes
- Measuring only the brand's own customers, which hides weak awareness.
- Reporting recognition and calling it awareness, without recall.
- Treating the client's wished-for image as the measured one.
- Reading the pyramid as a proven staircase and blaming every loyalty problem on the block below.
- Chasing a unique association that buyers do not care about.
- Claiming "strong brand equity" with no branded-versus-unbranded comparison and no outcome data.

## Quality checklist
- Are category, competitors and buyer definition written down?
- Are both recognition and recall reported, with recall asked by situation?
- Were associations collected in buyers' own words before any rating?
- Is each key association rated for strength, favourability and uniqueness against named rivals?
- Does every pyramid block show evidence, a gap, or "unknown"?
- Is the differential effect tested, or its absence stated?
- Are source measures and outcome measures both in the plan?

## Notes for Uzbekistan
- Run recall and association questions in the language the respondent chooses, Uzbek or Russian, and keep the words as given. An association can carry a different tone in each language; code them separately before merging (S3-03).
- Polite or agreeing answer habits can lift recognition and favourable ratings. Rely more on unprompted recall and on behaviour such as repeat buying (S3-07, S1-23).
- A Telegram-bot sample misses older and rural buyers; awareness measured there describes that sample only. Compare Tashkent with the regions before speaking of "the market".
- The pyramid was developed and mostly tested on Western consumer brands. Whether community and engagement mean the same thing here is a hypothesis to test, not an assumption.
